**Purpose:** the logical and narrative order of the whole manuscript, for a
reader who meets the framework for the first time. It fixes the order in which
ideas arrive and the question each chapter answers. Foundations r3 fixes the
definitions and the chain; this outline places them.

**Reader.** A strong undergraduate from a strong school, or an expert in one of
the fields the argument crosses: physics, information theory, moral
philosophy, biology or AI. No prior knowledge of the program is assumed. Every
term is defined at first use. Field background goes in boxes the specialist
can skip.

**The object.** The manuscript studies one physical object, Omega: the
completed structure of everything that can follow from every present. The
foundation argues why that object bears on value: value requires valuers,
consequence connects valuers to what follows, so the futures accessible from a
present are the substrate of prospective value. The ethics and its relevance
to alignment are developed through the study of that object.

**Cosmic nihilism** is answered head-on: grant the nihilist's premises, and the
structure is still not flat. Main treatment in the afterword; brief mentions in
chapters 1, 3, 7 and 14, and Overview section 8.

**Style.**
- One **TL;DR** per chapter: a headline of one or two sentences, then bold
  takeaways; no toy results.
- **Problem, then resolution.**
- **Plain-English definitions**, numbered by chapter until compilation;
  **Primer**, **Note** and **named** boxes; **bold headlines**; a **status
  table** and **references** at the end of each chapter.
- Analogies avoided because most are bad, not banned; grounded, accessible
  prose for a reader new to the framework (style sheet r25).
- **Philosophical chapters** (3, 4, 10) are tight argument in the manner of the
  great accessible philosophers, with no numbers or models in their first
  drafts.
- The thesis is stated boldly, and the manuscript earns it.

**Length.** About 160,000–200,000 words of main text, plus appendices. Not
binding.

## The spine

1. **The problem.** Every standard of good begins by deciding who counts, and
   that decision is never settled. Artificial agents make it urgent.
   *(Chapter 1)*
2. **The object.** Whatever can matter happens through some physical
   development. What can follow from a present is built from physics, before
   anyone is chosen to count: Omega. *(Chapter 2)*
3. **Value requires valuers.** Consequence connects valuers to what follows, so
   the futures accessible from a present are the substrate of prospective
   value. Whatever takes part in consequence is a valuer: admission is
   universal. *(Chapter 3)*
4. **The whole field.** Valuers overlap, and no boundary around one system,
   merely by being drawn, sets an exclusive moral boundary. So the
   comparison is of the whole field from a present, counted once. The same
   measure applied to a part can rank options differently.
   *(Chapter 4)*
5. **The measure.** Physics fixes what counts as different, and the
   requirement that a whole count agree with its parts weighted by how likely
   they are fixes how to count. The count is perplexity: lushness.
   *(Chapters 5 and 8)*
6. **Time.** Compare whole futures, not snapshots. Rate, head start,
   persistence and irreversibility come from one profile. *(Chapters 6–7)*
7. **Parts and wholes, exactly.** A part and the whole differ by one term, and
   the joint law counts shared futures once. *(Chapter 9)*
8. **The thesis.** Expansion of whole-field lushness is expansion of accessible
   value, and contraction its loss. It is demonstrated by correspondence, one
   class of cases at a time. *(Chapters 10–11)*
9. **Earning it:** organization, information, persistence, stasis and chaos, harm,
   protection, suffering, cooperation, domination, justice, persons.
   *(Parts IV–VI)*
10. **Deciding without the whole view.** The planning rules that the
    measure gives anyone who decides by it, and why they take that shape; the
    gradient, defined by comparing available continuations by the measure,
    which by the central thesis is an agent-neutral standard of prospective
    better and worse; what a participant who sees only part of the field
    still needs; controllers; alignment. *(Part VII; Gradient Ethics is Book
    Two)*
11. **Where it comes from.** From awareness to Alpha, and from Alpha to a
    universe. *(Part VIII)*
12. **What could overturn it.** The evidence by status, the hardest tests, and
    the program ahead. *(Part IX)*

## Part I — The problem and the foundation

The reader learns why standards of good are stuck, meets the physical object,
and is given the argument for why that object bears on value, before any
mathematics.

### 1. Who counts?
- **Question.** Ethics guides action. Whose lives, relations and possibilities
  enter that guidance, and why has no answer ever been settled?
- **Answer.** Every standard settles who and what counts before the inquiry
  begins, so every dispute about that starting point is a dispute about the
  whole standard.
- **Builds.** A survey of the standards (welfare, preference, rights, virtue,
  contract, capabilities, biocentric ethics) and the starting points each
  depends on; the is–ought problem stated fairly.
- **Then.** Why artificial agents make the problem urgent: they need a standard
  that does not depend on whose preferences trained them, favours no group,
  and stays defined as their understanding grows.
- **Hands on.** Three requirements for a different starting point: no group
  chosen in advance (who counts follows from an argument, not a list); stay
  well defined; be testable. Chapters 3 and 4 meet the first. *Drafted (r3).*
- *Primers:* moral philosophy; AI objectives.
- *Nihilism, briefly:* one response to the stalemate is to deny there is
  anything to settle. The afterword answers it.

### 2. What follows from a present
*Formerly chapter 3; revised as r7 and r8: frames as admissible slices, the
list of systems moved to chapter 3, the closing pointed at chapter 3, kept
economical, Omega as one physical structure rather than one probability
table.*
- **Question.** If what can matter happens through physical development, what,
  physically, is what can follow?
- **Answer.** It is built from the present outward. A present is a sufficient
  physical specification of the world on a section; orientation gives it a
  before and an after; a frame specifies the admissible section it sits on;
  the law fixes what can follow. Gathered from every present, that is Omega. No stock
  quantity of the present settles what follows; the differences lie in what
  can follow, and in what each part of the world can reach.
- **Order of the chapter:** the present; orientation; frames; the law,
  histories and futures; Omega; why present stocks do not determine what
  follows; access.
- **Builds.**
  - Whatever can matter happens physically (from former chapter 2), stated as
    the reason to study what can follow; the argument for why it bears on
    value is chapter 3's.
  - Present-rooted, not presentist.
  - Plain-English definitions: orientation, present, frame, history, Omega,
    access.
  - **Frames are not things.** A frame specifies an admissible present
    section; the present is the sufficient specification on it. Any
    admissible section may serve; a local section fixes only what follows in
    the region it alone determines; a global section serves the whole field. The universe has frames; it is
    not one. Which sections are admissible, and how local presents relate to a
    global one, is open.
  - Omega's probabilities belong to classical histories and to the records
    physical frames make.
  - **Access** acts through the law, never as a multiplier on the measure.
  - What Omega is not: a probability over beliefs, or a utility.
- *Primer:* probability and histories.
- *Formal:* A.1.

### 3. Value requires valuers
*New. The most important chapter. Philosophical argument; no numbers.
Drafted (r4). Replaces former chapter 9 r1.*
- **Question.** Physics describes what can follow. Where, in a physical world,
  does value enter, and what can bear it?
- **Answer.** Value requires valuers. Consequence is what connects them: it
  pertains to whatever takes part in it. From a present, what is realized is
  common to every option, so what remains open to value is what can follow:
  the futures accessible from a present are the substrate of prospective
  value. Anything that takes part in consequential continuation is a valuer,
  so admission is universal.
- **Order of the chapter** (foundations r3, steps 2–9, 14–15):
  1. **The foundational principle.** Value is relative to valuers. It fixes
     where value can be, not what is good. Relational is not subjective: the
     relata are physical. Compatible with intrinsic value (Moore's unseen
     world): even intrinsic value has a bearer that exists.
  2. **Consequence is the bridge.** What can matter to a valuer is what follows
     for it. Consequence is not branching.
  3. **Value faces accessible continuation.** Value already realized is common
     to all options and cancels from any comparison; a past can be uncertain
     without being open to change. The asymmetry is one of access.
  4. **Sign is relative.** A continuation can be worse than another
     continuation of the same present; an absolute negative is neither assumed
     nor needed. The measure's floor is a property of the measure.
  5. **Determinism is the limit case.** One continuation, nothing to compare;
     its systems remain valuers.
  6. **The futures field is the substrate.**
  7. **The metavalue.** Value still to come needs valuing still to come:
     continuing, recovering or arising. Persistence is worth what it keeps
     open; an ending is not bad in itself.
  8. **A reason, for agents.** Instrumental consistency connects valuing to its
     conditions; chapter 4 gives the scope, chapter 10 the magnitude. It
     licenses keeping the channel open, not maximum protection.
  9. **Universal admission** (the culmination). Anything that takes part in
     consequential continuation is a valuer: particles, gas, organisms,
     institutions, scattered and overlapping systems, the universe. Excluding
     any would need a criterion beyond consequence. Existence without
     consequence does not qualify. Identity, agency and consciousness are
     different questions. Deformers are a kind of valuer. Valuers are not
     equal and carry no weights; tiers are mechanisms, not ranks. The thermal
     baseline is taken on, not excluded.
- **Builds.** The inventory of what the framework rests on (no axioms of its
  own); why a hedonic definition brings in negative levels, without claiming to
  refute negative utilitarianism; why the principle is not an axiom.
- *Formal:* A.4.8, A.4.9, A.7.1 (revised), A.9.2, A.9.4.
- *Nihilism, briefly:* value is a relation within physics, not something
  projected onto it.

### 4. The whole field
*New. Philosophical argument. Absorbs the comparison with no index (former
chapter 10) and the earlier attempts (former chapter 2). Drafted (r3).*
- **Question.** With every physical participant admitted and none weighted,
  whose futures does a comparison compare?
- **Answer.** The joint field's, from a present. No subsystem boundary, merely
  by identifying a physical system, sets an exclusive moral boundary: even a
  well-identified organism is intertwined with its constituents, surroundings
  and successors. So the comparison names no part. The same measure applied to
  a part can favour self-directed power seeking; applied to the whole, it is
  the ethical comparison.
- **Order of the chapter:**
  1. Why separate scores fail: overlapping valuers' scores duplicate
     consequences, and their total depends on how the world is divided.
  2. Why a boundary does not settle it: three requirements (universal
     admission, no duplication, independence from how the field is divided);
     any method that meets them reconstructs one assessment of the whole field.
     Identifying a system does not isolate it (supporting research: Fields and
     Glazebrook 2023; Sandved-Smith et al. 2026). The stronger claim, that no
     sectioning of the field is justified, is kept for chapter 32.
  3. The comparison with no index; agent-neutral and agent-relative reasons.
  4. Local comparisons answer a different question: they *can* rank options
     differently, and when they do is for the models; misuse, and the defence
     the whole-field scope gives.
  5. **Rules for an admissible comparison** (foundations r3, section 7): one
     present; continuation laws, not single histories; one resolution;
     implemented, not conditioned; disclosed horizon; invariance. Mechanism
     contrasts are legitimate science, labelled.
  6. **Infinite totals, flagged.** One sentence: whole-field totals may be
     infinite, and meaningful comparisons between localized options use the
     construction of chapter 9. "Whole field" is not an automatically finite
     number.
  7. **What a measure must do** (short, to keep the argument's momentum).
     Earlier attempts built on freedom, options or possibility (Pattanaik and
     Xu; capabilities; option value; empowerment, causal entropic forces,
     maximum occupancy; open-endedness) stalled at four points. They become
     explicit questions for Part II: what counts as a different future
     (grain)? how much does an unlikely future count (weighting)? whose
     futures (subject: answered here in principle, exactly in chapter 9)? does
     noise count (busyness)?
  8. **The destination.** The central thesis stated briefly: expansion of
     whole-field lushness from an admissible present is expansion of
     accessible value. Chapter 10 defends it once Part II has built the
     measure.
- **Scope without the measurement.** The chapter argues in words; the entropy
  identities stay in chapter 9.
- *Open:* frame invariance; a global present.
- *Formal:* A.7.6, A.7.8–A.7.9; the new definition of admissible comparison.

### 5. Counting futures
*Formerly chapter 4; renumbered (r8), with openings and pointers revised.*
- **Question.** How much substrate does a present hold? The grain and
  weighting problems, from chapter 4.
- **Answer.** Physics decides what counts as the same, and probability decides
  how much there is. Futures matter only if reachable, so each counts with the
  probability of reaching it; with the composition rule and its companions,
  one measure remains: lushness.
- **Builds.** Resolution; the continuation structure; lushness; the composition
  rule as the commitment that selects the measure; the branch-volume form;
  horizons as later sections; why similarity weighting is excluded; counting
  everything, randomness included (busyness, from chapter 4); the
  exponential of path entropy; the compression boundary.
- *Primers:* entropy and perplexity; lushness is not the entropy of heat.
- *Formal:* A.1.1, A.2.

### 6. Futures through time
*Formerly chapter 5; renumbered (r5).*
- **Question.** Should a head start you can see now count for more than a lead
  that grows later?
- **Answer.** Compare the whole profile of the difference: slope the rate,
  level the offset, zero the crossover horizon. Over everything that follows,
  rate decides first and offset second.
- **Builds.** The profile; rate and offset; the crossover horizon; preparation,
  process and mechanism contrasts (only process contrasts are choices from one
  present); the offset theorem.
- *Primer:* Markov processes.
- *Formal:* A.3.

### 7. How long things last
*Formerly chapter 6; renumbered (r4). The formal side of chapter 3's metavalue.*
- **Question.** Should what exists be preserved? Is a fast gain worth taking if
  it may not last?
- **Answer.** Persistence is worth the rate it sustains beyond whatever would
  replace it. A gain must repay its cost over its expected life; an early end
  costs least when the world can rebuild what was lost; a finite generative
  window caps the payoff of any rate gain.
- **Builds.** Random horizons; what persistence is worth; the cost of an early
  end and regeneration; the generative window; gambles and finite stakes.
- *Primer:* expected value.
- *Formal:* A.4, A.5.
- *Nihilism, briefly:* transience does not zero worth.

### 8. Resolution: what counts as different
*Formerly chapter 7; renumbered (r5).*
- **Question.** Rankings can flip with how finely or how often we look. Can any
  verdict be trusted?
- **Answer.** Declare the resolution; only physically registrable distinctions
  count; compare options at one resolution. A finer level governs once its
  extra detail is shown faithful; otherwise a reversal is
  resolution-dependent. Timing counts only as finely as the system's own
  physics can record it; a clock is physical.
- **Builds.** As in r22 chapter 7.
- *Formal:* A.6.

### 9. Parts and wholes
*Formerly chapter 8; renumbered (r4), with the infinite exterior added. The exact form of chapter 4's argument.*
- **Question.** How do the futures of a part and of the whole relate, exactly?
- **Answer.** A part never has more lushness than the whole. Self and whole
  differ by one term: what an act does to the rest, given what it does to the
  part. That term moves with correlation as well as with the rest's own
  branching. Unaffected parts cancel; the joint law counts shared futures once.
- **Builds.** As in r22 chapter 8; plus an infinite world: a finite comparison
  with an infinite unchanged exterior (from former chapter 10).
- *Primer:* mutual information.
- *Formal:* A.7.1–A.7.7, A.7.10–A.7.11.

## Part III — The thesis and how it is tested

### 10. The central thesis
*Rebuilt from former chapter 11. Philosophical argument. Drafted (r6).*
- **Question.** What exactly is claimed, and how could it be shown?
- **Answer.** Expansion of whole-field lushness from an admissible present is
  expansion of accessible value, and contraction its loss. It is an
  identification, demonstrated by correspondence.
- **Order of the chapter:**
  1. **Object, measure and thesis are three things.**
  2. **The thesis, stated;** narrowing, widening and foreclosure, measured;
     the largest narrowing.
  3. **Why believe it.** Where value lies (chapter 3) and how far the
     comparison reaches (chapter 4); value depends on physical context, so no
     development has a fixed value on its own and the substrate is the joint
     continuation; the measure is contextual in the same sense. The
     identification of value with effective breadth is the substantive step;
     given it, and the combination requirement of chapter 5, lushness is the
     measure. A Note on the resemblance to quantum contextuality.
  4. **What it is not:** a definition of good, a rule to maximize disorder or
     activity, a reason to preserve everything, a complete description of
     structure, or a ranking of moments or worlds.
  5. **How it is tested.** Correspondence; benchmarks described physically;
     easy cases, where a mismatch counts against it; hard cases, such as the
     trails of turbulence, where definite predictions made in advance test it
     hardest; conflicts kept open until their cause is found (reflective
     equilibrium); three kinds of pressure.
  6. **What it implies,** stated once: equal prospective value for different
     continuations; transfers between valuers; numerical identity; tradeoffs;
     a ranking does not by itself give authority to act (Gradient Ethics);
     special protection, not special standing; determinism, realized value,
     no absolute negative, noise, scope, endings.
  7. **What failure would look like:** five named results.
- *Formal:* A.9.2–A.9.4; A.4.9; A.7.2, A.7.6; A.15.

### 11. How the thesis is tested
*Formerly chapter 12; drafted (r12): the four questions, the standard
report, the six readings of a run.*
- **Question.** What can small exact models show, and what can they not show?
- **Answer.** Two layers (oracle and bounded agent). Four questions, in the

### 15. Between stasis and chaos
*Formerly 16 (Activity is not breadth), reframed; drafted (r1).*
- **Question.** Universal admission counts noise and busyness. Must the measure
  then prefer chaos to structure?
- **Answer.** Activity counts, and the measure counts it. What opens the most
  over time is activity that builds. Stasis opens nothing. With a fixed
  repertoire, pure noise reaches the ceiling on branching quickly (A.2.4) and
  erodes the structures that could enlarge the repertoire. Lushness keeps
  growing faster than linearly only where the repertoire keeps growing
  (A.3.12): structure that persists long enough to build, with variation
  enough to explore. Possibility lies between stasis and chaos.
- **The objection at full strength.** Over short horizons, or with a fixed
  repertoire, noise can win. The chapter shows where and when that stops,
  rather than denying it.
- **Builds.** The repertoire ceiling and repertoire growth; the
  activity-matched comparator; the compression pairs; criticality as a
  statement about being, shown by the measure rather than assumed.
- **Test.** A variation parameter swept from frozen to fully random, in one
  ecology under one law and endowment: long-horizon lushness against it. The
  prediction is an interior peak that moves with the horizon. A peak at the
  chaotic end at every horizon counts against the thesis.
- *Formal:* A.2.4, A.3.11–A.3.12.
- *Status:* M, open.

## Part VII — Deciding without the whole view

What a ranking by whole-field lushness says to anyone deciding, and what it
cannot say to a participant who sees only part of the field. Chapter 23 gives
the planning rules and shows that each arises from a property of the measure:
it composes, counts every physical consequence, keeps information apart from
its use, weighs duration, and has its rate bounded by a finite repertoire.
Chapter 24 defines the gradient by comparing available continuations with
that measure, and interprets it by the central thesis. The
account of how a participant should climb it, Gradient Ethics, is named in
the text of chapter 24 and developed in Book Two. No chapter or section title
names it [143].

### 23. Choosing inside the structure
*Formerly 24; drafted (r4).* The decision rules, and why they are shaped as
they are: the decision recursion; implementation, not conditioning; the
value of information and option value; irreversibility and the strength of
commitment (Example 23.1); finite horizons with bounds on the unseen; the
robustness floor, the long-shot bound and the window applied; when ranking
by rate cannot choose; a fixed evaluation cannot cycle; what the measure
does not supply. Each rule arises from a property of the measure that
defines the gradient of chapter 24 (r4).
*Formal:* A.13.1, A.13.4–A.13.5, A.12, A.9.5–A.9.6, A.4.6, A.5.2–A.5.4.

### 24. The gradient
*Formerly 25 (One uncertainty, many places); rebuilt* [143], [144].
*Drafted (r2).*
- **The architecture.** Continuation structure, measure, planning results,
  gradient, ethical interpretation: five steps, kept apart.
- **The gradient.** The comparison of the continuations available from a
  present by expected whole-field lushness over the expected persistence
  (or by the profile where regimes do not end). Ties and unresolved
  comparisons are allowed, and a best continuation need not exist. Under
  determinism there is no gradient, since there is nothing to compare. The
  planning rules arise from the measure, not from the ordering alone.
- **An agent-neutral standard.** If the central thesis is true, the gradient
  is an agent-neutral standard of prospective better and worse: universal in
  who counts, in standpoint and in where it applies. The options available
  to particular agents differ, and evaluating a development is not
  authorizing anyone to bring it about. A correspondence, with the thesis's
  status, not a derivation.
- **Deciding without the whole view.** The optimum is what a decision-maker
  with the whole view would choose. A participant has a partial model, acts
  through its own physical process, and its errors are consequences. What
  deciding within the structure entails, climbing a gradient one can only
  partly see, is what the manuscript calls Gradient Ethics (named here, in
  the text).
- **What Book Two inherits and must answer.** Protection margins as a
  proposed mechanism, calibration and authority still to be developed
  (chapter 17); the questions (the decision rule,
  calibrating margins, identity and standing, the incumbent problem, how
  gains and losses are shared between lives, deference and correction); the
  constraints any answer must meet (an agent's uncertainty is not branching
  in the field; refraining has consequences too); what would count as
  failure. Diagnoses and partly constrains the maximizer worry inside this
  book; solving it is Book Two's first task.
- *Formal:* A.9.5 (cited); A.12.1; A.13.3 (cited).

### 25. Controllers that reshape the field
*Formerly 26; drafted (r4).* Agency as physically implemented control over

the controller's own dynamics, depends on its records and steers. Agency,
influence, leverage and standing are distinct; no quantitative marker of
agency is proposed. Leverage classes as consequence signatures; two ways to

*Formal:* A.3.11–A.3.12, A.8.3, A.13.2.

### 26. Alignment
*Formerly 27; narrowed; drafted (r2).* What the framework offers artificial
agents, and where an agent pursuing it could still go wrong: an objective

### 27. From awareness to Alpha
*Formerly 28 (Alpha, Logos and Omega); rebuilt on the awareness-first route
of Perspective and Manifest Divinity §§2–9 and Volume I §4, peeled off the
theology* [137]–[139].

  For readers who deny phenomenal awareness, the chain runs from seeming
  instead, and the step from seeming to awareness is named as a premise they
  may reject (a Note).

  bounded aware subject to be possible? The answer is bracketed between a
  minimal awareness-sufficient reality and a maximal formal one; a
  ruliad-like object models the upper limit and is not a premise.
- **The backward chain.** Distinction, relation, orientation, sectioning,
  perspective, subject invariant, then a dashed arrow to awareness: the hard
  problem is inherited, not solved. Sectioning is chapter 2's frames. Alpha
  is the minimal generative structure this reaches (primitive asymmetry, from
  which orientation is derived).
- **Time reversibility and the endpoint identity** (forward and backward
  conditional entropies over a window differ by the change in endpoint
  entropy; equal at any steady state, driven or not), which chapter 7 relies
  on for why our world rebuilds so little of what it loses.
- **Not here:** the global completion of perspective as manifest divine mind,
  the Logos, orientation darwinism; cosmopsychism, which can go in a small
  companion piece [141]. Epistemic order and explanatory order are
  kept apart, so starting from awareness and later asking how it is realized
