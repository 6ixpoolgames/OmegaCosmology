[Chapter 1](#chapter-1) ended with a promise: to begin from a physical object
rather than a list of who counts. This chapter builds that object. It starts
from a single present and works outward, defining each idea as it is needed,
until it reaches the whole: the universal possibility structure, which the
manuscript calls Omega. It then says what Omega is not, why no simple quantity
of the present can stand in for it, and how access enters. The chapter
develops [Overview section 1](#overview-1).

The chapter combines definitions with established results of physics, each
under its stated assumptions. It makes no ethical claim. Why this object bears
on value is the subject of [chapter 3](#chapter-3).

## 2.1 What can matter happens physically

**Whatever can matter happens through some physical development. So the place
to start is what physics says can happen.**

Take anything that might matter: a harvest, an illness, a friendship, a
species, a decision. Each happens through physical processes. Crops grow by
chemistry. Illness runs through cells. A friendship is carried by meetings,
words and memories, all of them physical events. A decision is made by a brain
or a machine, and it changes the world through what that brain or machine
does next. Even a thought makes a difference only through the physical
process that has it.

This is not a claim that only physics matters, or that a friendship is
nothing but its chemistry. It is a claim about where to look. If something can
matter, it does so through what happens, and what can happen is what physics
allows. So a study of what can matter can begin with a study of what can
happen, before it asks what is good.

That order has an advantage. What can happen is defined by physics, and
physics does not first decide who counts. A description of what can follow
from the world now includes insects, rivers and future people on the same
terms as everything else, because they are all part of what can happen.
Whether and how they count is a later question, and
[chapter 3](#chapter-3) takes it up.

## 2.2 Everything starts from a present

**What can follow is always what can follow from some state of the world now.**

Every assessment in this manuscript starts from a present: the way the world
is now. Three commitments make that precise.

- **Every assessment is made from a present.** What can follow is always what
  can follow from some state of things now.
- **The past acts only through traces.** Whatever bearing the past has on what
  follows, it has through the physical traces it left in the present: records,
  memories, structures, scars.
- **Futures are what a present can give rise to.** What follows is defined by
  the present and the law that carries it forward. It is not a separate given.

These are commitments of method, not a claim about what exists. The manuscript
does not assume that only the present is real. Relativity singles out no
universal present, and physical theories differ about what ultimately exists
([chapter 28](#chapter-28)). The method needs only that, wherever an
assessment is made, it is made from a present.

A state of the world cannot be a present on its own. To have something before
it and something that can follow, it needs three things:

1. an **orientation**, which orders before and after (section 2.3);
2. a **frame**, which picks out the slice of the world the present sits on
   (section 2.4);
3. a **law**, which fixes what the present can give rise to (section 2.6).

## 2.3 Orientation gives before and after

**Nothing follows from anything unless occurrences are ordered into before
and after.**

"What follows from a present" assumes that some occurrences come after others.
Without that order, there are states of the world, but nothing is a
continuation of anything else.

> **Plain-English definition 2.1: Orientation**
>
> The order of before and after along which one occurrence can follow
> another. It assumes nothing more: not that the law is irreversible, and not
> that every pair of events is ordered.

Three points fix what orientation includes.

- **It orders occurrences, not kinds of state.** A system can pass from one
  state to another and back. The same state then occurs twice, at two places
  in the order. Where events are too far apart in space for any signal to link
  them, the order can leave them unordered. It orders before and after where
  there is a before and after. It is not a single line through everything.
- **It does not require irreversibility.** A law under which every step could
  be undone still orders its steps. Irreversibility is a separate matter: the
  case in which what an act removes is never restored.
  [Chapters 7](#chapter-7) and [16](#chapter-16) show how much it changes.
- **It is an order, not a law.** It says which occurrences can come after
  which. How likely each continuation is comes from the law.

The manuscript does not take orientation as basic.
[Chapter 27](#chapter-27) gives a proposed account of where it comes from. The
argument of the rest of the manuscript needs only the order itself, and no
physical test depends on that account.

> **In Omega**
>
> Orientation gives Omega its direction. Everything that can follow from a
> present follows along it.

## 2.4 A frame picks the slice a present sits on

**A present is the world specified on a slice through its development. A
frame picks out the slice.**

To say what the world is like "now", we need to say which "now". In everyday
life the answer seems obvious. In physics it is not. Relativity shows that
observers in different states of motion slice space and time differently, and
none of their slices is the true one. So a present has to be given on a
chosen slice: a section through the world's development, cut across the order
of before and after. In a finite model, the slice is simply the state at a
declared step.

> **Plain-English definition 2.2: Frame**
>
> A choice of slice through the world's development on which a present can be
> given. It is not a thing, an observer or a point of view.

Four points fix what a frame does.

- **Any admissible slice may serve.** A slice is admissible when a present can
  be given on it: when the world specified there is complete enough for the
  law to fix what follows (section 2.5). Being a slice is not enough. It must
  support such a specification.
- **A slice can be local or global.** A local slice covers a region. The
  present on it fixes only what follows in the region it alone determines,
  because influences from outside the region can arrive later. A global slice
  covers everything within reach, and its present serves the whole field
  ([chapter 4](#chapter-4)).
- **The universe has frames. It is not one.** There are many admissible ways
  to slice the world's development, and none of them is the world.
- **Frames are not valuers.** A frame says where a present is given. It does
  not say what the present is of, or for whom anything follows. Who counts is
  settled by a different argument, from consequence, in
  [chapter 3](#chapter-3).

Two questions remain open. Which slices are admissible in general, especially
in curved spacetime and in quantum theory, is not settled. Nor is the
relation between local presents and a global one
([chapter 32](#chapter-32)). The manuscript requires that equivalent choices
of slice give the same verdict when options are compared, and states that
requirement as open until it is shown ([chapter 4](#chapter-4)).

> **In Omega**
>
> Omega contains what can follow from the present on every admissible slice.
> A frame picks out where one present sits. It does not pick out a part of
> Omega that belongs to anyone.

## 2.5 A present must be complete enough for the law

**A present is a specification of the world on a slice, full enough for the
law to fix what can follow.**

A present has to be **sufficient**. It must contain everything the law needs
in order to fix how things can continue. That includes positions and motions,
fields, chemical stocks, records, the links between a system and its
surroundings, and the state of any apparatus that will act. A simplified model
may leave out microscopic detail, but it must keep whatever its own law of
motion requires.

> **Plain-English definition 2.3: Present**
>
> The world specified on an admissible slice, fully enough for the law to fix
> what can follow from it. This includes the links between parts and the
> state of anything that will act.

What a sufficient present includes depends on the physics. In a finite
classical model, it is the full state of the model. In quantum theory, the
manuscript asks what physical systems can record. For that purpose the
present is the quantum state, the dynamics, the couplings actually in place,
and the correlations with the environment that matter for later records
([chapter 28](#chapter-28)).

Three consequences follow.

- **Two routes, one present.** A process cannot tell from its present how it
  arrived, unless the route left a trace. Two routes to the same complete
  physical state have the same continuations. The past is not lost: it stays
  in Omega, where it can be asked about. It is simply not an extra ingredient
  of the present.
- **Forgetting is not erasing.** An analyst who forgets something about a
  system has not changed the system's present. Erasing a physical memory
  does change it: the memory no longer supplies its record to the processes
  that read it. Other copies, or routes to reconstructing the record, may
  remain, each with its own physical cost.
- **A part's futures depend on its surroundings.** A cell's next hour depends
  on its nutrients, its neighbours and its temperature, not on the cell alone.
  A description of a part by itself is usually not sufficient, because what
  follows for the part depends on links it shares with the rest of the world.
  So the futures of a part are read from the present of the whole it sits in
  ([chapter 9](#chapter-9)).

> **Note: conditioning on a hidden configuration**
>
> Some descriptions of physics add variables that no record can fix exactly.
> In Bohmian mechanics, the exact position of every particle is such a
> variable (Dürr, Goldstein and Zanghì 1992). Adding that exact configuration
> to the present is a different construction from the one above. Under
> deterministic guidance it leaves one way to go on from every present. The
> two constructions answer different questions. The quantum state and
> couplings are sufficient to fix the probabilities of what can be recorded.
> They are not the complete state in Bohmian mechanics, where the exact
> configuration is part of the deterministic present. Sufficiency for what
> can be recorded and completeness of the underlying state are different
> claims. The manuscript uses the construction above, in which what can be
> recorded is fixed by the quantum state, dynamics and couplings, and names
> the other wherever it is meant. [Chapter 28](#chapter-28) takes up Bohmian
> mechanics as one description of Omega among several.

## 2.6 The law turns a present into histories

**From a present, the law fixes what can follow: whole histories, with their
probabilities.**

### The law

The third thing a present needs is the law: the actual dynamics of the world,
or of the physical model that stands in for it. It is not a menu of actions or
a set of scenarios someone finds interesting. Choices, controllers and agents
enter only as physical processes governed by the same law. A person deciding,
a thermostat switching and a reaction proceeding are all parts of what the law
carries forward.

Some physical laws give a complete present more than one possible
continuation. A radioactive nucleus may decay in the next second or it may
not, and quantum theory gives the probability of each. Other uncertainty has
a different source: a description leaves something out. Under a
deterministic law, a fully specified present has only one continuation
([chapter 3](#chapter-3) shows what follows from that). The two kinds of
uncertainty must not be confused.

Many systems are best described by a stochastic model at the level of detail
at which we can describe them. A seed may grow or rot. A small population may
spread or die out. Such a model states a law for that description, and it can
be tested as one. By itself, it does not show that the underlying physics
branches.

The law supplies probabilities over histories. In a finite model, each
history has a probability of its own. Where histories form a continuum,
probabilities attach to sets of histories, and a single exact history can
have probability zero and still be possible.

### Histories, not end points

What follows from a present is not a list of places the world might end up. It
is a set of complete histories: the full sequence of states passed through.

> **Plain-English definition 2.4: History**
>
> One complete way things can go on from a present: the sequence of states,
> or of recorded outcomes, passed through, not just where it ends.

The histories that can follow a present, with their probabilities, are its
**futures**. "Future" is used in this plain sense throughout. An option an
agent faces is a physically carried-out way of going on from its present, and
each option has futures of its own. Comparing them begins in
[chapter 4](#chapter-4).

The difference between histories and end points matters before any ethics is
stated. Take a system that stays idle, and the same system making an
excursion and returning to the same state. The end states agree. The
histories do not. During the excursion the system used resources and occupied
apparatus that something else might have used, and its surroundings may carry
the traces. In the same way, a delay that made a rescue impossible is part of
what happened, even if the final state matches that of a timely rescue. A loss
followed by recovery is not the same history as no loss at all.

Two further points keep histories honest.

- **Ending is not unknowing.** The history of a process can end: a machine
  halts, an organism dies, a reaction stops. That is a real outcome with its
  own probability, and the wider development goes on after it. An unfinished
  calculation is different. It marks the edge of what has been computed, not
  an end of what follows.
- **Only what the law permits.** The histories are those the law allows, not
  everything that can be described. What a model admits and what physics
  realizes are different questions, and the manuscript keeps them apart.

> **Primer: probability over histories**
>
> In a model with finitely many states, the law can be written as a table K.
> The entry K(x, y) is the probability that the next state is y, given that
> the present state is x. If the state is the full, sufficient state of the
> model, the probability of a whole history is the product of the
> probabilities of its steps, because each step depends only on the state it
> starts from. This is the Markov property of a complete state description
> (Norris 1997). A part's own history, read without the rest of the state,
> usually lacks this property, because what happens to it next depends on
> things outside it.

> \[
> P_x(x_1,\dots,x_n)=\prod_{t=0}^{n-1}K(x_t,x_{t+1}),\qquad x_0=x .
> \]
>
> *In words:* in a finite classical model whose state is complete, the
> probability of a history from a present is the product of the
> probabilities of its steps.

### The quantum case

In quantum theory, alternatives carry amplitudes rather than probabilities.
Amplitudes can reinforce or cancel each other, so a quantum process is not
simply a list of weighted branches.

Probabilities attach to records. When a difference becomes linked to the
surroundings, and the states of the surroundings that carry it can be told
apart, interference between the alternatives is suppressed for any process
that cannot reach the relevant part of the environment. This is decoherence.
Alternatives that have decohered behave as ordinary outcomes with ordinary
probabilities (Gell-Mann and Hartle 1993; Zurek 2003).

Decoherence does not by itself say which alternatives count as distinct. The
same quantum state gives one effective alternative under one question and two
under another ([Proposition A.14.1](#a-14-1); Dowker and Kent 1996). A
physical system's couplings settle it. They fix what that system records, and
quantum theory then gives the probabilities of the recorded histories
([Proposition A.14.3](#a-14-3)). Everything said above about histories
applies to them. The records of different systems need not fit into one
common history ([Proposition A.14.7](#a-14-7)). [Chapter 28](#chapter-28)
develops the quantum case.

## 2.7 Omega gathers every present

**Gathered from every present, what can follow is one physical object.**

Now take what can follow from every present, on every admissible slice, for
the whole world. In a classical model the picture is a branching one: from
each way things can go next, more ways open after that, each with its
probability. In quantum theory the underlying process is not a tree of
weighted branches, but records still carry probabilities fixed by the
process. Either way, the result is not a guess or anyone's opinion. It is one
definite physical object.

> **Plain-English definition 2.5: Omega, the universal possibility
> structure**
>
> Everything that can follow from every present, taken as one physical
> object. The law fixes which continuations are possible and the
> probabilities of outcomes that can be physically told apart. In quantum
> theory, those probabilities depend on the records and couplings involved.

Omega is one whole. Describing it from many slices does not multiply it. The
same structure can be read from different presents, and those readings are
readings of one thing.

But Omega is one physical structure, not one table of probabilities. In a
classical model the two coincide: the law gives one probability distribution
over all histories. In quantum theory they need not. Records made through
incompatible couplings cannot in general be combined into one ordinary
probability distribution (Dowker and Kent 1996). What Omega supplies is the
physical process from which the probabilities of any physically defined set
of records follow. What a measure of the whole field applies to in that case
is a question for Part II and [chapter 28](#chapter-28).

Omega is easy to mistake for four other things.

- **It is not a prediction.** A prediction is an attempt to describe part of
  what will happen, and it can be wrong. Omega is the structure that
  predictions try to describe.
- **It is not a belief.** A belief belongs to someone and can be mistaken.
  Omega's probabilities are set by physical law, not by anyone's confidence.
- **It is not a ranking.** Nothing in its definition says that any history is
  better than another. Whatever ordering the manuscript finds, it finds in
  the structure of Omega, not in an assumption about what is good.
- **It adds nothing to physics.** It contains no extra substance and no extra
  force. Nothing in it steers the world towards more possibility. Catalysts,
  reservoirs, interfering amplitudes and deliberating organisms shape it
  through their ordinary physical relations.

Different physical theories describe Omega differently. Standard quantum
theory, Bohmian mechanics and the many-worlds reading give the same
probabilities for records, and differ about what is ultimately actual.
Collapse theories change the dynamics slightly, and experiments already
exclude some versions of them (Donadi et al. 2021; Carlesso et al. 2022). The
manuscript treats each theory as a way of describing Omega, not as an
identification of it ([chapter 28](#chapter-28)).

Omega is far too large to write down, and no question in the manuscript needs
it written down. Every question compares a few options from the same present,
and what matters is the part of Omega the options affect.
[Chapter 4](#chapter-4) says when the rest cancels from a comparison, and
[chapter 9](#chapter-9) makes that exact. In the small exact models that test
the thesis, the part that matters is computed in full
([chapter 11](#chapter-11)).

> **In Omega**
>
> Omega is the object. Every later idea in this manuscript is defined from
> it. What follows from a present is a part of it. A measure of how much
> follows is a measure of that part. Every comparison sets two parts of it
> against each other.

## 2.8 No stock of the present settles what follows

**No single quantity the present holds, such as energy, order or stored
information, settles what can follow. What follows depends on the dynamics
and on how the world is organized.**

Once the law is fixed, what follows from a present is fixed by the present. So
any measure of what follows, including the one [chapter 5](#chapter-5)
builds, is a function of the present, given the law. That is not the idea
this section rejects. It rejects a different idea: that options can be ranked
by some stock the present holds, read off without asking what the law does
with it.

The idea is natural. We might rank options by the order the world is left in,
the energy left available, or the information stored. Each fails for the same
reason. What a stock leads to depends on the dynamics and on what is
organized around it.

- **Order.** A crystal and a cell can both be highly ordered. What follows
  from them differs enormously.
- **Energy.** A store of energy matters through what it drives. The same
  store opens different continuations with and without machinery that can
  use it. In either case its own thermal motion is part of what follows.
- **Information.** Under the unitary dynamics of standard quantum theory, the
  von Neumann entropy of a closed system stays constant (Nielsen and Chuang
  2000). It measures how mixed the state is, not how much structure it holds:
  a pure state has zero von Neumann entropy however rich its structure. Every
  option leaves the global value where it was, so it cannot rank options.
  Meanwhile the correlations between parts, the records they hold and what
  each part can use can change enormously. That is where the differences
  lie.

Two options that leave the same global quantity can differ greatly in how the
whole is organized: in its correlations, its records, and which developments
its parts can reach. The differences between options lie there.

## 2.9 Access is how the law plays out

**What a part of the world can reach depends on its machinery, records,
resources, couplings and capacity for repair. These act through the law. They
never multiply the count.**

Section 2.8 located the differences between options in what parts of the
world can reach. This section names that.

> **Plain-English definition 2.6: Access**
>
> Which developments a part of the world can actually reach, and how likely
> each is, given its machinery, records, resources, couplings and capacity
> for repair.

Access is not a quantity added to Omega. It is how the law plays out from a
particular present. Machinery that can speed a reaction makes that
reaction's continuations more likely. A record that a controller can read lets
its next step depend on what it reads. Resources used by one process are not
available to the next. Each of these changes which histories occur and how
likely each is, and it does so through the ordinary dynamics. When
[chapter 5](#chapter-5) measures how much can follow, access has already done
its work in the law. Nothing multiplies or weights the count by access
afterwards.

Three questions about any transformation are easily run together.

1. **Is it permitted?** The laws allow it.
2. **Is it ready?** Something exists that performs it reliably.
3. **Is it reachable?** That something can actually be obtained from here,
   with the resources, time and records available.

Each is a separate physical fact. A transformation can be permitted with
nothing ready to perform it. An apparatus can be possible in principle with no
route to building it. The distinction between what the laws permit and what a
physical process can reliably bring about is also the starting point of
constructor theory (Deutsch 2013; Deutsch and Marletto 2015).

Two rules follow.

- **A controller acts only on what it has.** A controller's next step can
  depend only on records it actually holds, not on facts an analyst knows. An
  operation is available only if something physically carries it out. Naming
  a reaction does not make it a choice.
- **Doing several things at once needs one route for all of them.** Two
  repairs, each possible on its own, may compete for the same fuel. That
  several things can be done together does not follow from each being
  possible. It needs one physical development in which all of them happen.

Two consequences matter later. A permitted transformation that no route can
reach from the present opens no future from that present. An accessible one
opens futures even when it is unlikely to be used
([Proposition A.2.7](#a-2-7)). And holding information is not the same as
having access: two arrangements can hold the same information and make very
different things reachable ([chapter 13](#chapter-13)).
[Chapter 11](#chapter-11) sets out what a claim of access must contain when
the models use it.

> **In Omega**
>
> Access is how the law plays out from a present. The machinery, records,
> resources and couplings in that present decide which histories in Omega
> follow from it, and with what probabilities. It is not a second structure
> laid over Omega.

## 2.10 From the object to value

**Omega is built. Whether it bears on value is the next question.**

This chapter has built a physical object without choosing anyone to count.
Omega includes everything that can follow, from every present, with its
probabilities. Insects, rivers, future people and artificial agents are in it
on the same terms as everything else, as parts of what can happen.

But nothing in Omega says that any history is better than another. A
description of what can follow is still only a description.
[Chapter 3](#chapter-3) asks how such an object could bear on value at all,
and argues that it does: value requires valuers, consequence connects them to
what follows, and so the futures open from a present are where value can
still be gained or lost.

**What this chapter does not claim.**
- That any future is better than another. Omega carries no ranking.
- That only physics matters, or that what matters is nothing but physics. It
  claims only that whatever matters happens through physical development.
- That orientation is basic. It is presupposed here, and chapter 27 offers an
  account of where it comes from.
- That frames are things, observers or valuers. A frame picks out a slice.
- That every slice is admissible, or that local and global presents are
  already related. Both are open.
- That conditioning on hidden variables is never appropriate. The
  manuscript's construction of the present is named, and so is the
  alternative.
- That one physical theory describes Omega finally. The manuscript's finite
  models are exact test cases, not claims about Omega's ultimate form.
