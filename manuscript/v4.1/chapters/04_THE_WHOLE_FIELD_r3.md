[Chapter 3](#chapter-3) found that every physical system is a valuer, and that
no valuer carries a weight. That answer to "who counts?" creates a new
problem. A cell, an organism, a forest and the universe all count, and they
contain one another. So whose futures does a comparison compare? This chapter
argues that the answer is the whole field: everything an option can reach,
taken together and counted once, with no part singled out.

The argument has three steps. Valuers that overlap cannot be scored
separately and added (4.1). Choosing valuers that do not overlap requires
boundaries, and a boundary does not carry moral authority just because it has
been drawn (4.2). What remains is a comparison of the whole field that names
no part (4.3). Comparisons of a single part stay useful, but they answer a
different question (4.4). Section 4.5 says what makes a comparison fair.
Section 4.6 names four questions that any measure of the whole field must
answer; Part II answers them. The chapter settles scope, not content. That a
wider whole field is better is the central thesis, defended in
[chapter 10](#chapter-10).

## 4.1 Why separate scores fail

**Valuers overlap. Their separate scores, added together, depend on how the
world was divided.**

The obvious way to compare options is to list the valuers, score how each
one's futures differ between the options, and add the scores. Many ethical
theories work this way with persons: one entry per life, summed.

Call this method separate scores. It fails at the first step, because valuers
are not separate. A cell lives inside an organism. The organism lives inside
a forest. The forest is part of a watershed, and the watershed is part of a
planet. A person belongs to a household, a town and a species at the same
time. Some valuers are nested, some overlap, and some are scattered, like a
population. Chapter 3 admitted all of them, because each takes part in
consequence.

Consider a forest in a drought. If the forest's score follows the forest in
full detail, it already includes what happens to its trees, because the trees
are part of the forest. Now add a separate score for each tree. Each tree's
loss is counted twice: once in its own score, and once inside the forest's. Add
the cells in each tree, and it is counted again. The drought has not changed.
Only the list has. With the forest alone, the total is one number. With the
forest and its trees, it is another. With the cells as well, it is a third.

This is not an objection to counting many consequences. A drought can harm
trees, insects and a town downstream in different ways. Those are different
consequences, and all of them count. The problem is narrower. It is the same
physical difference, entered again because two descriptions of the world
overlap.

So double counting is a symptom. The disease is that a ledger of separate
scores gives a verdict that depends on a choice of description, not on what
happens.

> **Plain-English definition 4.1: The whole field**
>
> Everything within causal reach of a present, taken together, with no part
> singled out. Its futures are the ways all of it can continue. It is not a
> sum over valuers.

> **In Omega**
>
> The whole field from a present is the part of Omega that can follow from
> that present, taken all at once.

## 4.2 Why a boundary does not settle it

**A boundary can be real without being a moral boundary.**

A defender of separate scores has an obvious reply. Choose valuers that do
not overlap. Count organisms, say, and not their cells or their forests. Each
consequence then has one owner, and nothing is counted twice.

The reply is serious, and it deserves its strongest form. Boundaries are not
inventions. A cell membrane separates the chemistry inside from the chemistry
outside. Skin, shells and bark mark where an organism's regulation stops.
Institutions have members and non-members. Physics, biology and law all depend
on drawing such lines, and they draw them well. Nothing in this chapter denies
that.

But the reply asks a boundary to do more than describe. It asks the boundary
to decide what matters. Three requirements show why that does not work.

**Universal admission.** Choosing organisms as the units does not erase what
happens to their cells. An organism's future includes the development of its
parts. But it does leave out whatever lies inside no organism: the water, the
soil, the air, and the relations between organisms that make a forest more
than a list of trees. And it gives one level of organization a special role.
Why organisms, rather than cells, forests or some other division? Chapter 3
gave no level that role. It excluded no physical participant without a reason
beyond consequence, and favouring one level brings back the kind of admission
test it rejected.

**No duplication.** One event can have many distinct consequences, and all of
them count. But describing one physical difference several times does not
create several differences. A drought that narrows a forest's futures does not
become two droughts because the same narrowing also appears in the
description of a tree. A new boundary around a consequence does not create a
new consequence. To count it again is to treat one thing as two, and that is a
distinction physics does not draw. The program's norm is that no distinction
is made without justification ([chapter 3](#chapter-3)). This is a direct
case of it.

**Independence from how the field is divided.** The same physical world can be
described as one organism, or as trillions of cells and their surroundings, or
as a forest with an organism in it. These are descriptions of one reality. A
comparison that changed with the choice among them would be ranking
descriptions, not options. A choice of description is not a physical
distinction, so the same norm says it must not change the verdict. If the
result changes when the units change, the choice of units has decided the
verdict, and nothing in the physical world has changed.

Scores for organisms alone fail the first requirement. Separate scores for
overlapping valuers fail the second. Could a cleverer method pass all three?
It could, but only in one way. To admit every participant and count each
difference once, whatever the description, the method must reconstruct a
single assessment of the whole field. It is then the whole-field comparison,
divided up for calculation. Every route that passes the three requirements
arrives at the same object of assessment: the whole field.

That conclusion is about what is assessed, not about how it is given a number.
Several ways of scoring the whole field could meet all three requirements.
Choosing among them is the work of Part II, which must justify the measure
the program uses.

**Identifying a system does not isolate it.** There is a further reason a
boundary cannot decide what matters. Even a well-identified organism is bound
up with what lies beyond it. Its constituents are replaced. It eats, breathes
and excretes. Its actions change its surroundings, and its surroundings change
it. Its successors inherit what it leaves. A boundary around it marks where
certain interactions are dense. It does not mark where the organism's
consequences stop.

A comparison that follows only one part of the world also treats as the same
any two futures that differ only outside that part. Suppose a farmer's
harvest is the same under two options. Under one, the river downstream stays
clean. Under the other, it is poisoned. A comparison confined to the farm
counts these as one future. Physics does not: the river is different. So
confining a comparison to a part is itself a judgement that two futures are
the same, and it is a judgement physics does not make.

> **Note: what the boundary research shows**
>
> Work in the physics of information supports caution about separating
> systems. Within their quantum-information framework, Fields and Glazebrook
> (2023) argue that no finite system can verify that a division of its
> environment isolates the consequences of its actions. Sandved-Smith and
> colleagues (2026) draw on that result. This is supporting research, not a
> premise. It bears on the limits of identifying unaffected systems. It does
> not by itself establish any moral conclusion, and the argument above does
> not depend on it.

**Dividing the field for calculation is still allowed.** The futures of the
whole can be split exactly into the futures of a part and the futures of the
rest, given what the part does. Nothing is counted twice in such a split
([chapter 9](#chapter-9)). Scientists should use such divisions freely. But
the split is a way of calculating one quantity for the whole. It does not
create a separate moral account for each piece.

So the claim of this section is precise. Boundaries are real. What lacks
justification is turning a useful boundary into an exclusive moral boundary
without a further argument.

> **Plain-English definition 4.2: Exclusive moral boundary**
>
> A line around a system that is treated as deciding which consequences
> count: those inside matter, and those outside do not. Identifying a system
> does not, by itself, establish one.

## 4.3 The whole field, counted once

**What remains is a comparison that names no one.**

The comparison that passes all three requirements takes the field as a whole.
Under each option, it asks how everything the options can reach can continue,
read at one level of detail. It takes no list of valuers and names no part.
So it needs no weights, no rule for overlap and no decision about where one
system ends.

> **Plain-English definition 4.3: The comparison with no index**
>
> A comparison of options that uses only how the whole field can continue
> under each option. It names no part of the field whose futures are to be
> compared.

> **In Omega**
>
> The comparison with no index reads, for each option, the part of Omega
> that follows from the present under that option. It does not project Omega
> onto any part.

Every participant is inside the field, so nothing is excluded. A change
appears once, however many valuers it falls within. And the field is one
field however it is described, so redividing it must not change the verdict.
That last point is a requirement on the measure, not yet a result. Whether the
measure built in Part II meets it is still open (section 4.5, rule 6).

This changes the starting point of ethics in a way worth stating directly.
Most ethical theories begin with a list: the beings who count. They then
argue about who belongs on it. The comparison with no index does not start
from a list. People, organisms and ecosystems are real, and the differences
between them can matter a great deal. But the physical world does not arrive
already divided into separate moral accounts. The comparison takes the world
as it is, and lets every physical difference and relationship count through
what it does to how the whole can continue.

Philosophers distinguish reasons that hold for anyone from reasons that hold
for a particular agent: agent-neutral and agent-relative reasons (Nagel 1986;
Parfit 1984). Sidgwick (1907) wrote of "the point of view of the universe".
The comparison with no index is related to these ideas, but it is not a
premise of impartiality. It does not assume that every valuer deserves equal
weight. It assigns none. Nor does it need an imagined spectator. The whole
field continues one way or another whether or not anyone observes it.

**Unchanged contributions cancel; changed relationships remain.** A comparison
of the whole field does not require knowing everything about the whole field.
Take a distant region whose own continuation is the same under both options,
and whose relation to the affected region is also the same. It adds the same
amount to both options, so it cancels from the difference
([chapter 9](#chapter-9)). The comparison then needs only what the options can
change.

Cancellation needs both conditions. A region can keep the same range of
futures of its own while its relation to another region changes. Suppose two
villages each keep the same range of possible harvests under two options. One
option ties their fates together through a single shared well. The other
keeps them independent. Neither village's own futures differ. The whole field
does, because what can happen to the two villages together is different.
Separate scores would miss this change. The comparison of the whole field keeps
it.

## 4.4 Local comparisons answer a different question

**The same count, applied to a part, can rank options differently.**

Nothing stops anyone from applying the same count to one part of the field: a
patient, a lake, a machine, a company or oneself. Such a local comparison asks
how that part's futures differ between options. It answers a real question. A
doctor needs a patient's prognosis. An ecologist needs to know what a lake can
still become. An agent deciding how to protect itself needs its own prospects.
A local comparison is not self-interest in disguise. Many valuers are not
agents, and an agent can study a part for reasons that have nothing to do with
its own advantage.

But the local comparison and the whole-field comparison ask different
questions, and their answers can differ. A part can gain while the whole
loses. Fencing a shared pasture can widen what one herder can do while
narrowing what the others can do. The reverse also happens. A part can lose
while the whole gains, as when one structure gives way to a successor that
opens more. How far the two answers differ depends on how the rest of the
field continues, and on how it is related to the part.
[Chapter 9](#chapter-9) makes that exact.

Applied by a system to its own futures, a local comparison can favour seizing
control of what others depend on. It also rewards uncontrolled variation, so
it does not always lead there. Whether, when and how often the two scopes give
different answers are questions for the models and the evidence, not for this
chapter. [Chapter 20](#chapter-20) tests the case of domination, and
[chapter 26](#chapter-26) considers what follows for artificial agents.

The distinction matters most where the measure could be misused. Anyone who
claims to be widening the future can be asked: whose? A gain in one part's
futures, bought at a larger cost to the rest, is not a gain for the whole
field. The framework's own standard rejects that claim. The defence against
misuse is not an extra rule. It is the scope argued in this chapter.

## 4.5 Fair comparison changes only the option

**A verdict can be manufactured by changing what is compared. Six rules
prevent that.**

Fixing the scope is not enough. A comparison of the whole field can still be
rigged by quietly changing what is compared. The rules below block that. They
are stated here in plain words. Appendix A gives the formal version, and
[chapter 11](#chapter-11) applies them to the models.

1. **One present.** The options must be continuations of the same present.
   Two different presents are not alternatives to each other.
2. **Ways of continuing, not chosen outcomes.** What is compared is how the
   world can continue under each option. Picking one history from each option
   would compare two settled outcomes, and a settled outcome has only one
   future.
3. **One level of detail.** Both options are read at the same grain, the same
   timing and the same horizon.
4. **Carried out, not selected.** An option is an act carried out from the
   present. It leaves open whatever the act leaves open. Selecting an outcome
   is different. It means keeping only the futures in which things went a
   certain way, and discarding the rest. That removes alternatives that the
   act itself did not remove.
5. **A stated horizon.** A comparison over a limited time is a legitimate
   partial assessment. It must say so. It must not pose as a verdict on what
   it leaves out.
6. **The same verdict under equivalent descriptions.** Equivalent descriptions
   of one comparison must give the same ranking. This is section 4.2's third
   requirement, applied to the measure. It is required but not yet proved,
   and it stays open until it is.

Rule 4 is more than a technicality. Chapter 3 showed that, under a
deterministic law, a fully specified present has one continuation. Selecting
outcomes after the fact can make two different presents look like two
options from one present. Rules 1 and 4 together prevent that mistake.

Comparisons that break these rules are not worthless. Comparing two physical
laws, or two prepared starting points, tests claims about mechanisms. Such
contrasts are good science. They are simply not choices from one present, and
they are labelled as what they are.

> **Plain-English definition 4.4: Admissible comparison**
>
> A comparison between options that are continuations of one present, read
> as ways of continuing at one level of detail and one stated horizon, and
> carried out rather than selected. Equivalent descriptions must rank them
> the same way.

## 4.6 Four questions for the measure

**Earlier measures settle four questions by choice. A measure of the whole
field cannot.**

The idea of valuing possibility is old. Theories of freedom rank sets of
opportunities (Pattanaik and Xu 1990). The capability approach asks what
people are able to do and to be (Sen 1999). Option value prices the keeping of
choices open under uncertainty. Empowerment measures how much an agent's
actions can affect what it will later sense (Klyubin and colleagues 2005).
Causal entropic forces and maximum occupancy model agents that keep many
future paths open (Wissner-Gross and Freer 2013; Ramírez-Ruiz and colleagues
2024). Research on open-endedness studies systems that keep producing
novelty.

These measures were built for their own purposes, and many serve them well.
Empowerment's focus on one agent, for example, is part of its design, not a
flaw. But a comparison of the whole field cannot borrow any of them unchanged.
Each settles four questions by choice or by design, and a comparison of the
whole field cannot leave them to choice:

- **Grain.** What counts as a different future?
- **Weighting.** How much does an unlikely future count?
- **Subject.** Whose futures are counted?
- **Noise.** Does random variation count?

This chapter has answered the third question in principle: the whole field's,
with no part named. [Chapter 9](#chapter-9) gives the exact answer. Part II
takes up all four. Physics fixes which differences are real and how likely
each future is. It does not, by itself, say which differences a calculation
should track, or how probabilities should be combined into one count. So the
measure must state its level of detail and justify it
([chapter 8](#chapter-8)). It must also justify its rule for combining
probabilities ([chapter 5](#chapter-5)). Noise counts. Whether structure
still earns an advantage over it is tested in [chapter 15](#chapter-15).

One caution belongs here. The whole field need not be finite. Its range of
futures may be infinite, so "the whole field" does not always name a number
that can be computed. Comparisons between options whose effects are local can
still be meaningful. That holds when the unchanged part cancels exactly, or
when a suitable limit exists. [Chapter 9](#chapter-9) sets out the
conditions.

## 4.7 Scope now, content later

**This chapter fixes where the comparison is made. The thesis says what it
finds.**

Chapters 3 and 4 together give a foundation without yet giving a verdict.
Value requires valuers. Every physical system is one. Their futures overlap,
and no boundary drawn around one of them decides what matters. So the
comparison that bears on value is the comparison of the whole field from a
present, made fairly.

The central thesis adds the content. It says that expansion of the whole
field's accessible futures, counted as Part II will count them, is expansion
of accessible value, and that contraction is its loss.
[Chapter 10](#chapter-10) defends that claim, and Parts IV to VI test it case
by case.

Keeping the two steps apart has a purpose. A reader can accept universal
admission and the whole-field scope while rejecting the thesis. The thesis
then stands or falls on its own evidence, and the scope does not fall with it.

**What this chapter does not claim.**
- That a wider whole field is better. That is the thesis.
- That counting many consequences of one event is double counting. Distinct
  consequences all count.
- That boundaries are unreal or useless.
- That no way of dividing the field could ever carry moral weight. The
  chapter claims only that identifying a system does not, by itself, give it
  that weight.
- That the three requirements fix a unique measure. They fix what is
  assessed; Part II justifies how it is counted.
- That the measure has been shown to give the same verdict under every
  equivalent description. That is required and still open.
- That local comparisons are illegitimate, or that they amount to
  self-interest.
- That local and whole-field comparisons usually disagree.
- That the whole field can always be computed, or is finite.
