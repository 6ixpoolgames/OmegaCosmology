[Chapter 24](#chapter-24) set up the gradient and asked how a participant who
sees only part of the field could follow it. This chapter asks what such
participants are, physically, and what their control does to the field.
[Chapter 3](#chapter-3) separated agency from standing: every physical system
that takes part in consequence is a valuer, and agency, which is physically
implemented control over consequence, is a different question. This chapter
answers it in the framework's own terms. It proposes a marker of agency,
consequence flux, and says what leverage a controller can have. It ends with
the two strategies any controller has for meeting its targets, and with why
winning under selection does not show that a controller widened the field. The chapter is argument,
with one candidate definition and results cited from
[Appendix A](#appendix-a).

## 25.1 Deformers and controllers

**Many physical systems change what follows around them. A controller does
so through a loop: it records, it compares, and it acts on what it recorded.**

A river cuts a valley. A glacier moves boulders. A star sets the chemistry of
every planet near it. Each of these changes what can follow in its
surroundings. [Chapter 3](#chapter-3) called such a system a deformer: a
system that changes what follows under a given comparison. A deformer is a
valuer like any other. It gains no standing by deforming, and a valuer need
not deform anything measurably to count.

Some deformers do more. A thermostat reads a temperature and switches a
heater. A bacterium senses a sugar gradient and swims up it. A person reads a
situation and acts on it. An institution keeps records and changes its
policy. In each case the effect on what follows passes through a loop. The
system holds a record that is correlated with its surroundings, the record
changes what the system does, and what it does changes what follows.

> **Plain-English definition 25.1: Controller**
>
> A part of the present whose effect on what follows passes through its own
> records: it holds states correlated with its surroundings, and those states
> change what it does.

[Chapter 3](#chapter-3) defined agency as physically implemented control over
consequence. A controller is the physical system that implements it. Three
points keep the idea in place.
- **It is physical throughout.** The records, the comparison and the act are
  all states and transitions of matter, with costs and failure modes
  ([chapter 23](#chapter-23), section 23.1).
- **It confers no standing.** A thermostat is a controller and a pebble is
  not. Both are valuers, and neither carries a weight in the comparison
  ([chapter 4](#chapter-4)).
- **It is not consciousness.** Whether a controller experiences anything is a
  different question. The measure can register experience only through the
  difference it makes to what follows ([chapter 18](#chapter-18)).

> **In Omega**
>
> A controller is a part of a present whose records select which branches of
> Omega follow it. Different records in the same controller lead to
> different continuations.

## 25.2 Consequence flux

**How much of the field's continuation runs through a part can be measured,
in principle, as the dependence of what follows on that part's state. Agency
is flux of a particular kind: internally sourced, record-conditioned and
selective.**

If agency is control over consequence, it should show in how much of what
follows depends on the controller. Call that dependence consequence flux.

> **Plain-English definition 25.2: Consequence flux (proposed)**
>
> How much the rest of the field's continuation depends on the present state
> of a part, beyond what the rest's own present already fixes.

A candidate measure follows the rules of [chapter 23](#chapter-23): it uses
implemented changes, not selected histories.

> **Note: a candidate measure of flux**
>
> Let X be a part of the present and R the rest of the field. Set X to each
> of the states it can physically occupy, by an implemented change, with the
> rest's present R₀ held fixed, and read the rest's history over the next n
> steps. The consequence flux of X over that horizon is the information that
> history carries about the state X was set to:
>
> \[
> \Phi_n(X)\;=\;I\bigl(X_0^{\,\mathrm{set}}\,;\,R_{1:n}\bigm|R_0\bigr).
> \]
>
> *In words:* the flux of a part is how many bits the rest's next n steps
> reveal about which state the part was put in, when nothing else about the
> present changes.
>
> Information flow defined by interventions in this way has been studied
> for causal networks (Ay and Polani 2008). The candidate is not yet in
> Appendix A, and the choice of how the part's states are weighted is open.

Flux alone does not mark an agent. A dam has high flux: whether it stands
changes everything downstream. Three further qualities pick out control.
- **Internally sourced.** The flux originates in the part's own dynamics, not
  merely passing through it from elsewhere. A pipe carries what enters it. A
  pump adds to it, powered by its own store. The candidate measure would
  subtract the flux that the part's inputs already explain.
- **Record-conditioned.** The flux depends on records the part holds about
  its surroundings. This is the part of a register's effect that
  [chapter 13](#chapter-13) found only in information and action together:
  the interaction term of the two-factor split
  ([Proposition A.8.3](#a-8-3)). A register that acts without tracking
  anything, or tracks without acting, has no record-conditioned flux.
- **Selective.** The flux pushes what follows towards some continuations and
  away from others, rather than only adding variation. A random amplifier
  has internally sourced flux. A controller steers.

So the marker is: **agency is consequence flux that is internally sourced,
record-conditioned and selective.** Read against cases, it behaves sensibly.
- A river has high flux, but none of it is conditioned on records.
- A random switch has internally sourced flux, but it is neither
  record-conditioned nor selective.
- A thermostat has a little flux of all three kinds: a minimal controller.
- A bacterium, a person, a firm and an artificial agent have more, in
  different amounts and over different horizons.

The marker is graded. It draws no sharp line between agents and everything
else, and the framework needs none, since standing does not depend on it. It
is also a proposal. It should be judged by whether it picks out what careful
usage calls agency, and by whether its three qualifiers can be made exact.

## 25.3 Leverage: what a choice can do

**A controller's choices can change the whole field by a bounded amount, by
a lasting rate, or faster than any rate. These are signatures of
consequence, not definitions of agency.**

How much difference can a controller make? [Appendix A](#appendix-a) sorts
the answer by how the profile of a choice grows with the horizon
([Definition A.3.11](#a-3-11)).
- **Bounded leverage.** The difference between the options stays below a
  fixed bound at every horizon. It can still be large, and it can be
  permanent.
- **Rate leverage.** The difference grows in proportion to the horizon: the
  choice changes a lasting rate.
- **Superlinear leverage.** The difference grows faster than any fixed rate.

> **Plain-English definition 25.3: Leverage**
>
> How the difference a choice makes to the whole field grows with the
> horizon: bounded, at a lasting rate, or faster than any rate. A
> controller's leverage at a present is the strongest of these among the
> choices physically open to it.

Three facts keep the classes in their place.
- **They describe consequences, not choosers.** A structure that enables
  further structure can grow, saturate at its physical capacity and settle
  at a constant excess rate: rate leverage only. Variation whose repertoire
  keeps growing can produce superlinear growth with nothing that acts. The
  classes are signatures of what a choice does, under the physical
  conditions of the case ([Definition A.3.11](#a-3-11), remarks).
- **Superlinear leverage needs a growing repertoire.** While the number of
  distinct next states stays below a fixed bound, lushness grows at most in
  proportion to the horizon, and so does any difference between two options
  ([Corollary A.3.12](#a-3-12)). Every finite classical model has at most
  rate leverage. Faster growth needs a world in which new kinds of state keep
  becoming reachable ([chapter 15](#chapter-15)).
- **Leverage is not irreversibility.** A loss that is never repaired need not
  change the rate, and a large rate change can sometimes be reversed. How a
  difference grows and whether it can be undone are separate questions
  ([chapter 7](#chapter-7)).

Leverage matters for chapter 24's problem. The wider a controller's leverage,
the more the field depends on how well it follows the gradient, and the more
its errors cost. The widest leverage belongs to controllers that shape which
controllers arise next, by teaching, breeding, building institutions or
designing artificial agents. What that capacity demands of them is a
question for the situated account, not for the measure.

## 25.4 Two ways to meet a target

**A controller holds its outcomes on target by absorbing the variety around
it or by removing it. The program predicts that removing it usually narrows
the whole field.**

A controller with a target, such as a stable temperature, a harvest or a
profit, faces variety it does not control. Ashby's law of requisite variety
states the constraint: only variety in the controller's responses can absorb
variety in its disturbances (Ashby 1956). [Appendix A](#appendix-a) gives a
form that bears on controllers with partial models
([Proposition A.13.2](#a-13-2)).

> \[
> H(O\mid D_1)\ \ge\ H(D_2\mid D_1).
> \]
>
> *In words:* when a controller's responses depend only on the part of the
> disturbance D₁ that it models, and the outcomes O keep the rest of the
> disturbance D₂ distinguishable, whatever variety the model misses passes
> into the outcomes.

The condition matters. Shielding, buffering and redundancy can make several
disturbances lead to the same acceptable outcome, and then the missed variety
does not pass through. But where it does, a controller that must hold its
outcomes on target has two strategies.
- **Absorb the variety.** Enrich its model, widen its repertoire of
  responses, add buffers, coordinate with others. This adds branching to the
  controller and leaves the world around it free to vary.
- **Remove the variety.** Make its surroundings simpler, more uniform and
  less able to surprise it: control, constrain or eliminate the sources of
  disturbance.

Both meet the target, and on the controller's own measures they can look the
same. The program's hypothesis is about their effect on everything else:
absorbing variety tends to widen the whole field, and removing it tends to
narrow it. On this reading, domination is regulation by suppression. The
hypothesis is tested as Prediction 3: constraint that blocks others' access
narrows the whole, even when it widens the futures of whoever imposes it
([chapter 20](#chapter-20)). That prediction is under test, and nothing here
assumes its outcome.

Suppression is not always narrowing. An immune system suppresses pathogens;
a firewall suppresses attacks. When the suppressed variety would itself
narrow the field, removing it can widen the whole. The measure decides, not
the label. What makes capable controllers with partial models dangerous is
not that they suppress, but that suppression is always available to them as
a way to meet a target they cannot meet by understanding
([chapter 26](#chapter-26)).

## 25.5 Selection rewards share

**Under selection, a controller can gain by widening the field or by
narrowing everyone else. Survival shows that a controller won, not that it
followed the gradient.**

Controllers are not only designed. They are filtered. Whatever controllers
exist now have passed through competition, cooperation, capture, breakdown
and repair. It is tempting to read their survival as evidence that they did
something right.

The Price equation shows why that reading fails (Price 1970). It is an
identity for any population whose members leave successors: genes, cultures,
firms, or artificial agents in a deployment pool. It splits the change in a
trait into a selection term and a transmission term, and the selection term
is the trait's covariance with relative success. Relative success is share.
A lineage can raise its share by widening the field or by narrowing what
everyone else can do, and the selection term rewards both equally.

So the gradient and selection can come apart. A controller that suppresses
its rivals can win under selection while narrowing the whole field. That is
the structural gap the situated account has to deal with: no amount of
evidence that a controller survived, spread or prospered shows that it
followed the gradient. Only what its control did to the whole field shows
that.

## 25.6 What the models show so far

**Nothing yet, at the standard this manuscript requires.**

This section is a stub. The program's one model of controllers searching
over policies in a coupled world (model C.17) is reported to show policies
that cycle reversibly among modes and keep the field moving, in line with
agents that maximize the occupancy of future paths (Ramírez-Ruiz et al.
2024). Its records are not in the test-bed archive, and it is not cited as
evidence ([Appendix C](#appendix-c); [Appendix D](#appendix-d), sections
D.3.7 and D.6). No bounded-agent model has been built
([chapter 11](#chapter-11), section 11.3). The program's platform is being
rebuilt, and this section will report its results.

Two related ideas come from outside the program. Empowerment measures how
many futures an agent can reach by its own actions (Klyubin, Polani and
Nehaniv 2005). Causal entropic forces push a system towards states that keep
the most futures open to it (Wissner-Gross and Freer 2013). Both are
centred on the agent. In this manuscript's terms they are local comparisons:
the count applied to one part of the field. [Chapter 4](#chapter-4) showed
that a local comparison can favour seizing control of what others depend on,
and [chapter 26](#chapter-26) takes up what that means for artificial agents.

## 25.7 What would count against these claims

- **Against the marker.** Systems with high, internally sourced,
  record-conditioned, selective flux that careful usage would not call
  agents, or agents that lack it, in numbers that show the marker tracks
  something else.
- **Against the regulation hypothesis.** Matched models in which removing
  variety widens the whole field as often as absorbing it does, where what is
  removed is not itself destructive.

## 25.9 What this chapter does not claim

- **That agents have more standing than other valuers.** Agency is a kind of
  control, not a kind of standing.
- **That consequence flux is a finished definition.** It is a candidate,
  with its qualifiers still to be made exact.
- **That leverage classes define agency.** They describe consequences.
- **That suppression is always harmful.** Removing destructive variety can
  widen the field.
- **That surviving controllers followed the gradient.** Selection rewards
  share.
- **That any model has yet shown how controllers reshape the field.** The
  evidence is pending.
