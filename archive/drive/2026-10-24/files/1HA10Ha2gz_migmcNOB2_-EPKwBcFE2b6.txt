PERSISTENCE, IDENTITY, AND AGENCY
v2.5 checkpoint
Current through 21 September 2026

CURRENT STATUS

The program treats persistent things as robust patterns in physical continuation rather than as primitive immutable objects. Identity is therefore tied to continuity of organization, causal role, memory, reconstruction, and consequence-bearing structure across change.

Agency is a stronger property. A persistent pattern becomes agent-like when its internal state and policy feed back into continuation in a way that systematically changes which futures remain available, reachable, or maintainable.

The core progression is:

    robust physical pattern
        -> persistence across change
        -> self-maintenance / recovery
        -> endogenous feedback
        -> policy-sensitive deformation
        -> agency.

Persistence, identity, agency, valuerhood, consciousness, and moral standing are distinct layers. They can overlap in real systems without being interchangeable.


1. PATTERNS AS THE BASIC EFFECTIVE UNITS

A macroscopic thing need not be fundamental in order to be physically real.

Let X_S denote a set of lower-level degrees of freedom and let

    Z_P = pi_P(X_S)

be an effective variable describing some organized pattern P.

A useful pattern is one whose effective description captures real correlations and predictive structure rather than an arbitrary grouping.

Candidate marks of robust patternhood include:

- persistence over nonzero continuation;
- predictive compression;
- stability across nearby descriptions;
- dynamical closure or partial closure;
- intervention relevance;
- recoverability after perturbation;
- consequence preservation.

A vortex is a useful analogy. Its molecules change continuously, yet a coherent higher-level structure persists and has stable causal effects.

The stronger general proposal is:

    thinghood is approximated by robust correlated patternhood.


2. IDENTITY WITHOUT MATERIAL CONSTANCY

Many important systems replace their material constituents.

Examples include:

- organisms;
- ecosystems;
- institutions;
- software processes;
- distributed control systems;
- rivers;
- vortices.

Identity cannot therefore be reduced to retention of the same microscopic particles.

A more useful criterion is continuity of organized relations.

Schematically:

    constituent turnover
        + causal reconstruction
        + preserved records
        + preserved continuation role
        -> effective identity.

Identity is strongest when later states remain attributable to the same organized process in a way that supports prediction, intervention, memory, and responsibility.


3. VORTICALITY

The project uses the term vorticality for organization that persists through recurrent throughput and self-reconstruction.

Its schematic form is:

    uptake
        -> transformation
        -> outward effect
        -> environmental feedback
        -> renewed conditions of persistence.

The important feature is not literal rotation.

It is regenerative organization across a boundary.

A vortical pattern may:

- replace its constituents;
- maintain internal relations;
- process matter or information;
- alter its surroundings;
- use those alterations to preserve itself;
- retain or regenerate control channels.

This makes vorticality relevant to organisms, institutions, distributed agents, and other process-like identities.

Vorticality by itself does not imply agency, consciousness, valuerhood, or standing.


4. PERSISTENCE

Persistence means that a recognizable consequence-bearing organization remains through some continuation interval.

A minimal notion is:

    P_t ~ P_{t+Delta}

under an equivalence relation that preserves the organization relevant to the inquiry.

The relation cannot be defined by superficial similarity alone.

It should preserve whichever features make the pattern causally and dynamically the same effective process.

Persistence can be:

- structural;
- functional;
- informational;
- causal;
- genealogical;
- phenomenal;
- institutional.

Different applications may require different combinations.


5. RECOVERABILITY

Persistence under ordinary conditions is weaker than recoverability.

Recoverability asks whether a pattern can return to a viable region after perturbation.

Let V denote a viability region and let R be an allowed repair class.

Then one can ask whether, after disturbance d,

    exists r in R

such that the system returns to V within horizon H.

Recoverability matters because two systems that look similar now can differ dramatically in continuation.

One may possess:

- repair mechanisms;
- redundancy;
- reserve capacity;
- correction channels;
- regeneration pathways.

The other may be one perturbation away from irreversible collapse.


6. SELF-MAINTENANCE

A persistent pattern can survive passively because the environment happens to preserve it.

Self-maintenance is stronger.

A pattern is self-maintaining when its own dynamics contribute causally to the conditions required for its persistence.

Examples include:

- homeostasis;
- immune repair;
- error correction;
- resource acquisition;
- institutional maintenance;
- software watchdog processes.

This introduces reflexivity:

    the system acts on conditions that help sustain the system.

Self-maintenance is a major precursor to agency.


7. CONTROL

Control enters when a system can select among actions or transformations whose consequences differ.

A simple controlled dynamical model is:

    x_{t+1} = F(x_t, a_t, e_t),

where x is the system state, a is an action, and e is environment.

If actions are produced by an internal policy,

    a_t ~ pi(. | m_t),

and internal memory or organization updates as

    m_{t+1} = G(m_t, o_{t+1}, a_t),

then the system contains an endogenous control loop.

The physical requirement is counterfactual:

    changing the internal policy or state changes continuation.

Without that dependence, the policy variable is only descriptive decoration.


8. AGENCY

Agency is policy-sensitive deformation of continuation generated by an endogenous feedback organization.

A useful working definition is:

    an agent is a persistent deformer whose internal policy,
    memory, or model counterfactually steers its own and/or
    surrounding continuation.

Important components include:

- action channels;
- internal state;
- feedback;
- observation;
- policy dependence;
- persistence of the control loop;
- correction;
- adaptation.

Stronger agency may include:

- planning;
- model-based prediction;
- self-modification;
- tool use;
- coordination;
- learning;
- expansion of action repertoire.


9. DEFORMERS

Agency is a special case of a broader category: deformer.

A deformer is any pattern whose presence or state changes continuation.

Examples include:

- a river;
- a dam;
- a hurricane;
- an organism;
- a computer;
- an institution;
- an optimizer.

To identify deformation, compare matched conditions:

    F | do(D = d_1)

versus

    F | do(D = d_2).

The difference between the resulting continuation structures is attributable to the changed state of D under the declared causal model.

A deformer need not be an agent.

A large asteroid can radically deform continuation with no endogenous policy at all.


10. DEFORMATION MAGNITUDE AND BREADTH

A system can have a large causal effect in only one direction.

Another can control many independent continuation dimensions.

These should be separated.

Deformation magnitude asks:

    How far does changing the system move continuation?

Deformation breadth asks:

    How many independent consequential directions can the system select or influence?

A one-bit catastrophic trigger may have high magnitude but low breadth.

A compact adaptive controller may have moderate magnitude but high breadth.


11. EFFECTIVE CONTROL VARIETY

Ashby's law of requisite variety provides a useful reference point.

A regulator must possess enough effective response variety to meet the relevant variety of disturbances.

The key word is effective.

Random internal complexity does not automatically increase control capability.

A good agentive representation therefore cares about:

- distinguishable disturbances;
- usable responses;
- retained correction channels;
- the mapping between them.

This connects agency to consequence geometry rather than raw internal complexity.


12. MAINTAINED OPTIONALITY

Not every reachable option is useful or stable.

A useful hierarchy is:

    raw optionality
        reachable alternatives

    viable optionality
        alternatives remaining within constraints

    robust optionality
        viable alternatives across uncertainty

    controlled optionality
        alternatives selectable through policy

    maintained optionality
        controlled alternatives retained while preserving
        the ability to continue controlling and correcting.

Agency is particularly associated with controlled and maintained optionality.

A system that can choose among many actions but destroys its own ability to learn or correct may have large immediate control and weak durable agency.


13. SELF-MODIFICATION

Advanced agents can modify:

- their policies;
- their models;
- their goals;
- their bodies;
- their interfaces;
- their control architecture.

Self-modification creates a continuity problem.

The agent should be able to change while retaining enough correction capacity to detect when the change has become destructive or misaligned with its own ongoing organization.

This makes preservation of the correction loop an important part of robust agency.


14. GENERATIVITY

Generativity is stronger than persistence and stronger than ordinary control.

A generative system reaches states that expose new consequential capabilities.

Examples include:

- building a tool;
- learning a new model;
- developing language;
- reproducing;
- founding an institution;
- inventing a technology.

The structural form is:

    current continuation
        -> generative transition
        -> new state
        -> new consequence directions.

Recursive generativity occurs when generated structures themselves become generators.


15. INTEGRATION

Integration constrains lower-level degrees of freedom.

This can reduce local independent continuation while creating new higher-level capabilities.

An organism is the central example.

Cells lose many independent trajectories, but coordinated organization enables:

- locomotion;
- perception;
- repair;
- learning;
- reproduction.

Therefore reduction of lower-level optionality is not automatically loss.

The correct question is what higher-level continuation becomes possible and what lower-level structures remain recoverable or represented.


16. IDENTITY ACROSS SCALE

A single physical system can support identities at several scales.

Examples:

    cells
        -> tissues
        -> organism
        -> group
        -> institution.

These identities can overlap without being identical.

A higher-level agent can constrain lower-level agents.

A lower-level component can remain a persistent process while participating in a larger control structure.

This creates genuine multiscale questions about:

- attribution;
- responsibility;
- recovery;
- independence;
- capture;
- valuer individuation.

No one scale is assumed to settle all of them.


17. PERSISTENCE AND CONSEQUENCE

Persistence is important because it makes consequences compound.

A transient perturbation may vanish.

A persistent organization can:

- accumulate records;
- redirect resources;
- retain learning;
- compound decisions;
- generate descendants;
- reorganize its environment.

Thus even modest local agency can acquire large long-horizon consequence through persistence.


18. AGENCY AND PERSPECTIVE

An agent acts from a situated Perspective.

It does not possess the universal physical state.

Its policy is based on:

- local records;
- bounded sensing;
- internal models;
- computational limits;
- current control interfaces.

Agency is therefore naturally perspectival.

The agent's effective continuation is the subset of universal possibility physically available from its situated boundary.


19. AGENCY IS NOT VALUERHOOD

An agent can systematically steer continuation without any claim yet that the outcomes matter to the system in the stronger valuer sense.

Examples may include:

- narrow automated controllers;
- optimization systems;
- infrastructure control loops.

Conversely, a valuer may have very little external causal power.

Thus the project separates:

    causal influence;
    agency;
    valuerhood;
    standing.

This separation becomes essential in ethics because power cannot be used as a proxy for moral relevance.


20. CURRENT RESEARCH TARGET

The present technical target is to derive higher-level agents from the same consequence framework used in the physical layer.

The desired chain is:

    physical process
        -> robust pattern
        -> sufficient predictive state
        -> self-maintaining feedback loop
        -> policy-sensitive continuation
        -> agent.

The next document asks when continuation differences become endogenous stakes rather than merely controlled alternatives.
