This appendix states the results on which the argument depends, and proves
each of them once, in one notation. The chapters state results in words and
refer here.

Every result is labelled by kind.
- **A theorem** is either proved here or, where it belongs to an established
  literature, stated with its source and proved in outline.
- **A conditional proposition** rests on physical premises. The premises are
  named, and they are not proved.

Each result ends with its scope: the conditions outside which it may fail.

Several results in sections A.4 and A.5 are new to this manuscript in their
application, not in their mathematics. Where the mathematics is known, its
source is given, and the claim made here is only the reading.

## A.1 Setting and notation

**Standing assumptions.** Unless a result says otherwise, it concerns a
**finite classical model**:
- a finite state space S;
- a stochastic kernel K on S, where K(x, y) is the probability of a step from
  x to y.

The step index counts time at the declared cadence (chapter 7). A **present**
is a state x ∈ S. When the preparation is itself randomized, it is a
probability distribution μ on S.

**Orientation.** The step index orders states from earlier to later, and
every result below presupposes that order. Without it nothing follows from a
present, and there is no continuation structure to measure. The order is all
that is required. The kernel itself need not be irreversible, and a reversible
law still has a future once its steps are ordered. Irreversibility enters
separately, as the case in which what an early end removes is never restored
(Proposition A.4.9). Chapter 28 discusses where the order comes from.

**Frames.** Orientation gives a future. A frame gives the standpoint from which
it is defined. Every continuation structure in this appendix is defined from a
present of some frame: the whole field's, unless a projection is named
(Definition A.7.1).

**Horizons as sections.** In general the horizon is a later admissible
section Γ along the orientation, and the log lushness ℓ(x; Γ) is the entropy
of the resolved history law from the frame's present x to Γ, under the native
law. In a finite classical model with a declared cadence, Γ is the section n
steps later, and ℓ(x; Γ) = ℓ_n(x). Three things follow.
- **The conditioning is declared.** For a frame smaller than the whole field,
  a comparison states whose histories are counted and on which present they
  are conditioned (Definition A.7.1). For the whole field there is no choice
  to make.
- **Bookkeeping adds nothing.** A section inserted only to group histories
  already resolved leaves the count unchanged (Corollary A.2.6). Resolving
  further physical events changes the history law itself (section A.6).
- **Where the field has no end, the object is the profile.** The map
  Γ ↦ ℓ(x; Γ) exists at each section. A rate, an offset or a finite limit is
  a reading of it where it exists (Corollary A.3.9), and need not exist when
  the state space grows or the law changes (Theorem A.3.5, scope).

**Histories.** The law of the first n steps from x is

\[
P_x(x_1,\dots,x_n)=\prod_{t=0}^{n-1}K(x_t,x_{t+1}),\qquad x_0=x .
\]

**The measure.**
- The **step entropy** is g(x) = −Σ_y K(x, y) log₂ K(x, y).
- The **log lushness** of n steps from x is the entropy of the history law,
  ℓ_n(x) = H(X_1, …, X_n | X_0 = x), in bits.
- The **lushness** is L_n(x) = 2^{ℓ_n(x)}, the effective number of distinct
  n-step histories.

L and ℓ rank developments identically. Every number quoted in bits is a value
of ℓ.

**What the quantity is called.** ℓ_n is the path entropy of the resolved
histories, conditioned on the full present. The entropy of the future paths
from a present over a horizon is also called causal path entropy
(Wissner-Gross and Freer 2013). L_n, its exponential, is the effective number
of resolved continuation histories: the number of equally likely histories
that would have the same entropy (Jost 2006). The formula is established. What
this appendix adds is the construction of the histories, the reason this count
is the unique one (Theorem A.2.3), and the comparisons built on it. One term
needs care. In language modelling, "perplexity" usually means a per-step
quantity, whose counterpart here is 2^h, with h the rate (Definition A.3.1).
Lushness is 2^{ℓ_n}, which covers the whole history to the horizon, and 2^h is
never called lushness.

**Vectors and kernels.**
- Functions on S are column vectors and distributions are row vectors, so
  (Kf)(x) = Σ_y K(x, y) f(y) and μf = Σ_y μ(y) f(y).
- Kᵗ is the t-step kernel.
- **1** is the function with value one everywhere.
- ‖f‖∞ = max_x |f(x)|.
- A stationary distribution π satisfies πK = π.

**Controlled models.** A controlled model has kernels K_a indexed by actions.
A policy chooses actions as a function of the state or of the history, and
induces a law on histories. A stationary policy σ induces a kernel K_σ.

**Random horizons.** A random horizon N is a random number of steps,
independent of the development. Under a constant hazard λ,
P(N = n) = λ(1 − λ)ⁿ for n = 0, 1, 2, …, so E[N] = (1 − λ)/λ. Results are
stated in terms of E[N]; this removes any dependence on how the first step is
counted.

### Definition A.1.1 (continuation structure at a resolution)

Fix a present x, a declared resolution ρ = (τ, f) (Definition A.6.1) and a
horizon of n steps.
- A **resolved history** is a sequence (f(X₁), …, f(X_n)) of states at grain
  f, read at cadence τ.
- The **continuation structure** of x is the pair (Ω, P):
  - Ω is the set of resolved histories that have positive probability;
  - P is their probability law, induced by the law of the development.
- Two continuation structures are the **same** if a probability-preserving
  bijection maps one onto the other.

The lushness of x at resolution ρ is the lushness of its continuation
structure, ℓ = H(P) and L = 2^ℓ. When f is the identity and τ is the native
step, this is the ℓ_n(x) defined above.

### Proposition A.1.2 (what any measure of the continuation structure respects)

Let B be any function of continuation structures. Then

> \[
> B(p_{\sigma(1)},\dots,p_{\sigma(k)})=B(p_1,\dots,p_k)\ \text{ for every permutation }\sigma,\qquad
> B(p_1,\dots,p_k,0)=B(p_1,\dots,p_k).
> \]
>
> *In words:* a measure of what can follow from a present cannot depend on what
> the histories are called, or on histories that cannot happen.

**Proof.**
- **Relabelling.** A relabelled structure is the same structure, by
  Definition A.1.1.
- **Impossible histories.** Ω contains only histories of positive probability,
  so adding one of probability zero leaves the structure unchanged. ∎

**Scope.** The proposition is nearly empty by design. It records that a
measure of the continuation structure can respond only to the resolved
histories and their probabilities. Anything else enters only as a separate
criterion.

**Quantum models.** A decoherent family of histories carries ordinary
probabilities, and every result below applies to such a family once it is
fixed. Section A.14 shows why fixing it is not innocent, and how a physical
frame's records fix it.

## A.2 The measure

### Theorem A.2.1 (chain rule)

For a finite classical model and a present x,

> \[
> \ell_n(x)=\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}(K^tg)(x),\qquad \ell_{n+1}=g+K\ell_n,\qquad \ell_0=0 .
> \]
>
> *In words:* the lushness of a horizon is the expected branching of each
> step, summed over the horizon, and it can be computed one step at a time
> without listing the histories.

**Proof.** By the chain rule for entropy (Cover and Thomas 2006, Theorem
2.5.1), H(X_1, …, X_n | X_0 = x) is the sum over t < n of
H(X_{t+1} | X_0 = x, X_1, …, X_t). By the Markov property, the t-th term is
the expected step entropy at the state reached at time t, which is (Kᵗg)(x).
Splitting off the first step gives the recurrence. ∎

### Corollary A.2.2 (three consequences)

(a) **Independent processes.**
- If processes A and B evolve independently, ℓ_AB = ℓ_A + ℓ_B.
- If they are coupled, ℓ_AB = ℓ_A + ℓ_B − I(A; B), where the mutual
  information I(A; B) is never negative.

(b) **Absorbing states.** A state y with K(y, y) = 1 has g(y) = 0. It adds no
branching, and a development absorbed there keeps its weight.

(c) **Preparations.** For a randomized preparation μ, the entropy of the whole
history including the starting state is H(μ) + Σ_{t<n} μKᵗg. The entropy
conditional on the starting state omits H(μ).
- A comparison must declare which of the two it uses.
- From an exact present, the two coincide.

**Proof.**
- (a) H(A, B) = H(A) + H(B) − I(A; B), and I(A; B) = 0 exactly under
  independence (Cover and Thomas 2006, chapter 2).
- (b) A point mass has zero entropy.
- (c) Apply the chain rule with X₀ included. ∎

### Theorem A.2.3 (the unique effective count of resolved continuations)

Let B assign a positive number to every finite continuation structure, written
through its probability vector p. Suppose B satisfies four conditions:
1. **sufficiency:** B is a function of the continuation structure, so it obeys
   Proposition A.1.2;
2. **continuity:** B(p, 1 − p) is continuous in p;
3. **recursive composition:** for a branching in two stages, with first-stage
   law q and second-stage laws p⁽ⁱ⁾,
   log₂ B(joint) = log₂ B(q) + Σ_i q_i log₂ B(p⁽ⁱ⁾);
4. **calibration:** B(1/k, …, 1/k) = k.

Then

> \[
> B(p)=2^{H(p)},\qquad H(p)=-\sum_i p_i\log_2p_i .
> \]
>
> *In words:* any effective count of resolved continuations meets four
> conditions: it depends only on the continuation structure, varies
> continuously with it, gives the same answer whether a branching future is
> evaluated whole or stage by stage, and counts k equally likely futures as k.
> Any such count is the exponential of Shannon's entropy, which is the
> perplexity of the continuation structure. Lushness is that count.

**Source and outline.** Write F = log₂ B.
- **The four conditions give Faddeev's (1956).**
  - Condition 1 gives symmetry.
  - Condition 2 gives continuity.
  - Condition 3 includes grouping: take a first stage that merges the
    outcomes 1 and 2, and a second stage that splits them.
  - Condition 4 with k = 2 gives F(½, ½) = 1.
- **Faddeev's theorem then applies.** It refines Shannon's characterization
  (Shannon 1948) and Khinchin's (1957). Its outline:
  1. Grouping and symmetry make F(1/n, …, 1/n) additive in n.
  2. Continuity makes it c·log n.
  3. The normalization fixes c.
  4. Grouping extends the result to rational vectors, and continuity to all
     vectors.
- **Conversely,** 2^H satisfies all four conditions; condition 3 is the chain
  rule. ∎

**Why each condition holds.**
- **Sufficiency and calibration are definitional.** Sufficiency is
  Proposition A.1.2. Calibration is what "effective number" means.
- **Continuity is required by measurement.** Physical laws are known to
  finite precision. A measure that jumped under arbitrarily small changes in
  the law would let differences no measurement can detect decide verdicts.
- **Recursive composition is required for consistent evaluation over time.**
  The same continuation structure can be presented in one stage or in many.
  An actor deciding step by step must be able to evaluate the future stage by
  stage and reach the verdict it would reach by evaluating the whole. The
  decision recursion (Theorem A.13.1) uses this condition at every step.
  Without it, a plan judged best now need not remain best when judged again
  later.

**Remarks.**
- **Grouping fails for the alternatives.** The Rényi entropies of order
  α ≠ 1 (Rényi 1961) are additive for independent systems but violate
  recursive composition. For (½, ¼, ¼), the order-2 entropy is
  −log₂ 0.375 = 1.415 bits, while composing in two stages gives 1 + ½ = 1.5
  bits.
- **Similarity weighting is excluded by sufficiency, not by a value
  judgement.** Measures that weight alternatives by their similarity (Leinster
  and Cobbold 2012) depend on a similarity matrix, and that matrix is not part
  of the continuation structure. There are two cases.
  - **The similarity has physical consequences:** different recoveries,
    capacities, interactions or downstream dynamics. Then, at an admissible
    resolution and over a long enough horizon, those consequences already
    appear as different resolved histories with different probabilities. They
    need no second weight.
  - **It has none.** Then weighting by it adds a separate criterion. It
    measures how diverse the futures are according to a chosen geometry, not
    how many futures the present supports.

  Physics determines what counts as the same and what counts as different.
  That is the resolution. Probability then determines the effective count.
  There is no third stage at which distinct resolved histories are counted as
  partly the same.
- **Where the commitment now lies.** The derivation moves the substantive
  commitment to two places.
  - **The resolution and horizon.** Are they fine and long enough for every
    consequential distinction to appear in the continuation structure? See
    Definition A.6.5 and section A.4.
  - **The central thesis.** Does what the measure compresses away matter
    (section A.15)?

  For quantum systems, the resolved histories are a frame's recorded
  histories (Proposition A.14.3). The derivation is complete for classical
  models, and for any quantum frame whose record structure is fixed. What
  fixes a record structure physically is open (section A.15).

### Proposition A.2.4 (repertoire ceiling)

With R(x) = {y : K(x, y) > 0}, the set of states reachable in one step,

> \[
> g(x)\le\log_2\lvert R(x)\rvert,\qquad
> \ell_n(x)\le\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\mathbb E_x\bigl[\log_2\lvert R(X_t)\rvert\bigr],
> \qquad h(x)\le\max_y\log_2\lvert R(y)\rvert\le\log_2\lvert S\rvert .
> \]
>
> *In words:* a step cannot branch more than the logarithm of the number of
> states it can reach, so no rate can exceed the logarithm of the largest
> repertoire.

**Proof.** The entropy of a distribution on m points is at most log₂ m, with
equality exactly for the uniform distribution (Cover and Thomas 2006, Theorem
2.6.4). Apply this to K(x, ·), and sum along the development by Theorem
A.2.1. The rate is an average of step entropies (Corollary A.3.4 below), so it
obeys the same bound. ∎

**Scope.**
- A change of K that leaves every R(x) unchanged can raise ℓ_n only up to the
  bound. Raising the bound itself requires changing which states are visited,
  or the repertoires.
- The bound is relative to the declared state space. Refining the grain
  enlarges S and the repertoires.

### Proposition A.2.5 (control invariance)

Let two physical implementations induce, from their presents, the same law on
the declared histories over n steps. Then

> \[
> \ell_n^{(1)}=\ell_n^{(2)} .
> \]
>
> *In words:* lushness sees only the law of the declared histories, so two
> processes with the same law have the same lushness, whoever or whatever
> implements them.

**Proof.** ℓ_n is the entropy of the history law, which is a function of that
law alone. ∎

**Scope.** The result is exact and nearly empty. Its use is negative.
- A difference between consent and coercion, or between distributed and
  concentrated control, can matter to lushness only through a difference in
  the declared law (section A.10).
- Whether the declared histories include the implementing structure is part
  of the declared resolution (section A.6).
- **Flexibility.** A controller's capacity to change its policy enters the
  history law wherever it is physically accessible from the present, however
  unlikely its use. The switching, and what follows it, are then branches of
  the same continuation (Corollary A.2.6, with E the history of policy
  selection), and Proposition A.2.7 gives the sign. Flexibility that is
  physically inaccessible from the present adds nothing to it. Running a
  different policy is a different option, compared as such (Definition
  A.3.6(b); Theorem A.13.1); that comparison does not add futures to the
  original present.

*Verification:* V41 (a policy register that switches with probability 0.1
gives 10.51 bits at eight steps, against 8.00 for a fixed random policy; with
switching probability zero the two laws coincide and tie).

### Corollary A.2.6 (branch volume)

For a present x, a horizon n and any function E of the resolved history,

> \[
> \ell=H(E)+\sum_e P(E=e)\,\ell_{\mid e},\qquad
> L=2^{H(E)}\prod_e L_{\mid e}^{\,P(E=e)},
> \]
>
> where ℓ_{|e} is the entropy of the history law given E = e, and
> L_{|e} = 2^{ℓ_{|e}}. When E is the history up to step k,
> ℓ_{|e} = ℓ_{n−k}(x_k), the lushness from the state that branch has reached.
>
> *In words:* the effective breadth of a future is the effective number of
> branches through any intermediate stage, times the geometric mean of the
> breadths within them, each branch continuing from where it actually stands.
> Grouping the same history differently leaves the answer unchanged.

**Proof.** E is a function of the history Y, so H(Y) = H(E) + H(Y | E), and
H(Y | E) = Σ_e P(E = e) H(Y | E = e) (Cover and Thomas 2006, chapter 2).
Exponentiate. When E = (X₁, …, X_k), the Markov property gives
H(X_{k+1}, …, X_n | X₀ = x, X₁, …, X_k) = ℓ_{n−k}(X_k) on each branch. ∎

**Scope.**
- It is the recursive-composition condition of Theorem A.2.3, written
  multiplicatively, and it adds no assumption.
- Each branch continues from its full residual state, with spent resources,
  records and correlations with its surroundings included. Continuing from a
  coarser description of the branch would count a different structure.
- A stage that resolves nothing new leaves L unchanged. Resolving further
  physical events changes the history, and can change L (Proposition A.6.2;
  Theorem A.6.3).

*Verification:* V39 (twenty random chains, with E the prefix and with a
coarse function of the state at step k; largest error 3 × 10⁻¹⁵ bits).

### Proposition A.2.7 (accessible possibility)

At one opportunity, let a possibility be taken with probability ε. Let P₀ be
the law of the resolved histories when it is not taken, which is the law
without it, and P₁ the law when it is taken, so that the law with it is
P_ε = (1 − ε)P₀ + εP₁.

> (a) If P₁ puts mass m > 0 on histories outside the support of P₀, then
> \[
> H(P_\varepsilon)-H(P_0)=\varepsilon\, m\log_2\frac1\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon),
> \]
> which is positive for every small enough ε > 0 and tends to zero with ε.
>
> (b) If P₁ is supported within the support of P₀, then
> \[
> H(P_\varepsilon)-H(P_0)=\varepsilon\bigl[H(P_1;P_0)-H(P_0)\bigr]+o(\varepsilon),
> \qquad H(P_1;P_0)=-\sum_y P_1(y)\log_2P_0(y),
> \]
> which can have either sign.
>
> (c) If ε = 0, nothing is added.
>
> *In words:* possibility that is physically inaccessible adds nothing.
> Possibility that is accessible and opens futures that were otherwise
> impossible adds to lushness once it is unlikely enough: by an amount that
> vanishes with its probability, but is positive. How unlikely is enough
> depends on where taking it leads. A possibility that only redirects the
> development among futures already possible can add or subtract. At larger
> probabilities, taking a possibility can narrow what follows, so the net
> sign then depends on where it leads.

**Proof.**
- (a) Split the entropy over the support S₀ of P₀ and its complement. Outside
  S₀ the law is εP₁, contributing
  εm log₂(1/ε) − ε Σ_{y∉S₀} P₁(y) log₂ P₁(y). On S₀ every term
  (1 − ε)P₀(y) + εP₁(y) stays bounded away from zero for small ε, so that part
  is differentiable at ε = 0 with value H(P₀) and a finite derivative.
- (b) All terms stay positive, so H(P_ε) is differentiable at ε = 0, with
  derivative −Σ_y (P₁(y) − P₀(y)) log₂ P₀(y) = H(P₁; P₀) − H(P₀); the terms
  in log₂ e cancel because Σ_y (P₁(y) − P₀(y)) = 0.
- (c) P_ε = P₀. ∎

**Scope.**
- **Several opportunities.** By Corollary A.2.6 the result applies at each
  opportunity in turn, conditional on the history before it.
- **A difference must be resolved.** A possibility whose taking leaves no
  trace at the declared resolution, and changes nothing that follows, adds no
  histories; it falls under (b) or adds nothing.
- **Continuity.** The gain in (a) tends to zero with ε, as condition 2 of
  Theorem A.2.3 requires. An accessible possibility counts for little when it
  is very unlikely, and never for nothing.
- **Possibility volume and lushness.** An accessible possibility that opens
  otherwise impossible histories enlarges the support of the history law at
  every positive probability. Lushness, the effective number, grows with it
  once the probability is small enough.
- **How small is small enough.** The threshold depends on the case and on the
  horizon. If taking the possibility leads to a lower rate for the rest of a
  horizon of T steps, the leading gain must outweigh a loss that grows with T.
  With every history of P₁ new (m = 1) and a drop of Δh bits per step, the
  gain is positive only when log₂(1/ε) exceeds about ΔhT − log₂ e: at 4,096
  steps and half a bit per step, ε below about 2^−2047. The threshold is
  established case by case, never presumed. If taking the possibility leads
  to a higher rate, the gain is positive at every probability.
- **The law without it is held fixed.** If keeping the possibility available
  has a standing cost, such as maintaining the machinery that makes it
  accessible, the law without it changes too, and the comparison is between
  two laws (Definition A.3.6(c); Proposition A.8.2, scope).
- **Finite models.** In models with continuous outcomes, events of
  probability zero can still be physically possible. The statement here is
  for finite models, where accessible and of positive probability coincide.

*Verification:* V42 (in 200 random cases, opening new histories gave a gain
of at least 1.6 × 10⁻⁵ bits at every ε ≤ 10⁻³, and redirecting onto the most
likely existing history always subtracted; the gain matched ε log₂(1/ε) to
within 0.3 per cent at ε = 10⁻⁴ and 10⁻⁶); V46 (with a drop of half a bit per
step after the possibility is taken, log₂(1/ε*) = T/2 − log₂ e to four
decimals at T = 64, 256, 1,024 and 4,096; with a gain of half a bit per step,
the result is positive at every probability tested).

## A.3 Rate, offset and horizon

Lushness is cumulative, and two numbers summarize how it grows:
- **the rate:** its long-run growth per step;
- **the offset:** the lasting difference a present makes beyond the rate.

This section defines both for every finite kernel. It then proves the identity
that relates them to lushness at every horizon.

### Definition A.3.1 (rate and offset)

For a finite kernel K, the Cesàro limit Π = lim_{n→∞} n⁻¹ Σ_{t<n} Kᵗ exists
and satisfies KΠ = ΠK = Π² = Π (Kemeny and Snell 1960). The matrix I − K + Π
is invertible (Lemma A.3.2), and its inverse Z is the **fundamental matrix**.
- The **rate** is h = Πg.
- The **offset** is b = Z(g − h).

### Lemma A.3.2 (properties)

(i) I − K + Π is invertible.
(ii) Kh = h and Πh = h.
(iii) ΠZ = ZΠ = Π.
(iv) Πb = 0 and (I − K)b = g − h.

**Proof.**
- (i) Suppose (I − K + Π)v = 0.
  - Applying Π, and using ΠK = Π² = Π, gives Πv = 0.
  - Then Kv = v, so v = n⁻¹ Σ_{t<n} Kᵗv for every n.
  - Taking the limit gives v = Πv = 0.
- (ii) Follows from KΠ = Π and Π² = Π.
- (iii) Π(I − K + Π) = Π and (I − K + Π)Π = Π. Multiply by Z on the right and
  on the left respectively.
- (iv) Πb = ΠZ(g − h) = Π(g − h) = h − h = 0, by (ii) and (iii). Then
  (I − K + Π)b = g − h gives (I − K)b = g − h. ∎

### Theorem A.3.3 (rate–offset identity)

For every finite kernel K, every present and every n ≥ 0,

> \[
> \ell_n=n\,h+b-K^nb .
> \]
>
> *In words:* the lushness of any horizon is the rate times the horizon, plus
> the offset, minus the part of the offset not yet realized.

**Proof.** By Theorem A.2.1, ℓ_n − nh = Σ_{t<n} Kᵗ(g − h), since Kᵗh = h. By
Lemma A.3.2(iv), g − h = (I − K)b, so the sum telescopes to b − Kⁿb. ∎

**Remarks.**
- The identity needs neither irreducibility nor aperiodicity.
- **Kⁿb is the part of the offset not yet realized at the horizon.**
  - When the closed classes of K are aperiodic, Kⁿ → Π and Kⁿb → Πb = 0, so
    ℓ_n = nh + b + o(1).
  - When some classes are periodic, Kⁿb oscillates with Cesàro mean zero.
- **The irreducible, aperiodic case.** Suppose K is irreducible and aperiodic,
  with ‖δ_xKᵗ − π‖_TV ≤ C rᵗ for some C < ∞ and r < 1 (Levin, Peres and Wilmer
  2009, Theorem 4.9). Then b(x) = Σ_{t≥0} ((Kᵗg)(x) − h), and
  |(Kⁿb)(x)| ≤ 2C‖g‖∞ rⁿ/(1 − r). The offset is realized at a geometric rate.
  The constants depend on K, and need not stay bounded as the system grows.

*Verification:* V1 (periodic, reducible and mixed chains; largest error
2 × 10⁻¹²).

### Corollary A.3.4 (where the rate comes from)

> \[
> h(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\ell_n(x)}{n}=\sum_{C}P_x(\text{absorbed in }C)\,\pi_Cg ,
> \]
>
> where the sum runs over the closed communicating classes C, and π_C is the
> stationary law of C.
>
> *In words:* the long-run rate of a present is the average of the rates of
> the closed regions its development can settle into, weighted by the chance
> of settling in each.

**Proof.** By Theorem A.3.3, ℓ_n/n = h + (b − Kⁿb)/n, and Kⁿb is bounded by
‖b‖∞. The rows of Π are mixtures of the stationary laws of the closed
classes, weighted by the absorption probabilities (Kemeny and Snell 1960). ∎

### Theorem A.3.5 (the offset theorem)

Let every development from the presents x and y be absorbed, with probability
one, in the same closed class C. Then

> \[
> h(x)=h(y)=\pi_Cg ;\qquad \pi\ell_n=n\,h\ \text{ and }\ \pi b=0\ \text{ when }K\text{ is irreducible.}
> \]
>
> *In words:* when every development from both starts settles into the same
> closed class of a finite law, a better start buys a lasting offset and never
> a faster rate, and the offsets average to zero over equilibrium. Starts that
> can settle into different closed classes can differ in rate.

**Proof.**
- The first claim is Corollary A.3.4 with a single class of weight one.
- If K is irreducible, Π = **1**π. Then 0 = Πb = (πb)**1**, and
  πℓ_n = Σ_{t<n} πKᵗg = nπg = nh. ∎

**Scope.**
- **When the theorem fails.** It assumes one finite law, with a single closed
  class that every development reaches. It does not hold, and the comparison
  is not decided in advance, in three situations:
  - the state space grows;
  - the law changes over time;
  - presents can reach different closed classes.
- **What it leaves open.** Since the offsets average to zero, some presents lie
  above equilibrium unless every offset is zero. Whether a specified organized
  present lies above a properly matched reference is a separate question
  (chapter 13).

### Definition A.3.6 (three kinds of comparison)

A comparison between two developments is:

(a) a **preparation comparison** when they follow one law from different
presents;
(b) a **process comparison** when they follow one controlled model under
different policies;
(c) a **mechanism comparison** when they follow different laws.

**What each kind can show.**
- Under Theorem A.3.5, a preparation comparison is decided by offsets alone.
- Process and mechanism comparisons can differ in rate.
- A rate advantage found in a mechanism comparison is a contrast between laws.
  It does not show that one start within a law is better than another
  (chapter 5).
- **One comparison.** Each kind is a way of building the two laws that are
  compared, and records how the contrast was made. The comparison itself is
  the same in every case (Corollary A.3.9).

### Proposition A.3.7 (crossover horizon)

Let developments a and b have, at their presents, Δh = h^b − h^a ≠ 0 and
Δb = b^b − b^a. Let β̄ = ‖b^a‖∞ + ‖b^b‖∞. Then for every n,
|ℓ_n^b − ℓ_n^a − (nΔh + Δb)| ≤ β̄. Suppose Δh and Δb have opposite signs.
- The difference has the sign of Δh for n > T× + β̄/|Δh|.
- It has the sign of Δb for n < T× − β̄/|Δh|.

Here

> \[
> T_\times=-\frac{\Delta b}{\Delta h}.
> \]
>
> *In words:* the development that starts behind but branches faster
> overtakes once the horizon exceeds its starting deficit divided by its rate
> advantage, up to an error no larger than the offsets not yet realized.

**Proof.** Apply Theorem A.3.3 to each development. The unrealized terms
satisfy |(Kⁿb)(x)| ≤ ‖b‖∞. The sign conditions follow by solving the linear
inequality. ∎

The bound is crude. In the examples of Appendix B, the sign changes within one
step of T× once the transients have died away.

### Corollary A.3.8 (rate outweighs offset)

> \[
> \Delta h<0\ \Longrightarrow\ \ell_n^b-\ell_n^a\to-\infty .
> \]
>
> *In words:* a change that permanently lowers the rate eventually outweighs
> any change whose cost is a finite offset.

**Proof.** By Proposition A.3.7, ℓ_n^b − ℓ_n^a ≤ nΔh + Δb + β̄. ∎

"Eventually" can be late. The crossing comes near T×, and section A.4 asks
whether the regime lasts that long.

**The limit comparison.**
- **What it is.** Ranking by rate, and then by offset, is two established
  orderings:
  - the overtaking criterion for developments of this form (von Weizsäcker
    1965);
  - the ordering by gain and then bias in average-reward dynamic programming
    (Veinott 1966).
- **How the manuscript uses it.** Rate-then-offset is the verdict over
  everything that follows. Corollary A.3.9 states it, with comparisons over
  an expected persistence as one of its readings.

### Corollary A.3.9 (the profile and the verdict over everything that follows)

For options a and b from the same present x, each a finite classical model,
call

\[
\Delta_n(a:b)=\ell_n^a(x)-\ell_n^b(x)
\]

the **profile** of the comparison. Write Δh = h^a(x) − h^b(x) and
Δb = b^a(x) − b^b(x). Then

> \[
> \Delta_n(a:b)=n\,\Delta h+\Delta b-\bigl[(K_a^n b^a)(x)-(K_b^n b^b)(x)\bigr],
> \]
>
> and, over everything that follows:
> - if Δh ≠ 0, the profile tends to +∞ or −∞ with the sign of Δh;
> - if Δh = 0, it tends to Δb (in Cesàro mean if either law is periodic);
> - if Δh = Δb = 0, the options tie.
>
> *In words:* the profile is the object of comparison. Its slope is the
> difference in rate, its limiting level the difference in offset, and its
> zero the crossover horizon. The verdict over everything that follows is rate
> first, then offset. When every regime ends physically and the developments
> share their final law, the rates tie, and the verdict is the limit of the
> difference in total lushness, which is finite.

**Proof.** Apply Theorem A.3.3 to each option and subtract. Π is the Cesàro
limit of the powers of K (Definition A.3.1), so Kⁿb tends to Πb = 0
(Lemma A.3.2(iv)) in Cesàro mean, and in the ordinary sense when the kernel is
aperiodic. ∎

**Readings of the profile.**
- **A finite verdict** is its sign at a stated horizon.
- **The crossover horizon** is its zero (Proposition A.3.7).
- **Persistence.** When a regime ends at a random time, its ending and what
  follows belong to the history law, and the profile of that whole law
  converges. Its limit is given in closed form by Lemma A.4.1, when the
  hazards are equal, and by Proposition A.4.8, when they differ. The
  comparison over an expected persistence is therefore a reading of this
  corollary, not a separate choice.
- **Recovery and regeneration** set how long the profile stays negative
  (Proposition A.4.9).
- **Bounded comparison** asks how much the part of the profile not yet seen
  can still change (Proposition A.12.1).
- **Contraction and expansion** are its sign (Definition A.9.2).

**Scope.**
- **What is left to commit to.** Verdicts concern everything that follows from
  the present. That is the definition of the object (chapter 3), not a further
  choice.
- **Bounded agents.** An agent cannot compute the limit. It acts on finite
  horizons, with bounds on the remainder (Proposition A.12.1). That is a
  decision procedure, not the comparison.
- **Ties.** If rate and offset both tie, transient differences that cancel in
  the limit do not decide the comparison.

*Verification:* V25 (ten pairs; the identity holds to 10⁻¹¹ bits, the sign
follows the rate gap, and with equal final rates the profile converges).

### Proposition A.3.10 (rate and profile by regime)

Partition the states into regimes R₁, …, R_m, declared from the physics of the
case before any computation: for example, the states in which a channel is
open and those in which it is closed. For a present x, write
q_t(R) = P_x(X_t ∈ R) and, where q_t(R) > 0,
ḡ_t(R) = E_x[g(X_t) | X_t ∈ R]. Let π_x be the row of Π at x, π_x(R) its mass
on R, and ḡ(R) = Σ_{y∈R} π_x(y)g(y)/π_x(R).

(a) **Grouping.**

> \[
> \ell_n(x)=\sum_{t<n}\sum_R q_t(R)\,\bar g_t(R),\qquad
> h(x)=\sum_R \pi_x(R)\,\bar g(R).
> \]
>
> *In words:* lushness accrues as the branching of each regime, weighted by
> the chance of being in it at each step, and the rate is the same average
> taken over where the development spends its time.

(b) **The split.** For options a and b from the same present, write
Δπ(R) = π^a_x(R) − π^b_x(R) and Δḡ(R) = ḡ^a(R) − ḡ^b(R). Then

> \[
> \Delta h=\underbrace{\sum_R\Delta\pi(R)\,\bar g^{\,b}(R)}_{\text{occupancy}}
> +\underbrace{\sum_R\pi^b_x(R)\,\Delta\bar g(R)}_{\text{conditional branching}}
> +\underbrace{\sum_R\Delta\pi(R)\,\Delta\bar g(R)}_{\text{interaction}},
> \]
>
> and the same split holds at every step of the profile, with q_t in place of
> π_x.
>
> *In words:* a difference in rate splits exactly into three parts: what comes
> from spending more or less time in each regime, priced at the reference
> branching; what comes from branching more or less within each regime; and
> what the two add only together. With two regimes, the occupancy term is the
> change in the time spent in one regime, times the excess branching of that
> regime over the other.

**Proof.**
- (a) By Theorem A.2.1, the t-th term is (Kᵗg)(x) = Σ_y P_x(X_t = y)g(y);
  group the states by regime. The rate is h(x) = Σ_y π_x(y)g(y) (Definition
  A.3.1); group the same way.
- (b) Write π^a = π^b + Δπ and ḡ^a = ḡ^b + Δḡ in each regime and expand
  π^a(R)ḡ^a(R) − π^b(R)ḡ^b(R). With two regimes R₁ and R₂,
  Σ_R Δπ(R) = 0, so the occupancy term is Δπ(R₁)·[ḡ^b(R₁) − ḡ^b(R₂)]. ∎

**Remarks.**
- **Nothing is omitted.** The branching of a state, g(y), includes every
  transition out of it, those that change regime among them.
- **The partition decides the split.** The three terms depend on the declared
  partition, not on the physics alone. With a single regime, every difference
  falls in the conditional-branching term, since the occupancy of the only
  regime is one under both options. With one regime per state, under a single
  kernel, every difference in the profile falls in the occupancy term, since
  each state branches alike under both options. This is why the partition is
  declared from the physics of the case before any computation.
- **Occupancy and access.** The occupancy term reads as access only where the
  partition is physically one of access: the states in which a channel is
  open and those in which it is closed. Access gained within a regime, such as
  a further pathway opened while the apparatus is working, appears in the
  conditional-branching term. The split locates a difference; it does not
  measure access, which acts through the law (chapter 3).
- **The persistence relation.** In the models, a structure's contribution is
  approximated by its uptime times its excess branching (chapter 15). That
  is the occupancy term with two regimes. It is exact when branching within
  each regime does not change, and the other two terms are its exact error.
- **Accounting, not causation.** The split attributes a difference to the
  regimes declared. Whether a given structure causes a change in occupancy
  needs a mechanism comparison (Definition A.3.6(c)).
- **The reference matters.** The occupancy and conditional-branching terms
  are priced at option b. Exchanging the roles turns the occupancy term into
  −(occupancy + interaction) and the conditional-branching term into
  −(conditional branching + interaction), and leaves the interaction term
  unchanged, so the total reverses. A report names the reference, as with the
  factorial identity (Proposition A.8.3).
- **Empty regimes.** Where a regime has no mass under one option, its average
  branching under that option is undefined. Taking it equal to the other
  option's preserves the identity, because that average is multiplied by
  zero. But the rule is a convention, and it sets the allocation: a regime
  reachable under only one option then has its whole contribution counted as
  occupancy. A report names the convention and marks the terms that depend on
  it.
- **Subjects.** The split is stated for the whole field, whose resolved
  history is Markov in the finite classical model. A subject's own history is
  generally not Markov (section A.7). A split of a subject's difference groups
  the conditional increments of Theorem A.7.3, or their bounds, never the step
  entropies of full states.

*Verification:* V40 (thirty pairs of random seven-state kernels, a third of
them reducible; the split holds to 2 × 10⁻¹², the two-regime form of the
occupancy term to the same precision, and the grouping of the profile to
4 × 10⁻¹⁵ bits); V43 (exchanging the options leaves the interaction term
unchanged to machine precision while the total reverses; on one profile step
under one kernel, a single regime puts the whole difference in the
conditional-branching term and one regime per state puts it in the occupancy
term).

### Definition A.3.11 (leverage of a choice)

For options a and b from the same present, with profile Δ_n(a : b)
(Corollary A.3.9), the choice between them has:
- **bounded leverage** if |Δ_n(a : b)| stays below a fixed bound at every
  horizon;
- **rate leverage** if Δ_n(a : b) = nΔh + o(n), with Δh ≠ 0;
- **superlinear leverage** if |Δ_n(a : b)|/n is unbounded.

A frame's leverage at a present is the strongest of these among the pairs of
options physically available to it.

**Remarks.**
- **Log lushness.** Every class concerns ℓ. Lushness L = 2^ℓ already grows
  exponentially when ℓ grows at a constant rate.
- **The classes are not exhaustive.** A difference can be unbounded and still
  grow more slowly than the horizon. A structure that adds one fair choice at
  steps 1, 2, 4, 8, … differs from one that does not by ⌊log₂ n⌋ + 1 bits at
  horizon n, and the branching of an uncertain ending grows like log₂ m
  (Proposition A.4.8). A difference can also oscillate without settling.
- **Bounded is not transient.** Within one closed class every difference is
  bounded (Theorem A.3.5), yet the offset it leaves is permanent; only its
  further accumulation stops. A bounded difference can be large.
- **Irreversibility is a separate property.** A loss that is never
  regenerated need not change the rate. When both options end in the same
  residual law, its cost is finite (Proposition A.4.9 with γ = 0).
  Irreversibility concerns whether a difference can be undone; leverage
  concerns how it grows.
- **Consequences, not agency.** The classes describe what a choice does to
  the profile under the physical conditions of the case. They do not define
  agency, or any transition in it.
  - A structure that recursively enables further structure can grow,
    saturate at physical capacity and sustain a constant excess rate
    thereafter: rate leverage only. It can also vanish and leave a bounded
    difference.
  - Conversely, variation whose repertoire keeps growing can produce
    superlinear growth with nothing that acts.

  Which capacity the classes are signatures of is open (section A.15).

*Verification:* V44.

### Corollary A.3.12 (repertoire and growth)

For any law on resolved histories from a present, let M_t be the largest
number of values the resolved state at step t + 1 takes with positive
probability, given any history to step t. Then

> \[
> \ell_n\le\sum_{t<n}\log_2M_t ,\qquad
> M_t\le M\ \text{for every }t\ \Longrightarrow\ \ell_n\le n\log_2M\ \text{ and }\ \lvert\Delta_n(a:b)\rvert\le n\log_2M .
> \]
>
> Superlinear growth of ℓ_n, and superlinear leverage, therefore require a
> one-step repertoire that is unbounded along the development.
>
> *In words:* while the number of distinct next states stays below a fixed
> bound, log lushness grows at most in proportion to the horizon, and so does
> any difference between two options. Faster growth requires a repertoire that
> grows without bound.

**Proof.** By the chain rule, ℓ_n = Σ_{t<n} H(X_{t+1} | X₀, …, X_t). Each term
is an average of entropies of laws on at most M_t values, so it is at most
log₂ M_t (Cover and Thomas 2006, Theorem 2.6.4). If M_t ≤ M for every t, the
lushness of each option lies between 0 and n log₂ M, and so does the absolute
value of their difference. If ℓ_n/n is unbounded, the M_t therefore cannot all
lie below one bound. ∎

**Scope.**
- **The converse fails.** A repertoire can grow while the law concentrates on
  a few of its states, so a growing repertoire need not give superlinear
  growth. Superlinear growth does not establish agency (Definition A.3.11,
  remarks).
- **Finite classical models.** For a kernel on a finite S, M_t ≤ |S|, so
  every finite classical model has at most rate leverage (Proposition A.2.4).
  Superlinear leverage needs a growing state space or a law that changes, the
  cases in which Theorem A.3.5 does not apply.
- **Physical bounds.** Whether physics bounds the repertoire of every
  admissible resolution is open (section A.15).

*Verification:* V44 (200 random laws with step-dependent repertoires, none
above the bound; a repertoire of t + 1 at step t gives ℓ_n = log₂ n!, with
ℓ_n/n reaching 11.8 bits at n = 10⁴; a repertoire growing to a cap of 64,
against a fixed repertoire of 2, gives a difference per step that tends to
5 bits, rate leverage only; one fair choice at steps 1, 2, 4, 8, … gives
⌊log₂ n⌋ + 1 bits; two presents in one closed class keep a difference of 0.499
bits from n = 100 to n = 3,000).

## A.4 Random horizons and temporary rates

Rates are long-run quantities, and no physical regime lasts forever. This
section asks what the measure ranks when the laws that distinguish two options
persist for a finite random time, after which both relax into a common law.

The answer is classical in dynamic programming: it is the Laurent expansion of
discounted value (Blackwell 1962; Veinott 1969; Puterman 1994, chapter 8).
What is new here is its reading for lushness, and two consequences:
- the persistence at which rate and offset trade;
- the floor on an acceptable rate gain.

**The persistence setting.**
- Two options start from their presents and follow kernels K_a and K_b,
  possibly on different state spaces.
- At every step, before branching, the regime that distinguishes the options
  ends with probability λ, independently of the state.
- The development then enters a common law at a fixed entry state. Any entry
  law that does not depend on the option gives the same result.
- N is the number of steps the regime completes, so P(N = n) = λ(1 − λ)ⁿ and
  m = E[N] = (1 − λ)/λ.

### Lemma A.4.1 (what the persistence setting compares)

In the persistence setting, the difference between the two options' total
lushness over any overall horizon T equals
E[ℓ^b_{N∧T}] − E[ℓ^a_{N∧T}]. As T grows, this tends to
ΔE[ℓ_N] = E[ℓ^b_N] − E[ℓ^a_N].

**Proof.** The chain rule splits the history into the regime, with its ending
time, and what follows.
- **After the regime.** Given the ending time, what follows starts in the
  common law at an entry state that does not depend on the option. Its entropy
  depends only on the time remaining, and the ending time has the same law for
  both options.
- **During the regime.** By the grouping rule, each step from a state x has
  entropy h₂(λ) + (1 − λ)g(x).
  - The h₂(λ) terms sum to a quantity that depends only on the law of N and
    on T.
  - The expected sum of the remaining terms is
    Σ_{t<T} (1 − λ)^{t+1} (Kᵗg)(x). This equals
    Σ_{t<T} P(N > t)(Kᵗg)(x) = E[ℓ_{N∧T}(x)]. ∎

*Verification:* V4 (an explicit chain with an end state; difference
4 × 10⁻¹² bits).

### Theorem A.4.2 (random-horizon identity)

For a finite kernel, a present x, and any random horizon N independent of the
development with E[N] < ∞,

> \[
> \mathbb E[\ell_N(x)]=h(x)\,\mathbb E[N]+b(x)-\mathbb E[(K^Nb)(x)],\qquad
> \bigl\lvert\mathbb E[(K^Nb)(x)]\bigr\rvert\le\lVert b\rVert_\infty .
> \]
>
> *In words:* a regime that lasts a random time is worth its rate times its
> expected duration, plus its offset, less the expected part of the offset
> still unrealized when it ends; and that last part is bounded however long
> the regime lasts.

**Proof.** Take the expectation of Theorem A.3.3 over N. The expectation
exists because ℓ_n ≤ n log₂|S|. The bound holds because Kⁿ is stochastic. ∎

*Verification:* V2 (an arbitrary horizon law on 0–119 steps; error
2 × 10⁻¹³).

### Theorem A.4.3 (constant hazard)

Under a constant hazard with m = E[N] > 0,

> \[
> \mathbb E[\ell_N]=h\,m+b-Z\,(mI+Z)^{-1}b
> =h\,m+b-\frac{Zb}{m}+O(m^{-2}).
> \]
>
> *In words:* when the regime ends at a constant hazard, its lushness is the
> rate times the expected persistence, plus the offset, less the share of the
> offset that is not realized before the end; that share shrinks in inverse
> proportion to the persistence.

**Proof.**
1. Let α = 1 − λ and A = K − Π, so that ΠA = AΠ = 0. Then
   E[Kᴺb] = λ Σ_n αⁿKⁿb = λRb, where R = (I − αK)⁻¹.
2. Write I − αK = (1 − α)Π + (I − Π − αA). Using ΠA = AΠ = 0, one checks
   directly that R = Π/λ + (I − αA)⁻¹(I − Π).
3. Since Πb = 0, this gives Rb = (I − αA)⁻¹b.
4. Since I − A = I − K + Π = Z⁻¹, we have I − αA = λI + αZ⁻¹. Hence
   λRb = λZ(λZ + αI)⁻¹b = Z(mI + Z)⁻¹b, with m = α/λ.
5. **Invertibility.** The eigenvalues of Z⁻¹ = I − K + Π are 1, on the range
   of Π, and 1 − κ for the eigenvalues κ ≠ 1 of K, which satisfy |κ| ≤ 1. All
   of them have positive real part, and so do the eigenvalues of Z. So mI + Z
   and λI + αZ⁻¹ are invertible for every m ≥ 0 and λ > 0.
6. **The expansion.** For m larger than the spectral radius of Z,
   Z(mI + Z)⁻¹ = Σ_{k≥1} (−1)^{k+1} Zᵏ/mᵏ. ∎

**Source.** This is the Laurent expansion of discounted value (Blackwell
1962; Veinott 1969; Puterman 1994, chapter 8), summed into closed form.

**Reading Zb.** For an aperiodic K, Zb = Σ_{t≥0} Kᵗb is the unrealized offset
summed over time. When the unrealized offset falls steadily to zero, Zb equals
the offset times the mean time at which it is realized.
- If the unrealized offset decays geometrically with mean realization time τ,
  then Z(mI + Z)⁻¹b = b·τ/(m + τ).
- The share of the offset realized before the regime ends is then m/(m + τ).

*Verification:* V3 (residual of order λ², periodic chains included); V9
(closed form, largest error 1.3 × 10⁻⁹ bits on totals up to 10⁴ bits).

### Corollary A.4.4 (when the faster option wins)

Let two options in the persistence setting have Δh ≠ 0, and Δb of the
opposite sign, so that T× > 0. Let τ_b = Δ(Zb)/Δb. Then

> \[
> \Delta\mathbb E[\ell_N]=\Delta h\,m+\Delta b-\frac{\Delta(Zb)}{m}+O(m^{-2}),
> \qquad m^*=T_\times-\tau_b+O(1/T_\times),
> \]
>
> where m* is the expected persistence at which the options tie. Suppose
> Δ[Z(mI + Z)⁻¹b] = Δb·τ/(m + τ) for every m. This holds when the unrealized
> offset difference decays geometrically with mean realization time τ. Then
> the tie is exactly at m* = T× − τ.
>
> *In words:* the faster option wins when its regime is expected to last
> longer than the crossover horizon less the mean time the offset difference
> takes to be realized.

**Proof.**
- Apply Theorem A.4.3 to each option and subtract.
- **The general case.** Set the difference to zero, and write
  Δ(Zb) = τ_bΔb = −τ_bΔh T×. This gives m = T× − τ_b T×/m + O(1/m). Near
  m = T×, that is m = T× − τ_b + O(1/T×).
- **The geometric case.** The condition becomes Δh·m + Δb·m/(m + τ) = 0,
  that is, m + τ = T×. ∎

The rate-then-offset rule and the persistence comparison agree exactly when
the expected persistence exceeds m*. To first order, that is when it exceeds
T×.

**Example.**
- **Destroying a transient structure.** One option keeps a rich transient
  structure before settling into a residual process at h₂(0.1) = 0.469 bits per
  step. The structure has sixteen equally likely states and lasts a mean of
  1,000 steps, and its offset over the residual is 3,538.4 bits. The
  alternative destroys the structure at once, for a residual that branches 0.1
  bits per step faster.
  - T× = 35,384 steps and τ_b = 1,000 steps.
  - The options tie at an expected persistence of 34,384 steps, which agrees
    with exact computation to 10⁻⁶ steps.
- **Losing a generator that is later rebuilt.** When the generator is rebuilt
  after a mean of 10,000 steps, τ_b = 10,000. The tie then falls at 62,046
  steps against a T× of 72,046.

*Verification:* V5 (nine cases; the largest error of the corrected tie is 0.56
steps).

### Proposition A.4.5 (window decomposition)

Let τ be a stopping time of the development with E_x[τ] < ∞. Then

> \[
> b(x)=\mathbb E_x\Bigl[\sum_{t<\tau}\bigl(g(X_t)-h(X_t)\bigr)\Bigr]+\mathbb E_x\bigl[b(X_\tau)\bigr].
> \]
>
> *In words:* the offset of a present is the excess branching, above the
> rate, that it accumulates before any chosen moment, plus the offset of
> wherever it stands at that moment.

**Proof.**
1. Let M_n = b(X_n) + Σ_{t<n} (g − h)(X_t).
2. By Lemma A.3.2(iv),
   E[M_{n+1} − M_n | X₀, …, X_n] = (Kb)(X_n) − b(X_n) + g(X_n) − h(X_n) = 0.
   So M is a martingale, and its increments are bounded by
   2‖b‖∞ + ‖g‖∞ + ‖h‖∞.
3. By the optional stopping theorem for integrable stopping times with bounded
   increments (Williams 1991, chapter 10), E_x[M_τ] = M₀ = b(x). ∎

**The case of a common destination.** Let τ be the entry time into a closed
class F with rate h_F, and suppose every state before τ reaches F with
probability one. Then h(X_t) = h_F before τ, and the excess is the branching
above h_F.

This is the formal form of the persistence relation of chapter 15: what a
structure contributes is its excess branching over its expected functional
life.

*Verification:* V6 (the decomposition holds to 2 × 10⁻¹² bits).

### Corollary A.4.6 (the robustness floor)

Let two options reach the same closed class F, with rate h_F, at entry states
with equal offsets.
- **Option a** accumulates an excess branching of c before it enters F.
- **Option b** enters at once a regime whose kernel has step entropy h_F + ε at
  every state. The regime ends at hazard μ per step, and the development then
  enters F.

Then

> \[
> b^b-b^a=\frac{\varepsilon}{\mu}-c+\beta,\qquad
> \beta=\frac{h_2(\mu)}{\mu}-(h_F+\varepsilon),
> \]
>
> so option b is preferred if and only if
>
> \[
> \varepsilon>\frac{\mu\,(c+h_F)-h_2(\mu)}{1-\mu}.
> \]
>
> *In words:* a rate gain is worth taking only if, spread over its expected
> life, it repays the finite branching given up. The ending of the gain is
> itself an uncertain event, and the measure counts the branching of that
> event too.

**Proof.**
1. Apply Proposition A.4.5 to each option, with τ the entry time into F. The
   exit terms are equal.
2. For option a, the excess is c by definition.
3. For option b, each regime step has step entropy h₂(μ) + (1 − μ)(h_F + ε),
   by the grouping rule. That is an excess over h_F of
   h₂(μ) + ε − μ(h_F + ε).
4. The regime lasts an expected 1/μ steps. Multiply, and subtract. ∎

**Remarks.**
- **The explicit threshold.** The threshold rearranges b^b − b^a > 0, and β is
  the decomposition that explains it. To first order in μ, the threshold is
  μ(c + h_F) − h₂(μ).
- **When the threshold is negative.** It is negative when
  c < h₂(μ)/μ − h_F, about log₂(e/μ) − h_F bits. For so small a cost, the
  branching of the uncertain ending alone repays it. The measure counts the
  timing of an ending as it counts any other event.
- **β is small.** Since h₂(μ)/μ = log₂(e/μ) + O(μ log(1/μ)), β is about
  log₂(e/μ) bits less one step of branching. It grows only with the logarithm
  of the gain's expected life, while the gain's contribution grows in
  proportion to that life.
- **The floor rises with the hazard.** A gain that will not last must be large
  to be worth its cost. Strict rate priority is the limit μ → 0, in which
  every gain is assumed to last forever.
- **Uncertainty about μ.** Expected lushness averages the offset difference
  over the credences, so the floor uses the expected life, the average of 1/μ,
  and that average is dominated by the most optimistic hazard. A margin set by
  the actor's model error (chapter 25) is a policy for acting under such
  uncertainty. It is not a theorem of the measure, and it is not stated here.

**Example.** The structure of the previous example (c = 3,538.4 bits) set
against a gain on h_F = 0.469.

| μ | Threshold (bits per step) |
|---|---|
| 10⁻³ | 3.53 |
| 10⁻⁴ | 0.352 |
| 10⁻⁵ | 0.0352 |
| 10⁻⁶ | 0.00352 |

- A gain of 0.1 bits per step loses by 2,524 bits at μ = 10⁻⁴, and wins by
  6,479 bits at μ = 10⁻⁵.
- A gain of 0.01 bits per step loses at μ = 10⁻⁵ and wins at μ = 10⁻⁶.

Each verdict agrees with the threshold.

The corollary's β, 14.16 and 17.48 bits, agrees with the exact computation to
10⁻⁶ bits.

*Verification:* V6.

### Corollary A.4.7 (persistence and accrual)

Fix a present x and the kernel K, and write c_t(x) = (Kᵗg)(x) for the
branching expected at step t.

(a) **Random horizon.** For any random horizon N independent of the
development, with E[N] < ∞,

> \[
> \mathbb E[\ell_N(x)]=\sum_{t\ge 0}\Pr(N>t)\,c_t(x).
> \]
>
> If N₂ stochastically dominates N₁, so that Pr(N₂ > t) ≥ Pr(N₁ > t) for
> every t, then E[ℓ_{N₂}(x)] ≥ E[ℓ_{N₁}(x)]. The inequality is strict if
> Pr(N₂ > t) > Pr(N₁ > t) at some t with c_t(x) > 0.

(b) **State-dependent ending.** Let the regime end before each step with
probability κ(y), where y is the current state, and let A_κ(x) be the
branching accrued while it runs:

> \[
> A_\kappa(x)=\sum_{t\ge 0}\delta_x\,(S_\kappa K)^t S_\kappa\, g,\qquad S_\kappa=\operatorname{diag}(1-\kappa).
> \]
>
> If κ′ ≤ κ at every state, then A_{κ′}(x) ≥ A_κ(x).
>
> *In words:* holding the law fixed, a regime that lasts longer accrues no
> less of its lushness. Each step of added persistence adds exactly the
> branching expected at that step, and that is never negative.

**Proof.**
- **(a)** Averaging Theorem A.2.1 over the law of N gives
  Σ_n Pr(N = n) Σ_{t<n} c_t = Σ_t Pr(N > t) c_t. Every c_t is non-negative,
  since g ≥ 0 and Kᵗ has non-negative entries. A larger Pr(N > t) at every t
  therefore gives a sum no smaller.
- **(b)** The row vector δ_x(S_κK)ᵗS_κ gives the probability of reaching each
  state at step t with the regime still running and continuing through that
  step. Its entries are sums of products of entries of S_κ and K. All of
  these entries are non-negative, and each product is non-decreasing in every
  entry of S_κ. Lowering κ raises every entry of S_κ, and g ≥ 0. ∎

Under a constant hazard, (b) reduces to (a), with Pr(N > t) = (1 − λ)^{t+1}.
The identity in (a) is the one Lemma A.4.1 uses.

**Beyond Markov models.** For any law on finite histories, the chain rule
writes ℓ_n as the sum over t < n of H(X_{t+1} | X₀, …, X_t), and these terms
are non-negative. Part (a) holds unchanged.

**Scope.**
- **The law is held fixed.** Keeping a structure going longer usually changes
  the law: it uses resources, and it can suppress other channels. The
  comparison is then between laws.
- **The accrual is the regime's own.** E[ℓ_N] and A_κ count the regime's
  branching. They are not the lushness of the whole field, which goes on after
  the regime ends. Proposition A.4.8 includes what follows.

*Verification:* V18 (400 random kernels; the identity holds to 10⁻¹³ bits,
and in no case did longer persistence or a lower hazard reduce the accrual).

### Proposition A.4.8 (what persistence is worth to the whole field)

In the persistence setting, let:
- the regime's kernel K have rate h(x) and offset b(x) at the present x;
- the common law that follows the regime have rate h′ and offset b′ at its
  entry state x′;
- ℓ_T(x) be the lushness of the whole development, regime and common law
  together, over T steps.

Then, as T → ∞ (in Cesàro mean if the common law is periodic),

> \[
> \ell_T(x)-h'T\ \longrightarrow\ m\,\bigl(h(x)-h'\bigr)+b(x)-\bigl[Z(mI+Z)^{-1}b\bigr](x)+H(N)-h'+b',
> \]
> \[
> H(N)=\frac{h_2(\lambda)}{\lambda}=(m+1)\log_2(m+1)-m\log_2 m .
> \]
>
> The worth of one more unit of expected persistence is therefore
>
> \[
> \frac{d}{dm}\Bigl[\lim_{T\to\infty}\bigl(\ell_T(x)-h'T\bigr)\Bigr]
> =h(x)-h'+\log_2\!\Bigl(1+\frac1m\Bigr)+\bigl[Z(mI+Z)^{-2}b\bigr](x)
> =h(x)-h'+O(1/m).
> \]
>
> *In words:* over a long horizon, each step a regime is expected to last is
> worth its rate less the rate of whatever follows it. The other terms are
> the share of the regime's offset that it realizes and the branching of its
> uncertain ending. Both matter less the longer the regime lasts. Persistence
> of a regime that branches less than its successor lowers the lushness of
> the whole.

**Proof.**
1. By the chain rule, split the whole history into the regime with its
   ending time, and the common law that follows.
2. **The regime.** A step taken from state y while the regime runs has entropy
   h₂(λ) + (1 − λ)g(y), by the grouping rule. The regime is still running at
   step t with probability (1 − λ)ᵗ.
   - The h₂(λ) terms sum to h₂(λ)Σ_t(1 − λ)ᵗ = h₂(λ)/λ. This is the entropy
     of the geometric ending time N.
   - The branching terms sum to E[ℓ_N(x)] (Lemma A.4.1), which Theorem A.4.3
     evaluates.
3. **The common law.** It starts at step N + 1 from x′ and runs for T − N − 1
   steps. By Theorem A.3.3 its expected lushness is
   E[(T − N − 1)h′ + b′ − (K′^{T−N−1}b′)(x′)], where K′ is its kernel. This
   tends to h′T − (m + 1)h′ + b′, since K′ⁿb′ tends to Π′b′ = 0 (in Cesàro
   mean if K′ is periodic).
4. The contributions of regimes still running at T vanish geometrically as T
   grows. Collecting the terms gives the limit. Differentiating in m, with
   d(mI + Z)⁻¹/dm = −(mI + Z)⁻², gives the worth. The last term is O(m⁻²),
   and log₂(1 + 1/m) ≤ log₂(e)/m. ∎

**Remarks.**
- **The worth of persistence is a difference of rates.** In the measure,
  persistence has no value of its own. It is worth what the regime adds over
  its successor, step for step. This is the exact sense in which persistence
  is instrumental (chapters 6 and 9).
- **Relation to the robustness floor.** Corollary A.4.6 prices a gain that
  ends at a known hazard against an excess taken at once. Proposition A.4.8
  gives the same accounting as a function of the expected persistence.
- **The branching of the ending.** H(N) grows only with the logarithm of m,
  while the rate term grows in proportion to m. A regime that does not branch
  at all still adds the branching of its uncertain ending, which over a
  horizon T is at most log₂(T + 1). The measure counts when something ends as
  it counts any other event.

*Verification:* V19 (twelve cases; the limit holds to 1.1 × 10⁻⁹ bits and the
worth to 10⁻¹⁰).

### Proposition A.4.9 (the cost of an early end)

**The setting.**
- The field alternates between two blocks of states, a **regime** R and a
  **successor** S.
- Before each step in R, the regime ends with probability λ and the
  development enters S. Before each step in S, the regime is regenerated with
  probability γ and the development enters R. The entry states are fixed.
- Otherwise the development branches within its block. Every state of R has
  the same step entropy g_R within R, and every state of S the same step
  entropy g_S within S.

Let s_R = h₂(λ) + (1 − λ)g_R and s_S = h₂(γ) + (1 − γ)g_S be the step
entropies of the two blocks, switching included. An **early end** moves the
development from R to S now. For 0 < λ + γ ≤ 2, its cost at long horizons is

> \[
> C=\lim_{T\to\infty}\bigl[\ell_T(x_R)-\ell_T(x_S)\bigr]=\frac{s_R-s_S}{\lambda+\gamma},
> \]
>
> for any x_R in R and x_S in S, in Cesàro mean when λ + γ = 2.
>
> *In words:* ending a regime early costs the difference between the breadth
> it adds each step and the breadth its successor adds, divided by the sum of
> the hazard of ending and the hazard of regeneration. Regeneration shrinks
> the difference, whichever its sign. When the regime can never be
> regenerated, γ = 0, and the cost is (s_R − s_S)/λ: everything the regime
> would have added, beyond its successor, over its expected remaining life. If
> the successor branches more, s_R < s_S, the cost is negative: the early end
> widens the future.

**Proof.**
1. Every state of a block has the same step entropy, switching included, and
   the same probability of leaving the block. So
   ℓ_T(y) = Σ_{t<T} E_y[s(B_t)], where B_t is the block occupied at step t.
   B is a two-state Markov chain that leaves R with probability λ and leaves S
   with probability γ.
2. For such a chain, Pr_R(B_t = R) − Pr_S(B_t = R) = (1 − λ − γ)ᵗ. So the
   difference at step t is (s_R − s_S)(1 − λ − γ)ᵗ.
3. Summing over t < T gives (s_R − s_S)[1 − (1 − λ − γ)^T]/(λ + γ). This
   converges when λ + γ < 2, and converges in Cesàro mean when λ + γ = 2. ∎

**Remarks.**
- **Orientation and irreversibility.** γ measures how readily the dynamics
  restore what an early end removes.
  - Where γ = 0 at the declared resolution, the difference is permanent.
  - If s_R > s_S, removing regeneration makes the cost of an early end as
    large as it can be for the given λ. If s_R < s_S, it makes the gain from
    an early end as large as it can be.
  - Precautionary protection (Proposition A.9.5) and option value
    (Proposition A.9.6) take their force from the first case.
  - The formal core takes γ as given. Its relation to the directional
    asymmetry of the dynamics is argued in chapter 28, not proved here.
- **When neither hazard is positive.** If λ = γ = 0, an early end changes the
  rate, and its cost grows in proportion to the horizon (Corollary A.3.8).
- **Relation to Corollary A.4.4.** The generator rebuilt after a mean of
  10,000 steps, in the example there, is a case of regeneration, and its tie
  moves accordingly.
- **The assumptions.** Equal step entropies within each block make the block
  process exact. Without them, the same form holds with block averages to the
  extent that each block mixes internally faster than the blocks switch. That
  approximation is not bounded here.

**Example.** A regime branches 1.486 bits per step within itself and ends at
hazard λ = 0.01. Its successor branches 0.848 bits per step.
- If the regime can never be regenerated, an early end costs 70.38 bits.
- If it is regenerated at hazard γ = 0.002, after a mean of 500 steps, the
  cost falls to 57.06 bits.

*Verification:* V20 (six cases, including γ = 0 and λ + γ = 2; largest error
2 × 10⁻¹⁰ bits).

## A.5 Gambles and finite stakes

Section A.4 priced a gain by how long it lasts. This section prices it by how
likely it is, and asks what bounds a finite physical world places on the
stakes.

### Proposition A.5.1 (gambles)

Let I be the outcome of a chance event with law p = (p₁, …, p_k), and X the
history that follows, with ℓ_i = H(X | I = i). Then

> \[
> H(X)=H(p)+\sum_ip_i\ell_i-H(I\mid X),\qquad
> \sum_ip_i\ell_i\ \le\ H(X)\ \le\ H(p)+\sum_ip_i\ell_i .
> \]
>
> *In words:* the lushness of a gamble is the probability-weighted lushness
> of its outcomes plus a bonus no larger than the uncertainty of the gamble
> itself. The bonus is whole when the history records the outcome, and absent
> when the history carries no trace of it.

**Proof.** H(X, I) = H(I) + H(X | I) = H(X) + H(I | X), and
0 ≤ H(I | X) ≤ H(I).
- The upper bound holds with equality exactly when H(I | X) = 0, that is,
  when the history determines the outcome.
- The lower bound holds with equality exactly when H(I | X) = H(I), that is,
  when X is independent of I.

(Cover and Thomas 2006, chapter 2.) ∎

*Verification:* V7.

### Corollary A.5.2 (risk neutrality and recorded mixtures)

In a finite classical model, the outcome of a step is part of the history.

(a) **Gambles.** Suppose a gamble's outcome i leads to a development with
lushness ℓ_i and rate h_i. Then

> \[
> \ell(\text{gamble})=H(p)+\sum_ip_i\ell_i,\qquad h(\text{gamble})=\sum_ip_ih_i .
> \]

(b) **Recorded mixtures of policies.** When the chance device is the actor's
own, let a recorded random choice select policy A with probability q and
policy B otherwise, with lushness ℓ_A and ℓ_B over the horizon. Then

> \[
> \ell_{\text{mix}}(q)=h_2(q)+q\,\ell_A+(1-q)\,\ell_B,\qquad
> \max_q\ell_{\text{mix}}=\log_2\bigl(2^{\ell_A}+2^{\ell_B}\bigr),
> \]
>
> attained at q* = L_A/(L_A + L_B).
>
> *In words:* the measure values a gamble at the probability-weighted value of
> its outcomes, plus a bonus no larger than the uncertainty of the gamble; in
> the long run, it values it at exactly the probability-weighted rate. When
> the gamble is the actor's own randomization between two policies, the bonus
> is at most one bit when they are close, and almost nothing when one is far
> better, so a mixture cannot rescue a much worse policy.

**Proof.**
- **(a)** The first identity is the recurrence of Theorem A.2.1 at the
  gamble's step. The second is Corollary A.3.4.
- **(b)** The first identity is (a) with two outcomes. ℓ_mix is concave in q.
  Its derivative, log₂((1 − q)/q) + ℓ_A − ℓ_B, vanishes at q*, and
  substituting gives log₂(L_A + L_B). ∎

Both parts are one fact, the chain rule H(I, X) = H(I) + H(X | I) for a
recorded choice I, read twice.

**Remarks.**
- **Neutral in rate.** The measure is risk-neutral in rate, and risk-seeking by
  at most H(p) ≤ log₂ k bits in total, whatever the horizon. It gives no weight
  to the spread of outcomes as such.
- **Ruin counts by its size.** An irreversible loss is large because it is
  permanent, so its size grows with the horizon (Corollary A.3.8). Its
  probability is not inflated.
- **Mixtures.** The gain of the best mixture over the better fixed policy is
  log₂(1 + 2^{−|ℓ_A − ℓ_B|}). An equal mixture loses to the better policy
  exactly when |ℓ_A − ℓ_B| > 2. Part (b) requires the choice to be recorded.
- **Prudence.** Prudence beyond expectation, if it is warranted, must come
  from what the actor does not know (chapter 25). The measure does not supply
  it.

*Verification:* V16 (part b).

### Proposition A.5.3 (finite stakes; conditional)

**Premises.**
- **(F1) Finite repertoire.** At the declared grain, every state can reach at
  most M states in one step. By Proposition A.2.4, no rate exceeds log₂ M.
- **(F2) Residual floor.** Ruin leaves a residual process whose rate is
  r_th ≥ 0.
- **(F3) Common relaxation.** The options' regimes are in the persistence
  setting, with expected persistence m.

**Setting.**
- The status quo runs at rate h₀.
- A sure alternative runs at h₀ + Δh, with Δh ≥ 0.
- A gamble records its outcome. Then, with probability p, it runs at h₀ + u,
  where u ≤ u_max = log₂ M − h₀; otherwise it falls to the floor r_th.
- The downside is d = h₀ − r_th.

**Statement.**

> \[
> \Delta\mathbb E[\ell_N]_{\text{gamble}-\text{sure}}=m\bigl[p\,u-(1-p)\,d-\Delta h\bigr]+B,\qquad \lvert B\rvert\le\bar B ,
> \]
>
> where B̄ depends on the options' offsets and not on m. Hence, if
>
> \[
> p<\frac{\Delta h+d}{u_{\max}+d},
> \]
>
> the gamble loses to the sure alternative, for every available upside, once
> m > B̄/[Δh + (1 − p)d − p·u_max].
>
> *In words:* when a gamble's possible gain is capped and its possible loss is
> real, a small enough chance of success loses, both to doing nothing and to
> any sure gain, once the regimes last long enough.

**Proof.**
1. The gamble's rate is p(h₀ + u) + (1 − p)r_th, by Corollary A.5.2.
2. By Theorem A.4.2, the difference in E[ℓ_N] is m times the difference in
   rates, plus Δb − ΔE[Kᴺb]. The absolute value of that remainder is at most
   B̄ = 2(‖b_gamble‖∞ + ‖b_sure‖∞).
3. Since u ≤ u_max, the coefficient of m is at most
   p·u_max − (1 − p)d − Δh. It is negative exactly under the stated condition
   on p. ∎

**Special cases.**
- **No sure gain (Δh = 0).** A long shot loses to the status quo whenever
  p < d/(d + u_max).
- **No downside (d = 0).** The condition reduces to p·u_max < Δh. This is the
  bound on fanaticism for finite systems.

**Scope.**
- **The premises are hypotheses.** They are physical hypotheses at the declared
  grain, and the result holds only given them.
  - M grows as the grain is refined. So u_max is a property of the declared
    description, not of the world alone.
  - (F1) is a premise, not a consequence of known bounds. Physical
    information bounds are a proposed justification still to be developed.
    The speed of evolution between orthogonal states is bounded by the mean
    energy above the ground state (Margolus and Levitin 1998), and the
    information a bounded region of bounded energy can hold is bounded
    (Bekenstein 1981). Turning them into a bound on the resolved histories
    counted here needs a further argument, connecting those histories to
    physical records and their resources (section A.15).
  - No universal bound on branching is claimed. Holographic bounds concern the
    information associated with regions, not rates of branching.
- **The result is first order in m.** B̄ includes the gamble's own H(p) and the
  offsets. Over short regimes, a gamble slightly below the threshold can win.
- **At equality,** the rates tie and the offsets decide.
- **A higher floor shrinks d and lowers the threshold.** The more a ruined world
  still branches, the more ruin the measure tolerates.
- **Between the threshold and certainty,** the comparison is probability times
  size. The premises make it finite; they do not decide it.

**Example.** Status quo 1 bit per step, floor 0.3 bits, M = 16, so u_max = 3
and d = 0.7.
- The threshold is 0.7/3.7 = 0.189.
- The exact tie probabilities at the best possible upside are 0.198 at m = 9,
  0.1900 at m = 99 and 0.18927 at m = 999.
- They approach the threshold with a correction of about 0.08/m, which in this
  model favours the status quo.

*Verification:* V8.

### Corollary A.5.4 (persistence cap)

Under (F3), let the world itself relax at hazard λ = 1/T_G, where T_G is the
length of the generative window.
- Any rate gain Δh contributes at most Δh·m < Δh·T_G.
- A gain that itself ends at hazard μ has an expected life below T_G, since
  the two hazards combine.

Hence

> \[
> \text{a gain bought at a finite cost } c \text{ is preferred only if }\ \Delta h>\frac{c}{T_G}
> \ \text{ (to first order), whatever its own robustness.}
> \]
>
> *In words:* no gain can outlast the window in which it operates. So a gain
> too small to repay its cost within that window is never worth taking, and
> optimistic estimates of how long a gain will last are capped by the window.

**Proof.**
- Theorem A.4.2 with m = (1 − λ)/λ < T_G.
- Two independent constant hazards μ and λ combine into the hazard
  μ + λ − μλ > λ. ∎

**Under uncertainty about μ,** the expected life is an average, over the
credences, of quantities below T_G. The optimistic tail that dominates the
floor of Corollary A.4.6 is therefore cut off at the window.

**Example.** A cost of 3,538 bits, with the gain's own hazard 10⁻⁴ or 10⁻⁶,
equally likely. With no window, the floor built on the expected life is 0.007
bits per step. With T_G = 10⁵, it is 0.071 bits per step.

## A.6 Resolution: cadence and grain

Lushness is defined at a declared resolution, which has two parts:
- a **cadence**, at which the development is read;
- a **grain**, at which states are distinguished.

This section shows how the measure depends on each, and why rankings can
change with the resolution.

### Definition A.6.1 (declared resolution)

- **Cadence.** A continuous-time law with generator Q, on a finite S, is read
  at a cadence τ > 0 through the kernel K_τ = exp(τQ). A horizon T corresponds
  to T/τ steps.
- **Grain.** A grain is a map f from the states of a finer description onto
  S. The development at grain f is the process f(X_t).
- **Resolution.** A resolution is a pair (τ, f). Every quantity in this
  appendix is computed at a declared resolution.

### Proposition A.6.2 (a coarser grain shows less lushness)

For a map f and a present x of the finer description,

> \[
> H\bigl(f(X_1),\dots,f(X_n)\mid X_0=x\bigr)\ \le\ \ell_n(x),
> \]
>
> with equality exactly when the coarse history determines the fine history.
>
> *In words:* a coarser grain can only hide branching; it cannot create it.

**Proof.** The coarse history is a function of the fine history, and a
function of a random variable has no more entropy than the variable (Cover
and Thomas 2006, chapter 2). Equality holds exactly when the function is
invertible on the support. ∎

**Scope.**
- **The inequality concerns one development.** It does not preserve rankings,
  because a grain can hide more of one development than of another. Take five
  states, and a grain that merges the first four into one state while keeping
  the fifth apart.
  - A development spread evenly over the first four states branches 2 bits
    per step at the fine grain and nothing at the coarse grain.
  - A development alternating at random between the fourth and fifth states
    branches 1 bit per step at both grains.

  The ranking reverses.
- **The coarse process is in general not Markov.** Its lushness is therefore
  not given by Theorem A.2.1 applied to any kernel on the coarse states.
  Section A.7 gives bounds for it.

### Theorem A.6.3 (temporal expansion)

Let Q generate a continuous-time chain on a finite S, read at cadence τ from a
present x over a fixed physical horizon T.
- The cadences considered are those for which T/τ is a whole number, and the
  limit τ → 0 is taken along them.
- The cadence, the horizon and the entries of Q are all expressed in one
  declared unit of time.
- Let q(y) = −Q(y, y) be the rate of leaving state y.
- Let φ(y) = q(y) log₂ e − Σ_{z≠y} Q(y, z) log₂ Q(y, z).

Then, as τ → 0,

> \[
> \ell_T(\tau)=\bar N(T)\,\log_2\frac1\tau+F(T)+o(1),\qquad
> \bar N(T)=\int_0^T\mathbb E_x[q(X_s)]\,ds,\qquad
> F(T)=\int_0^T\mathbb E_x[\varphi(X_s)]\,ds .
> \]
>
> *In words:* read finely enough, a process's lushness splits into two parts.
> The first is the expected number of events it produces, multiplied by a
> term that grows without bound as the clock gets finer. The second is a
> finite term, set by which events happen and how unevenly.

**Source and outline.** For jump processes this is the τ-entropy of Gaspard
and Wang (1993, section 3.3).
1. **One step.** K_τ = I + τQ + O(τ²). From state y, a step stays with
   probability 1 − q(y)τ + O(τ²), and moves to z with probability
   Q(y, z)τ + O(τ²).
2. **Its entropy.** The step entropy is therefore
   g_τ(y) = q(y)τ log₂(1/τ) + φ(y)τ + O(τ² log(1/τ)). The term q(y)τ log₂ e
   in φ comes from the probability of staying.
3. **The sum.** At step t, the development read at cadence τ has exactly the
   law of the continuous chain at time tτ. Summing over the T/τ steps (Theorem
   A.2.1) turns τ Σ_t E[·(X_{tτ})] into the time integrals, with an error of
   order τ log(1/τ). ∎

**Units.** N̄(T) is a number of events and does not depend on the unit of
time. The two other terms do.
- Suppose the unit is made c times smaller, so that numerical times are
  multiplied by c and rates divided by c.
- Then log₂(1/τ) falls by log₂ c, and F(T) rises by N̄(T) log₂ c.
- So ℓ_T(τ) is unchanged, as it must be: it depends on the physical cadence,
  not on the unit in which the cadence is written (V10b).

*Verification:* V10. For a three-state chain over T = 5, the error is 2.0,
0.37, 0.053 and 0.0069 bits at τ = 10⁻¹, 10⁻², 10⁻³ and 10⁻⁴.

### Corollary A.6.4 (crossover resolution)

For two developments with N̄₁ > N̄₂ over the same horizon,

> \[
> \ell_1-\ell_2\to+\infty\ \text{ as }\ \tau\to0,\qquad
> \log_2\frac1{\tau_\times}=\frac{F_2-F_1}{\bar N_1-\bar N_2},
> \]
>
> where τ× is the cadence at which the leading terms tie. If F₂ > F₁, the
> ranking reverses at τ×. If F₁ ≥ F₂, there is no crossover, and the first
> development leads at every sufficiently fine cadence.
>
> *In words:* at a fine enough cadence, the measure prefers whichever
> development produces more events. At coarser cadences it can prefer the one
> whose events are more varied, and the two verdicts meet at a definite
> resolution. A development that leads on both counts leads at every fine
> resolution.

**Proof.** Subtract the expansions of Theorem A.6.3. ∎

**Scope.**
- **The formal root of the activity objection.** At any fixed cadence, the
  objection that lushness rewards activity is a question of what is measured.
  As the cadence is refined, event counts come to dominate (chapter 16).
- **The crossover is a physical time.** Under a change of unit, log₂(1/τ×)
  shifts by exactly the amount that rescales τ×. So the crossover names the
  same physical cadence in every unit.
- **The crossover is computed from the leading terms.** Near τ×, the o(1)
  terms can move it. Its exact location must be found at the actual cadence.
- **Cadence must be justified.** This is why the cadence is part of the
  declared resolution, and must be justified physically (chapter 7).

### Definition A.6.5 (admissible resolutions and sign stability)

- **Admissible grain.** A grain is admissible if it preserves the relations
  named by the physics of the model, and is not chosen by which option it
  favours. Those relations are:
  - actuator dependence;
  - resource conservation;
  - the control alternatives;
  - the recovery routes;
  - the distinction between continuations that the comparison tests.
- **Admissible cadences.** These form a band [τ_min, τ_max].
  - τ_min is the formation time of the fastest record-bearing variable.
  - τ_max is the coarsest cadence that still resolves every native family of
    events.
- **Sign stability.** A ranking is sign-stable if the difference has the same
  sign at every admissible resolution.
  - A small difference with a stable sign is a ranking.
  - A change of sign within the admissible resolutions means the ranking is not
    decided at the declared resolution, however large the differences are.

Cadence robustness alone is not grain robustness.

## A.7 Subjects and marginal histories

The measure can be applied to the whole field or to any part of it. This
section:
- defines the lushness of a part;
- shows how it relates to the whole;
- gives computable bounds on it.

### Definition A.7.1 (projection, valuer and the whole field)

A **projection** is a map π_D from S onto states S_D. Its history is
D_t = π_D(X_t), and its lushness from the present x is

\[
\ell^D_n(x)=H(D_1,\dots,D_n\mid X_0=x).
\]

- The **whole field** is the projection given by the identity map.
- A **valuer** is anything that physically instantiates a frame relative to
  which its possible continuations are defined (chapter 9). Formally, it is
  represented by a projection. In this appendix, a **subject** is a valuer,
  and every result stated for a subject holds for every projection.
- The lushness of a subject is conditional on the full present. A subject can
  instead be assessed from its own present alone, through
  H(D₁, …, D_n | D₀).
  - Averaged over the full presents compatible with the subject's present,
    that quantity is no smaller than ℓ^D_n, since conditioning on less cannot
    lower entropy.
  - For a particular full present, it can be larger or smaller (V11b: 3.50
    bits from the subject's own present, against 2.58, 3.62 and 3.34 bits from
    the three compatible full presents).

  A comparison must declare which quantity it uses.
- **Who counts as a valuer.**
  - **The whole field** is the ultimate frame and counts. No situated process
    occupies it.
  - **Every physical system counts,** gas included, at the declared
    resolution.
  - **Being a valuer requires** neither experience nor agency, neither
    persistence nor present branching. A frame whose continuation is
    determined is a valuer with no breadth at present.
  - **A function that summarizes the field** without being instantiated in it,
    such as the parity of the number of particles, is a projection but not a
    valuer.
- **What makes a frame physically instantiated** is not formalized here
  (section A.15). The candidate is a set of the field's degrees of freedom,
  possibly moving and reidentified by the dynamics, read at the declared grain,
  with a boundary set by the interactions. No numbered result depends on where
  the line falls, because the unindexed comparison takes the whole field
  (Corollary A.7.9).

### Proposition A.7.2 (the whole and its parts)

Let the state be a pair X = (D, E), where E is the rest of the field. Then

> \[
> \ell^{\text{whole}}_n=\ell^D_n+\ell^E_n-I_n(D;E)\ \ge\ \max\bigl(\ell^D_n,\ell^E_n\bigr),
> \]
>
> where I_n(D; E) is the mutual information between the two n-step histories,
> given the present.
>
> *In words:* the lushness of the field is the lushness of its parts less
> what their histories share, and no part has more lushness than the whole.

**Proof.** The identity is Corollary A.2.2(a). The inequality holds because
each part's history is a function of the whole history (Proposition A.6.2). ∎

The mutual information I_n(D; E) measures how far the futures of the part and
of the rest are intertwined.

### Theorem A.7.3 (bounds on a subject's lushness)

Fix a subject D and a present x, and condition everything on X₀ = x. For t ≥ 0
and 0 ≤ k ≤ t, let c_t = H(D_{t+1} | D_1, …, D_t). Then

> \[
> H\bigl(D_{t+1}\mid D_{t-k+1},\dots,D_t,\,X_{t-k}\bigr)\ \le\ c_t\ \le\
> H\bigl(D_{t+1}\mid D_{t-k+1},\dots,D_t\bigr),
> \qquad \ell^D_n=\sum_{t<n}c_t .
> \]
>
> The lower bound is non-decreasing in k, the upper bound is non-increasing
> in k, and both equal c_t at k = t.
>
> *In words:* a subject's lushness lies between two bounds. The lower bound
> adds knowledge of the full state of the field some steps back; the upper
> bound uses only the subject's own last few states. Looking further back
> narrows the interval from both sides.

**Proof.**
- **Upper bound.** Conditioning on fewer variables cannot lower entropy. So
  dropping more of the subject's past can only raise the bound.
- **Lower bound.** Adding X_{t−k} to the conditioning cannot raise entropy.
  Given X_{t−k}, the Markov property makes D₁, …, D_{t−k} irrelevant to
  D_{t−k+1}, …, D_{t+1}. So c_t ≥ H(D_{t+1} | D₁, …, D_t, X_{t−k}), which
  equals the stated bound.
- **Monotonicity.** It follows in both cases from the same two facts.
- **At k = t.** Both bounds condition on the whole history of the subject and
  on the present.
- **The sum.** It is the chain rule for the subject's history. ∎

**Source.** These are the standard bounds for functions of Markov chains
(Cover and Thomas 2006, section 4.5), here at a finite horizon from a given
present.

*Verification:* V11. For a six-state field with a two-state subject, the
bounds hold at every step checked and narrow monotonically.

### Definition A.7.4 (resolved subject rankings)

Two options are **resolved for subject D at depth k** if the intervals of
Theorem A.7.3, summed over the horizon, do not overlap. At steps with t < k,
take k = t, where the bounds are exact. Otherwise the
subject's ranking is **unresolved at that depth**.

"Unresolved" is a report about the bounds, not a tie. The bounds are wide when
the subject's own states reveal little of the state of the field. Private
rankings are then often unresolved at the depths that can be computed
(chapters 8 and 21).

### Proposition A.7.5 (subjects can disagree)

There are finite models, with options a and b, such that

> \[
> \ell^D(a)>\ell^D(b)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\ell^{\text{whole}}(a)<\ell^{\text{whole}}(b).
> \]
>
> *In words:* what is best for a part's own lushness can be worse for the
> lushness of the whole.

**Proof.** Let D be one binary coordinate and E a pair of binary coordinates.
- Under a, D is a fair coin at every step and E is fixed. Then ℓ^D = n and
  ℓ^whole = n.
- Under b, D is fixed and E is a pair of fair coins. Then ℓ^D = 0 and
  ℓ^whole = 2n. ∎

Intertwining does not remove the possibility. Chapter 21 reports a model
result in a coupled world: a dominating subsystem gains while the governed and
the whole lose.

**Scope.** The proposition shows that rankings relative to different subjects
can differ. Which ranking ethical judgement tracks is part of the central
thesis (Definition A.7.8, remarks).

### Proposition A.7.6 (the self–whole gap)

Let the state be a pair X = (D, E), and compare options a and b from the same
present x. Write

\[
\ell^{E\mid D}_n=H(E_1,\dots,E_n\mid D_1,\dots,D_n,\,X_0=x)
\]

for the lushness of the rest of the field given the subject's history, and Δ
for a difference between a and b. Then

> \[
> \Delta\ell^{\text{whole}}_n=\Delta\ell^D_n+\Delta\ell^{E\mid D}_n .
> \]
>
> *In words:* the whole and a part rank options by the same measure, and they
> differ by exactly one term: what an option does to the breadth of the rest
> of the field, given what it does to the part.

**Proof.** By the chain rule, ℓ^whole_n = ℓ^D_n + ℓ^{E∣D}_n under each option.
Subtract. ∎

**Remarks.**
- **When part and whole disagree.** They agree in sign unless the second term
  has the opposite sign to the first and a larger magnitude. Proposition A.7.5
  is a case in which it does.
- **A model case.** In the domination model of chapter 21, at its reference
  horizon and cadence, blocking changes the whole by −13.637 bits and the
  dominating subsystem by a certified [+4.849, +14.059] bits. The rest of the
  field, given the dominator, therefore changes by between −27.696 and
  −18.486 bits. That term is the cost the dominator places on everything else.
- **The bounds apply.** ℓ^D_n is conditional on the full present, as in
  Definition A.7.1, so Theorem A.7.3 bounds it, and the identity transfers the
  bounds to the gap.

*Verification:* V21.

### Proposition A.7.7 (subjects that lose nothing)

For a subject D and a present x,

> \[
> \ell^D_n(x)\le\ell^{\text{whole}}_n(x),
> \]
>
> with equality if and only if the map (x₁, …, x_n) ↦ (π_D(x₁), …, π_D(x_n))
> is injective on the histories of positive probability. A subject whose map
> is injective in this sense under every option ranks every pair of options
> exactly as the whole field does.
>
> *In words:* a part has as much lushness as the whole only when following
> the part loses no distinction between the possible histories of the whole.
> A subject that loses nothing is, for the measure, the whole field.

**Proof.** Let Y be the whole history and f the map. Then
H(Y) = H(Y, f(Y)) = H(f(Y)) + H(Y | f(Y)). The last term is non-negative, and
it is zero if and only if Y is a function of f(Y) almost surely, that is, if
and only if f is injective on the support of Y. ∎

**Source.** The inequality is the entropy of a function (Cover and Thomas
2006, chapter 2). Proposition A.6.2 applies the same fact to grain.

*Verification:* V21.

### Definition A.7.8 (the unindexed comparison)

The **unindexed comparison** of options is the comparison that takes as input
the continuation structure of each option at the declared resolution
(Definition A.1.1), and nothing else. It takes no subject as an input: no index
that names a part of the field whose histories are to be compared.

**Remarks.**
- **No ethical vocabulary.** The definition does not say what ethics is. It
  defines a comparison. The central thesis is a claim about it: that what is
  recognized as good corresponds to expansion in this comparison, and what is
  recognized as harm to contraction (chapter 11).
- **The indexed comparison.** The same comparison applied to a subject is
  indexed. Prudence and motivation track it, usually for the self or for the
  part of the field that supports it. Philosophers call reasons of the first
  kind agent-neutral and of the second agent-relative (Nagel 1986). That
  ethical judgement tracks the unindexed comparison, and prudence the indexed
  one, is part of the central thesis, and it is tested (chapters 10 and 21;
  Proposition A.7.6).
- **The ultimate frame.** The unindexed comparison is the continuation
  structure from the ultimate frame, the whole field (Definition A.7.1).

### Corollary A.7.9 (the subject of the unindexed comparison)

> The unindexed comparison ranks options by the lushness of the whole field.
>
> *In words:* a comparison that names no part of the field has only the whole
> field's continuation structure to measure, and the only measure of that is
> lushness.

**Proof.**
1. Under Definition A.7.8, the input to the comparison is the continuation
   structure of each option, (Ω, P), at the declared resolution.
2. A subject's history law is (π_D)_*P. By Proposition A.7.7, it is the same
   structure as (Ω, P), in the sense of Definition A.1.1, exactly when π_D
   loses no resolved history of positive probability.
3. Otherwise it merges resolved histories that the declared resolution
   distinguishes. To rank by it is to supply a second criterion of sameness,
   the index D, which the continuation structure does not contain.
4. So the unindexed comparison measures (Ω, P) itself. By Theorem A.2.3, the
   effective count of a continuation structure is its lushness, which here is
   the lushness of the whole field. ∎

**Remarks.**
- **One principle twice.** The step that excludes a subject is the step that
  derives the measure: physics, at the declared resolution, decides what
  counts as the same. Following a part counts as the same two histories that
  differ only outside it.
- **What follows and what is claimed.** The corollary follows from the
  definition. Whether ethical judgement tracks this comparison, and not the
  indexed one, is the central thesis, tested class by class (chapter 11). No
  premise of impartiality, or of equal weight for subjects, is used.
- **No ledger of subjects.** Nothing is admitted, weighed or counted twice.
  Overlapping and nested subjects need no rule, because the joint law already
  accounts for what parts share (Proposition A.7.2).

### Proposition A.7.10 (unaffected parts cancel)

Let the field be a pair X = (R, U). Suppose that under both options the
history law factorizes as

\[
P_a=Q_a\otimes M,\qquad P_b=Q_b\otimes M,
\]

where Q is the law of R's histories and M is the law of U's histories, the
same under both options. Then

> \[
> \Delta\ell^{\text{whole}}_n=H(Q_a)-H(Q_b).
> \]
>
> *In words:* a part of the field whose law neither option changes, and whose
> histories are independent of the rest, adds the same amount to both options
> and drops out of the comparison.

**Proof.** The entropy of a product law is the sum of the entropies of its
factors (Corollary A.2.2(a)). The term H(M) is common to both options. ∎

**Scope.**
- **Independence is needed.** A region the options cannot affect, but whose
  histories are correlated with those they do affect, can still change the
  difference through the mutual information (Proposition A.7.2).
- **Causal reach.** A region outside the causal future of the decision cannot
  have its law changed by the options. It drops out if, in addition, its
  histories are independent of those of the regions the options do affect.
- **Dependence does not decide what is compared.** A region the actor does not
  depend on still changes the comparison whenever the options affect it.

*Verification:* V22.

### Proposition A.7.11 (finite comparison with an infinite unchanged exterior; conditional)

Fix a horizon and a declared resolution, and condition everything on the same
full present. Split the resolved history of the field into R, the history of
the region the options can affect, and U, the history of everything outside
it.

**Premises.**
- **(E1) Unchanged exterior.** Both options, as implemented, induce the same
  law on U.
- **(E2) Finite affected entropy.** H_a(R) and H_b(R) are finite.
- **(E3) Exhaustion.** There are finite exterior descriptions U₁, U₂, …,
  each a function of U and of the next, and each of finite entropy, which
  together determine U at the declared resolution.

**Statement.** For every k,

> \[
> \Delta_k=H_a(R,U_k)-H_b(R,U_k)=H_a(R\mid U_k)-H_b(R\mid U_k),
> \]
>
> and, as k → ∞,
>
> \[
> \Delta_k\ \longrightarrow\ \Delta_{\text{whole}}=H_a(R\mid U)-H_b(R\mid U)=\Delta H(R)-\Delta I(R;U),
> \]
>
> which is finite and does not depend on the exhausting sequence.
>
> *In words:* when the options leave the law of everything outside a region
> unchanged, and the region's own history has finite entropy, the comparison
> of the whole field is finite and well defined, even if the whole field's
> total is infinite. It is the limit of comparisons over larger and larger
> finite parts of the field, and the exterior's correlations with the region
> stay in it.

**Proof.**
1. Under each option, H(R, U_k) = H(U_k) + H(R | U_k). By (E1) and (E3),
   H(U_k) is the same under both options and finite, so it cancels.
2. U_k is a function of U_{k+1}, so H(R | U_{k+1}) ≤ H(R | U_k): conditioning on
   more cannot raise entropy. Each sequence is non-increasing and lies between
   0 and H(R), which is finite by (E2), so it converges. Equivalently,
   I(R; U_k) = H(R) − H(R | U_k) is non-decreasing and bounded by H(R).
3. Because the U_k together determine U, I(R; U_k) converges to I(R; U)
   (Pinsker 1964; Gray 2011). The limit depends only on the information U
   carries, so every exhausting sequence gives it.
4. Hence Δ_k converges to
   [H_a(R) − I_a(R; U)] − [H_b(R) − I_b(R; U)] = H_a(R | U) − H_b(R | U). ∎

**Scope.**
- **What it is not.** It assigns no finite value to either infinite total,
  and it is not a new objective. It is the whole-field comparison, obtained
  as the limit of finite whole-field comparisons.
- **Finite extent is not finite entropy.** A region of finite size can still
  have infinitely many resolved degrees of freedom. (E2) is a premise, tied to
  the open question of whether physics bounds every admissible resolution
  (section A.15).
- **The exterior must be unchanged under the implemented options.**
  No-signalling supplies (E1) for interventions localized in the region, with
  the exterior outside their causal future (compare Proposition A.14.8).
  Conditioning on a selected outcome can change the exterior's conditional law
  without any signal, so the options compared are implemented, never
  conditioned (Theorem A.13.1, remarks; Definition A.14.6).
- **Exhaustion recovers the same information at the same resolution.**
  Exhausting sequences agree when they recover the same exterior information
  at the declared resolution. Omitting distant variables, changing the grain
  while expanding, or changing what counts as a history defines a different
  limit.
- **One horizon at a time.** The result defines Δ_whole at each horizon. Its
  behaviour as the horizon grows is a separate question, answered by the
  profile where it exists (Corollary A.3.9). A joint limit in space and time
  needs its own argument.
- **Classical resolved histories.** The result applies to classical models
  and to a fixed decoherent family of a quantum frame (Proposition A.14.3),
  not to arbitrary quantum subsystems.
- **Independence is the special case.** When R and U are independent under
  both options, ΔI(R; U) = 0, and the result reduces to Proposition A.7.10.

**Consequence.** An infinite whole-field total need not prevent a
well-defined comparison between localized alternatives. This answers one class
of the problems that infinite totals raise for comparing outcomes (Bostrom
2011), not every form of them.

*Verification:* V45 (fourteen exterior bits with the same law under both
options, and a four-valued affected history correlated with them; the three
forms of Δ_k agree to 6 × 10⁻¹⁵ bits at every stage, both conditional
entropies are non-increasing under every order of exhaustion tested, the
three orders reach the same limit exactly, the stage increments in the
nearest-first order fall to 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ bits, and independent exterior
variables change the difference by at most 6 × 10⁻¹⁵ bits).

## A.8 Information and action

An inner register is a part of the state that records, computes or varies.
This section shows three things:
- exactly what a register adds to lushness;
- what it adds when it cannot act on the world;
- how the advantage of informed action divides.

### Proposition A.8.1 (what a register adds)

Let the state be X = (Y, S), where S is a register and Y is every other
coordinate. Read each step in the order Y, then S. Then, from a present x,

> \[
> \ell_n=\sum_{t<n}H\bigl(Y_{t+1}\mid X_0,\dots,X_t\bigr)+\sum_{t<n}H\bigl(S_{t+1}\mid X_0,\dots,X_t,\,Y_{t+1}\bigr).
> \]
>
> *In words:* a register adds exactly the part of its switching that the past
> and the rest of the present do not predict.

**Proof.** Apply the chain rule for entropy step by step, with Y_{t+1} taken
before S_{t+1}. ∎

A register whose next state is fixed by the past and the rest of the present
adds nothing through the second sum. It can still change the first sum, by
changing the law of Y.

### Proposition A.8.2 (a register that cannot act)

Suppose the register cannot act on the world: the law of Y_{t+1} given the
past depends only on Y_t, through a kernel K_Y. Let the register have m
states. Then

> \[
> \ell_n=\ell^Y_n+\sum_{t<n}H\bigl(S_{t+1}\mid X_0,\dots,X_t,\,Y_{t+1}\bigr),\qquad
> 0\le H\bigl(S_{t+1}\mid X_0,\dots,X_t,\,Y_{t+1}\bigr)\le\log_2m .
> \]
>
> Here ℓ^Y_n is the lushness of the world under K_Y alone, which is the same
> for every such register. The register's term reaches its maximum, n log₂ m,
> exactly when the register's next state is uniform and independent of
> everything else.
>
> *In words:* a register that cannot influence the world leaves the world's
> lushness unchanged, and contributes only its own unpredictable switching.
> Among such registers, the measure ranks highest the one that tracks nothing
> at all.

**Proof.**
- **The world's term.** The law of Y does not depend on S, so the world's
  history has the law generated by K_Y whatever the register does. The first
  sum of Proposition A.8.1 is therefore Σ_t H(Y_{t+1} | Y_t) = ℓ^Y_n.
- **The register's term.** Each term of the second sum is the entropy of a
  variable with m values. It is at most log₂ m, with equality exactly for the
  uniform law independent of the conditioning (Cover and Thomas 2006, Theorem
  2.6.4). ∎

This is the formal content of the aphorism: if your internal state cannot
influence the world, might as well dream.
- At the same repertoire, a register that only records the world, which is
  information without leverage, scores no higher than one that varies at
  random.
- A register that copies the world exactly adds nothing.

**Scope.** "Cannot act" must hold physically, costs included. A register whose
upkeep draws on resources that the rest of the world uses acts on the world
through that cost. The law of Y then depends on S, and the proposition does
not apply.

*Verification:* V12. The model is a two-state world with four register
designs, run for 200 steps. The world's lushness is 160.8 bits in every
design. The register adds:

| Register design | Bits added |
|---|---|
| Copies the world | 0 |
| Noisy tracking | 57.3 |
| Dreams (uniform, independent) | 200 |

### Proposition A.8.3 (dividing the advantage of informed action)

Consider four arms of one model, built from two choices:
- the register carries information (I) or uncoupled variation (R);
- the register acts on the world (actuation X) or does not (0).

Let ℓ_IX, ℓ_I0, ℓ_RX and ℓ_R0 be their complete lushness over a common
horizon, with every physical cost inside the model. Define:
- C = ℓ_I0 − ℓ_R0, what information adds alone;
- A = ℓ_RX − ℓ_R0, what action adds alone;
- G = (ℓ_IX − ℓ_I0) − (ℓ_RX − ℓ_R0), what they add together.

Then

> \[
> \ell_{IX}-\ell_{R0}=C+A+G ,
> \]
>
> and informed action beats uncoupled variation exactly when G > −(C + A).
>
> *In words:* the advantage of informed action over idle variation divides
> exactly into what information adds alone, what action adds alone, and what
> they add together.

**Proof.** Substitute the definitions. ∎

The identity is empty. Its use is to name the terms, and Proposition A.8.2
constrains one of them.

### Corollary A.8.4 (information pays through action)

Suppose:
- the arms without actuation satisfy the conditions of Proposition A.8.2, with
  the same world kernel and the same present;
- the registers have equal repertoires;
- the uncoupled register is uniform and independent.

Then

> \[
> C\le0,\qquad\text{so}\qquad \ell_{IX}>\ell_{R0}\ \text{ requires }\ A+G>0 .
> \]
>
> *In words:* information without action cannot raise lushness above that of
> a register that varies freely. So whatever informed action gains, it gains
> by acting.

**Proof.** Without actuation, the world's lushness is the same in both arms.
By Proposition A.8.2, the informed register's term is at most the uniform
register's. So C ≤ 0, and C + A + G > 0 requires A + G > −C ≥ 0. ∎

**Scope.**
- **The conditions are narrow.** The corollary concerns equal repertoires and
  an uncoupled register at maximal entropy.
- **Costs count as action.** A register whose upkeep draws on the world's
  resources acts on the world even without an actuator (Proposition A.8.2,
  scope). C can then have either sign. A paid register that varies less than
  uniformly can lose to an inactive register or beat it, depending on its cost
  (chapter 14).
- **Whether G is positive** in physical models is a model result (chapter 14).

## A.9 Harm and protection

### Definition A.9.1 (recovery)

Let C be a set of recovered states, and let C° be the largest subset of C that
the dynamics cannot leave. (A union of closed subsets is closed, so C° exists;
it may be empty.)
- A development **recovers** if it enters C.
- It **recovers permanently** if it enters C°.

**Remarks.**
- **Recovery is not permanent recovery.** A development can recover with
  probability one and recover permanently with probability zero: it can
  return to C infinitely often and never stay.
- **Harm is classified by rate and offset**, not by the words "recoverable"
  and "irreversible". A harm that lowers the rate eventually outweighs any
  harm whose cost is a finite offset (Corollary A.3.8). In growing or
  non-stationary systems, the categories must be restated along the realized
  growth.

### Definition A.9.2 (contraction and expansion)

Compare options a and b from the same present x, for a subject D, at horizon
n, and let

\[
\Delta^D_n(a:b)=\ell^D_n(a)-\ell^D_n(b).
\]

- **Contraction and expansion.** Option a contracts D's continuation relative
  to b by [−Δ^D_n(a : b)]₊, and expands it by [Δ^D_n(a : b)]₊.
- **Resolved and unresolved.** A contraction is resolved if Δ^D_n(a : b) < 0
  at every admissible resolution (Definition A.6.5). If the sign changes
  across admissible resolutions, the comparison is unresolved, not neutral.
- **Offset and rate.** A contraction that converges to a finite amount as the
  horizon grows is an offset contraction. One that grows in proportion to the
  horizon is a rate contraction (Definition A.3.6, Corollary A.3.8).

**Remarks.**
- **Options, not moments.** The comparison is between options from the same
  present, not between a present and its own later states. The passage of
  time narrows what remains to follow, and that narrowing is not a
  contraction.
- **A comparison, not a level.** Lushness is never negative, and the
  definition introduces no negative quantity. Loss appears only as a
  difference between what follows one option and what follows another.
- **Description and evaluation.** Contraction and expansion are descriptive
  quantities. The central thesis claims that what is recognized as harm
  corresponds to whole-field contraction, and what is recognized as benefit
  to expansion (chapter 11). This definition does not make that claim. Material injury, the loss of a named
  capacity or resource, is a third thing. Whether every material injury that
  matters ethically appears as a contraction is part of the central thesis
  (chapters 17 and 32).

### Definition A.9.3 (foreclosure)

In the setting of Definition A.9.2, let the resolved histories of both options
lie in one labelled set. The **foreclosure** of a relative to b for D is

\[
\Theta^D_n(a:b)=\operatorname{supp}P^D_b\setminus\operatorname{supp}P^D_a ,
\]

the resolved histories that are possible under b and impossible under a. Its
**weight** is P^D_b(Θ^D_n(a : b)).

**Remarks.**
- **Foreclosure and contraction are independent.** Either can occur without
  the other, so a report states both.
  - *Contraction without foreclosure.* Under b, two histories are equally
    likely; under a, the same two have probabilities 0.9 and 0.1. Nothing is
    foreclosed, and Δ = h₂(0.9) − 1 = −0.531 bits.
  - *Foreclosure without contraction.* Under b, histories 1 and 2 are equally
    likely; under a, histories 2, 3 and 4 are. History 1 is foreclosed, with
    weight ½, and Δ = log₂ 3 − 1 = +0.585 bits.
- **Finite models only.** The definition is exact for finite models. Physical
  laws seldom assign probability exactly zero, so foreclosure in physical
  systems needs a version tied to the declared resolution. None is given here
  (section A.15).

*Verification:* V23.

### Proposition A.9.4 (the largest contraction)

For any options a and b, subject D and horizon n,

> \[
> \Delta^D_n(a:b)\ \ge\ -\ell^D_n(b),
> \]
>
> with equality if and only if ℓ^D_n(a) = 0, that is, if and only if under a
> the subject's history to horizon n is determined.
>
> *In words:* no option can take away more than everything the alternative
> would have kept open. The largest contraction available from any present
> leaves the subject's continuation determined, a future with no alternatives
> left, and nothing ranks below it.

**Proof.** ℓ^D_n(a) ≥ 0, with equality if and only if the law of the subject's
history is a point mass (Cover and Thomas 2006, chapter 2). ∎

**Remarks.**
- **Maximal contraction is not total foreclosure.** The two are different
  quantities (Definition A.9.3). If b allows histories h₁ and h₂, and a
  produces h₁ with certainty, the contraction is maximal, yet only h₂ is
  foreclosed: h₁, which b also allowed, remains. Total foreclosure, in which
  every history that b allows becomes impossible, is a different condition.
- **A determined future and no future rank alike.** The measure registers
  open alternatives, not existence.
- **One subject and the whole.** A subject that ends now has a determined
  history from now on. That is the largest contraction available to it from
  that point. It forecloses the histories in which the subject continues. It
  is not the largest contraction for the whole field unless nothing else
  remains open.
- **No premise about the bottom.** The bottom of the ranking is fixed by the
  mathematics of the measure. It does not rest on a premise that every view of
  value ranks the absence of valuing lowest, a premise that views assigning
  negative value to some states reject (Smart 1958). Within the manuscript's
  account, disvalue is a contraction relative to an alternative, not a
  negative level (chapter 9).

*Verification:* V23.

**Decision under uncertainty.** Precautionary protection (Proposition A.9.5),
option value (Proposition A.9.6), deference and correction (Proposition A.13.3)
and the prudential margin of chapter 25 are applications of one structure:
- uncertainty about the law or the state;
- information that may arrive;
- what remains actionable;
- how reversible a step is (γ in Proposition A.4.9).

They differ only in where the uncertainty lies. Irreversibility is not a
further value. It sets how much later information can still change the
outcome.

### Proposition A.9.5 (precautionary protection)

Let d_P irreversibly destroy a structure P. Suppose:
- preserving P costs a finite offset c;
- p > 0 is the credence that a valued development depends on P;
- under that dependence, d_P lowers the rate by Δh > 0 for the expected
  persistence m of the regime (section A.4).

Then preserving P is preferred, to first order in m, when

> \[
> p\,\Delta h\,m>c .
> \]
>
> *In words:* for destruction that cannot be undone, even a modest credence
> that the destroyed structure matters outweighs a bounded cost of preserving
> it, provided the regime in which it matters is expected to last long enough.

**Proof.** By Theorem A.4.2, the expected loss from d_P is p(Δh·m + O(1)),
and the cost of preserving P is c. ∎

**Scope.**
- The conclusion is graded in p, Δh, c and m.
- If the dynamics can rebuild P, the loss is shortened accordingly
  (Proposition A.4.9).
- It reverses when preserving P itself lowers the rate.
- It does not apply to credences too small to outweigh the largest physically
  available gain (Proposition A.5.3).
- **A constant excess rate is assumed.** Where the contribution at stake
  expands or saturates, the loss is the expected complete difference in the
  profile under the actual laws of persistence, disruption and regeneration
  (Theorem A.4.2; Proposition A.4.8), and the expected persistence alone does
  not determine it. A small credence outweighs a bounded cost only when the
  loss it multiplies is physically supported, with competing losses and the
  probability of sustaining the contribution included.

### Proposition A.9.6 (option value)

Let an actor maximize expected lushness under uncertainty about which of
finitely many hypotheses holds. Suppose a signal Z can be observed before an
irreversible choice among actions. Then

> \[
> \mathbb E_Z\Bigl[\max_a\mathbb E[\ell\mid a,Z]\Bigr]\ \ge\ \max_a\mathbb E[\ell\mid a],
> \]
>
> with strict inequality exactly when, with positive probability, the signal
> makes some action strictly better than the action that is best without it.
>
> *In words:* when something can be learned before a step that cannot be
> undone, learning first is never worse than committing, apart from what
> learning costs. It is better whenever what might be learned could change the
> choice.

**Proof.** Let a* be the best action without the signal. For every value z,
max_a E[ℓ | a, z] ≥ E[ℓ | a*, z]. Averaging over Z gives
E[ℓ | a*] = max_a E[ℓ | a]. The inequality is strict exactly under the stated
condition. ∎

If observing costs w in lushness, learning first is preferred when the
difference exceeds w.

**Source.** This is the irreversibility effect (Arrow and Fisher 1974; Henry
1974), restated in the measure's terms.

**Scope.**
- **The first layer of prudence.** The proposition comes from expectation
  alone, whenever information can arrive before an irreversible step.
- **The second layer is not here.** A margin set by the actor's model error is
  a policy for acting when the model itself may be wrong. It is not a theorem
  of the measure (chapter 25).

**Example.** A structure matters with credence 0.1. If it matters, destroying
it costs 1,000 bits; preserving it costs 50 bits. Values are measured relative
to destroying a structure that does not matter.
- Committing now means preserving, worth −50.
- A fully reliable signal raises the expected value to −5, so it is worth 45
  bits.
- A signal of accuracy 0.6 is worth nothing, because neither of its values
  would change the choice.

*Verification:* V13.

## A.10 Cooperation and concentration

### Proposition A.10.1 (coupling at fixed marginals)

For processes A and B with fixed marginal history laws,

> \[
> H(A,B)=H(A)+H(B)-I(A;B)\le H(A)+H(B),
> \]
>
> with equality exactly under independence.
>
> *In words:* coupling two processes while leaving each one's own lushness
> unchanged narrows the whole. So cooperation pays only by changing what the
> parties can reach.

**Proof.** Mutual information is never negative, and it vanishes exactly
under independence (Cover and Thomas 2006, chapter 2). ∎

A gain in joint lushness from cooperation must therefore come from one of two
sources:
- changes in the marginal laws, which enlarge the support or even out the
  weights of what each process reaches;
- joint outcomes outside the product of the separate supports.

### Proposition A.10.2 (division and concentration)

Let a resource R > 0 be divided as r₁, …, r_N ≥ 0, with Σ_i r_i = R, among
independent processes. Each acts uniformly over f(r_i) transformations, where
f ≥ 1 on [0, R], so that φ = log₂ f is finite there. The joint log lushness
per step is Φ(r) = Σ_i φ(r_i). Then

> \[
> \varphi\ \text{concave}\Rightarrow\Phi\ \text{is largest at}\ r_i=R/N;\qquad
> \varphi\ \text{linear}\Rightarrow\Phi\ \text{is constant};\qquad
> \varphi\ \text{convex}\Rightarrow\Phi\ \text{is largest under concentration}.
> \]
>
> *In words:* whether sharing or concentrating a resource gives more lushness
> depends only on whether the logarithm of the returns bends down, stays
> straight or bends up.

**Proof.**
- **Concave φ.** A sum of concave functions of the coordinates is
  Schur-concave, so it is largest at the equal division (Marshall, Olkin and
  Arnold 2011).
- **Convex φ.** A sum of convex functions is Schur-convex, so it is largest at
  an extreme point of the simplex.
- **Linear φ.** Φ is linear with a constant sum.
- **Power returns.** For f(r) = c·r^k with c, k > 0, φ is concave on r > 0
  but not defined at r = 0. The concave case is unaffected, since its maximum
  is interior.
- **Returns undefined at zero.** If φ is defined only for r > 0 and is convex,
  concentration gives the supremum, approached as the division concentrates;
  it is not attained.
- **Connections.** Maximizing Φ is maximizing the product Π_i f(r_i). That is
  the Nash product (Nash 1950) and, when f is throughput, proportional
  fairness (Kelly 1997). ∎

The proposition assumes independent processes and uniform repertoires. Coupled
processes fall under Proposition A.10.1.

### Corollary A.10.3 (concentration is conditional)

By Proposition A.2.5,

> \[
> \text{same declared law}\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{same lushness, however control is distributed.}
> \]
>
> *In words:* concentrating control costs lushness only through what it does
> to the law of the development. Overhead, lost local information and lost
> complementarity are ways it can do so, not consequences it must have.

**Proof.** Proposition A.2.5. ∎

**Scope.** Even among independent processes, the effect has either sign
(Proposition A.10.2). Whether concentration costs lushness in a given physical
model is a model result (chapter 20).

## A.11 Matched comparisons

### Proposition A.11.1 (the matched-reference criterion)

Let μ (organized) and ν (reference) be preparations under one finite
irreducible law K, compared by entropy conditional on the starting state. Then

> \[
> \ell_T(\mu)-\ell_T(\nu)=(\mu-\nu)\bigl(b-K^Tb\bigr)\ \longrightarrow\ (\mu-\nu)\,b
> \quad\text{when }K\text{ is aperiodic.}
> \]
>
> *In words:* under a common law that every development shares, an organized
> preparation beats its matched reference by exactly the difference in their
> offsets, less the part not yet realized. It can buy a lasting offset, never
> a faster rate.

**Proof.** Apply Theorem A.3.3 to each starting state and average. The rate
terms cancel, because h is constant under an irreducible K (Theorem A.3.5). ∎

**Scope.**
- **What a different rate requires.** A different long-run rate requires
  leaving these conditions, through one of:
  - different closed classes under a fixed law;
  - a growing state space;
  - a law that changes over time.

  Each must be declared, with its resources accounted for.
- **What may be matched.** Matching ℓ_T itself in advance makes the comparison
  vacuous; what is matched must be the inputs.
- **What matching is for.** It explains a comparison. It does not constrain
  what an optimizer may do (chapter 12).

*Verification:* V17 (error 4 × 10⁻¹⁵ bits).

### Proposition A.11.2 (matched transients)

Let A and B be preparations under one law, and suppose their distributions
over states coincide at step m. Then

> \[
> \ell_{m+n}(A)-\ell_{m+n}(B)=\ell_m(A)-\ell_m(B)\qquad\text{for every }n .
> \]
>
> *In words:* once two developments reach the same distribution over states,
> their lushness never separates further, whatever they passed through on the
> way.

**Proof.** By the chain rule and the Markov property,
ℓ_{m+n}(A) = ℓ_m(A) + Σ_y P_A(X_m = y) ℓ_n(y), and likewise for B. The second
terms coincide when the distributions do. ∎

If the distributions at m differ by total variation ε, the second terms differ
by at most ε·max_y ℓ_n(y), since ℓ_n ≥ 0. The conclusion then holds
approximately, at a rate set by how quickly the remaining difference decays.

## A.12 Bounded comparison

### Proposition A.12.1 (the unresolved remainder)

Let options a and b generate kernels K_a and K_b from a common present, with
step entropies g_a and g_b, and let Δ_n = ℓ_n^a − ℓ_n^b. For n̄ < n,
Δ_n = Δ_{n̄} + R, with

R = Σ_{t=n̄}^{n−1} δ_t and δ_t = E_a[g_a(X_t)] − E_b[g_b(X_t)].

There are two cases.

> \[
> \text{(i)}\ \ \lvert R\rvert\le\frac{4C\lVert g\rVert_\infty\,r^{\bar n-\tau}}{1-r};\qquad
> \text{(ii)}\ \ R=(n-\bar n)(h_a-h_b)+O(1).
> \]
>
> - **(i)** Both options follow, after some τ ≤ n̄, one finite irreducible
>   aperiodic kernel K, with ‖δ_xK^s − π‖_TV ≤ C r^s.
> - **(ii)** The options settle into closed classes with rates h_a ≠ h_b.
>
> *In words:* if after the act both options come under the same dynamics
> that forgets its start, what the actor cannot see is bounded. If the act's
> effects persist, what it cannot see grows with the horizon, with a sign set
> by the long-run effect it knows least well.

**Proof.** The decomposition is Theorem A.2.1 applied to each option.
- **(i)** Let μ_a and μ_b be the options' distributions at τ. Then
  δ_t = (μ_aK^{t−τ} − μ_bK^{t−τ})g. Each of μ_aK^s and μ_bK^s lies within C r^s
  of π in total variation, so |δ_t| ≤ 4C‖g‖∞ r^{t−τ}. Summing the geometric
  tail gives the bound.
- **(ii)** Follows from Theorem A.3.3 applied to each option. ∎

**Scope.** The condition in (i) concerns the dynamics after the act, not
whether the act itself could be undone. The bound is informative only with
usable estimates of C and r. Reversibility enters as an option: an act that
can be undone gives the actor a route from case (ii) back to case (i) once its
effects are seen (Proposition A.9.6).

### Proposition A.12.2 (when removal is licensed)

**The model.** A host develops at rate h_host. A process X is of one of two
types:
- **generative,** with credence 1 − p, in which case it adds Δh_X to the rate;
- **excisive,** with credence p, in which case it consumes the host if it
  escapes, leaving rate zero.

Containment and escape compete. In each step, exactly one of three things
happens:
- the type of X is read, with probability q;
- X escapes, with probability ε;
- neither happens, with probability 1 − q − ε.

The type is therefore read first with probability s = q/(q + ε), and an
excisive X that is read in time is removed. The same s holds for competing
exponential clocks with rates q and ε in continuous time. It does not hold for
independent per-step chances of reading and escaping, where both can occur in
the same step.

**The result.** Comparing long-run rates, removal beats containment exactly
when

> \[
> p\,(1-s)\,h_{\text{host}}>(1-p)\,\Delta h_X .
> \]
>
> *In words:* remove only when the expected loss from an escape outweighs the
> expected loss from destroying something generative.

**Proof.** By Corollary A.3.4, the long-run rates are:
- removal: h_host;
- containment: (1 − p)(h_host + Δh_X) + p·s·h_host.

Removal wins exactly when the first exceeds the second, which rearranges to
the stated condition. ∎

**Example.** h_host = 4 and Δh_X = 2 bits per step, with q = 0.05. Removal is
licensed:
- never, when ε = 0;
- when p > 0.75, if ε = 0.01;
- when p > 0.5, if ε = 0.05.

*Verification:* V14.

**Scope.** This is a comparison of long-run rates. Over a finite persistence
(section A.4), the comparison of totals adds the offsets.

## A.13 Decision and correction

### Theorem A.13.1 (the decision recursion)

For a finite controlled model with kernels K_a and step entropies g_a, define
V₀ = 0 and

> \[
> V_n(x)=\max_a\Bigl[g_a(x)+\sum_yK_a(x,y)\,V_{n-1}(y)\Bigr].
> \]
>
> V_n(x) is the largest lushness over n steps that any deterministic policy
> can attain from x. A policy that chooses a maximizing action at each state
> and number of remaining steps attains it.
>
> *In words:* the value of the best choice now is the branching it produces at
> once plus the expected value of the best choices afterwards, and plans built
> this way remain best when judged again from any state they reach.

**Proof.** By induction on n. Take any deterministic policy σ, possibly
depending on the history.
1. Its action at the present is fixed, say a.
2. The chain rule gives ℓ_n^σ(x) = g_a(x) + Σ_y K_a(x, y) ℓ_{n−1}^{σ_y}(y),
   where σ_y is the policy σ continues with after reaching y.
3. By the inductive hypothesis, ℓ_{n−1}^{σ_y}(y) ≤ V_{n−1}(y). So
   ℓ_n^σ(x) ≤ V_n(x), with equality for the maximizing policy.

Time consistency is Bellman's principle of optimality (Bellman 1957). ∎

**Remarks.**
- **Randomizing policies.** A policy that randomizes adds the branching of its
  randomizing device, which the recursion counts as part of the action
  (Corollary A.5.2(b)).
- **Uncertainty about the kernel.** Suppose the actor is uncertain which of
  finitely many kernels holds, with credences updated by observations that the
  true kernel generates. Then the same recursion over credences is the
  optimality equation for expected lushness. Each possible kernel keeps its own
  history law, so uncertainty enters the comparison, not the measure.
- **Conditioning is not intervention.** Let Z be a fair coin, let C = Z with
  probability 0.9, and let Y = Z. Then P(Y = 1 | C = 1) = 0.45/0.5 = 0.9, while
  setting C = 1 by an actuator leaves P(Y = 1) = 0.5 (Pearl 2009). The
  recursion compares implemented actions, never conditioned ones.

### Proposition A.13.2 (requisite variety and the thin model)

Let disturbances D, responses R and outcomes O = f(D, R) satisfy, for every
response r, that d ↦ f(d, r) is injective. Then H(O) ≥ H(D) − H(R) (Ashby
1956).

Now let D = (D₁, D₂), and suppose the regulator's response depends only on
what it models, R = ρ(D₁), with d₂ ↦ f((d₁, d₂), r) injective for every d₁ and
r. Then

> \[
> H(O\mid D_1)\ \ge\ H(D_2\mid D_1).
> \]
>
> *In words:* under the stated condition, whatever variety a regulator's model
> misses passes into its outcomes. Holding outcomes to a target then requires
> either accommodating that variety or suppressing it.

**Proof.**
- **Ashby's inequality.** Injectivity in each column gives H(D | R, O) = 0. So
  H(D | R) = I(D; O | R) ≤ H(O | R) ≤ H(O), and H(D) ≤ H(R) + H(D | R).
- **The thin model.** For each value of D₁ the response is fixed, and O then
  determines D₂. So H(O | D₁ = d₁) ≥ H(D₂ | D₁ = d₁). Average over D₁. ∎

The injectivity condition is necessary.
- Without it, shielding, buffering or redundancy can make several disturbances
  produce the same outcome; that is accommodation.
- Changing the law of D₂ is suppression.

The good regulator theorem (Conant and Ashby 1970) is cited, not proved here.

### Proposition A.13.3 (deference and correction: a decision model)

This is an analytical decision model with stipulated payoffs. It is not a
theorem that the measure generates these numbers.

**The model.** An action gains a if the system's model is right, which has
credence p, and causes a lasting harm b otherwise.
- **Deferring.** The system defers to an overseer who approves good actions
  and blocks bad ones with accuracy α, at a cost of delay c.
- **A correction channel.** Kept at cost k, it turns a lasting harm b into a
  correctable harm b_c.

**The result.**

> \[
> \text{act}\succ\text{defer}\iff p>\frac{\alpha b-c}{\alpha b+(1-\alpha)a},\qquad
> \text{keep}-\text{disable}=(1-p)(b-b_c)-k .
> \]
>
> *In words:* where harm would last, a system should defer unless its
> confidence is high. A channel of correction is worth exactly the harm it
> turns from lasting to repairable, weighted by the remaining doubt, less what
> the channel costs.

**Proof.**
- **Act or defer.** Acting with oversight disabled yields pa − (1 − p)b.
  Deferring yields pαa − (1 − p)(1 − α)b − c. The first exceeds the second
  exactly when p[a(1 − α) + αb] > αb − c.
- **Keep or disable.** Keeping a channel that costs k yields
  pa − (1 − p)b_c − k, against pa − (1 − p)b for disabling it. ∎

**Toward computed payoffs.** A correction channel is a hazard of regeneration
applied to harms. Suppose the harm ends a regime whose step entropy exceeds its
successor's by s_R − s_S, and the channel restores the regime at hazard γ. Then
Proposition A.4.9 computes two of the stipulated payoffs:
b = (s_R − s_S)/λ and b_c = (s_R − s_S)/(λ + γ). Computing the others from
continuation laws is open.

**Remarks.**
- **The channel's value tracks doubt.** It is proportional to the remaining
  doubt 1 − p. It is greatest where the actor's model error is greatest, and it
  vanishes as that error vanishes. Correction reduces the doubt that a
  prudential margin compensates for (chapter 25).
- **The gains and losses are stipulated** net consequences, not a computation
  of lushness over complete histories. In manuscript prose the result is
  reported as a property of the decision model, never as a theorem of the
  measure.

**Example.** With a = b = 1,000 bits and b_c = c = 10 bits, the thresholds are
0.89 for α = 0.9 and 0.59 for α = 0.6.

*Verification:* V15.

### Proposition A.13.4 (coherence of plan revision)

Let ≻ be a strict, transitive, irreflexive preference over complete plans, in
which pure loss of resources is strictly worse. Suppose each revision
P_i → P_{i+1} satisfies P_{i+1} ≻ P_i. Then no finite sequence of revisions
returns to P₀ with an added loss L:

> \[
> P_0\oplus L\prec P_0\prec P_k\qquad\text{for every }k\ge1 .
> \]
>
> *In words:* an agent that revises a fixed evaluation only towards strictly
> better plans cannot be led round a cycle that leaves it worse off.

**Proof.** Transitivity gives P_k ≻ P₀, and P₀ ≻ P₀ ⊕ L by assumption. So
P_k ≠ P₀ ⊕ L, by irreflexivity. ∎

The result assumes that the evaluation is fixed across revisions. Plans that
include learning need a further analysis.

### Proposition A.13.5 (when ranking by rate can choose)

Let {o_k} be an infinite family of options whose rates h_k increase strictly
towards a supremum that no option attains. Then

> \[
> h_{k+1}>h_k\ \text{for every }k\ \Longrightarrow\ \text{no option is maximal under rate-then-offset.}
> \]
>
> *In words:* if every option is beaten on rate by another, ranking by rate
> chooses nothing, while a comparison over a finite persistence can still
> choose.

**Proof.** Each o_k is outranked on rate by o_{k+1}. ∎

**When rate may come first.** Rate-then-offset gives the verdict of the
comparison over the expected persistence only under three conditions:
1. **The window condition.** The expected persistence must exceed the tie of
   Corollary A.4.4.
2. **The existence condition.** A maximal rate must be attained, as above.
3. **The replacement condition.** A loss that is replaced after a mean time R
   costs an offset realized over about R steps; in Corollary A.4.4, τ_b ≈ R.
   Rate-then-offset treats such a loss as free however long R is. It is
   correct only if the persistence exceeds the tie.

**Example.** Option k waits a mean of 10k steps, then runs at 2 − 1/k bits per
step. Every option is outranked on rate by the next. Over a finite persistence,
the best option is k = 8 at hazard 10⁻³ and k = 70 at hazard 10⁻⁵.

## A.14 Quantum frames

A quantum state does not by itself fix which alternatives count as distinct
futures. This section shows that a physical frame does. A frame's own
interactions fix what it records. Quantum theory then gives the probabilities
of the recorded histories, and the classical results apply to them unchanged.
Two problems remain open, and both are listed in section A.15: which frames are
physically instantiated, and covariance across quantum reference frames.

### Proposition A.14.1 (a state alone does not fix a continuation structure)

Let an idle qubit have Hamiltonian zero and state ρ = |0⟩⟨0|, and consider
single-time projective families in the Z and X bases. Both families are
decoherent, with

> \[
> D_Z=\operatorname{diag}(1,0),\qquad D_X=\operatorname{diag}(\tfrac12,\tfrac12),
> \qquad 2^{H(\operatorname{diag}D_Z)}=1,\qquad 2^{H(\operatorname{diag}D_X)}=2 .
> \]
>
> *In words:* the same quantum state has breadth 1 under one question and 2
> under another. So no number computed from the state alone can agree with
> classical lushness on every family.

**Proof.** A single-time family is trivially decoherent, since distinct
orthogonal projectors at one time give D_{αβ} = Tr(P_α ρ P_β) = 0 for α ≠ β.
The diagonals are ⟨0|P_α|0⟩, which are (1, 0) in the Z basis and (½, ½) in
the X basis. ∎

This is the set-selection problem of decoherent histories (Dowker and Kent
1996; Gell-Mann and Hartle 1993). The rest of the section resolves it by
dependence on the realized interface: what a physical frame actually records.

### Definition A.14.2 (resolved frame and record)

- A **resolved frame** F̂ = (F, 𝓜_F) is a physical frame F (Definition
  A.7.1: a locus with orientation and reidentification), together with the
  quantum instruments 𝓜_F = {𝓜^F_r} that its couplings implement at each step.
  An instrument is a family of completely positive maps whose sum preserves the
  trace. The instruments are fixed by the frame's actual couplings, detectors
  and memories, not by an analyst's choice of basis.
- A **record** is an outcome r of the frame's instrument that is retained at
  the declared resolution over the horizon.
  - A record need not change the probabilities of later records. Its
    occurrence is what counts.
  - A correlation that the dynamics undoes within the horizon is not a record
    at that resolution.
  - Redundant copies in the environment certify that a record is objective
    (Zurek 2003). They are not required: a single internal memory can hold a
    record.

**Remarks.**
- **Which records a frame has.** In decohering regimes the coupling between a
  frame and its surroundings selects a pointer observable: the one whose
  distinctions survive the interaction (einselection; Zurek 2003). The frame's
  records are then fixed by the dynamics, given the frame. That the dynamics
  also fixes the frame is not claimed (section A.15).
- **Unresolved distinctions stay in the process.** A coherent distinction the
  frame has not recorded remains in the quantum process between records. It
  enters the history law when, and only when, it changes the law of some later
  record (Proposition A.14.3).

*Verification:* V29 (with the coupling along Z, Z records form and no X
coherence appears); V30 (rotating the coupling rotates the selected basis).

### Proposition A.14.3 (frame-relative quantum lushness)

Let a resolved frame F̂ record outcomes r₁, …, r_n at its successive steps,
with the dynamics between records included in its instruments. Then

> \[
> P_{\hat F}(r_1,\dots,r_n)=\operatorname{Tr}\bigl[\mathcal M^F_{r_n}\circ\cdots\circ\mathcal M^F_{r_1}(\rho)\bigr]
> \]
>
> is a probability law on the frame's recorded histories, and
> ℓ_F = H(P_F̂) is its lushness. Every result of sections A.1–A.13 applies to
> it unchanged.
>
> *In words:* quantum theory gives the probabilities of what the frame
> records, and lushness counts them. No separate quantum measure is
> introduced.

**Proof.** Each 𝓜_r is completely positive, so every term is non-negative.
Summing over r_n, then r_{n−1}, and so on, uses the trace preservation of
Σ_r 𝓜_r at each step and gives total probability one. The result is a finite
probability law on recorded histories, the setting of Definition A.1.1. ∎

**Source.** Quantum instruments (Davies and Lewis 1970). Processes with memory
between records are covered by the process-tensor representation (Pollock et
al. 2018), which gives the same kind of law.

**Remark.** In the example of Proposition A.14.1, a frame whose coupling
records Z sees breadth 1, and a frame whose coupling records X sees breadth 2.
Which it records is a physical fact about the frame.

*Verification:* V31 (random instruments: total probability one).

### Proposition A.14.4 (the classical limit)

Let each step of the process act on the basis states as a classical kernel K,

\[
\Phi\bigl(|x\rangle\langle x|\bigr)=\sum_y K(x,y)\,|y\rangle\langle y| ,
\]

and let the frame record the basis state without disturbing it,
𝓜_x(ρ) = |x⟩⟨x| ρ |x⟩⟨x|. Then, from the present x₀,

> \[
> P_{\hat F}(x_1,\dots,x_n)=\prod_{t=0}^{n-1}K(x_t,x_{t+1}),\qquad \ell_F=\ell_n(x_0).
> \]
>
> *In words:* in the classical limit, frame-relative quantum lushness is the
> lushness of section A.1 exactly.

**Proof.** After the record at step t, the state is |x_t⟩⟨x_t|, weighted by
the probability of the history so far. Applying Φ and recording gives x_{t+1}
with probability K(x_t, x_{t+1}), and leaves |x_{t+1}⟩⟨x_{t+1}|. Induction on
t gives the product, which is the history law of section A.1. ∎

*Verification:* V32 (agreement to 10⁻¹⁰ bits at every horizon checked).

### Proposition A.14.5 (coarse-graining a frame's records)

Let F̂ and Ĝ be resolved frames, and let q map Ĝ's outcomes to F̂'s so that at
every step

\[
\mathcal M^F_f=\sum_{g:\,q(g)=f}\mathcal M^G_g .
\]

Then

> \[
> P_{\hat F}=q_*P_{\hat G},\qquad H\bigl(P_{\hat F}\bigr)\le H\bigl(P_{\hat G}\bigr).
> \]
>
> *In words:* a frame that records less than another, of the same process,
> sees no more lushness.

**Proof.** Instruments compose linearly, so summing Ĝ's histories over the
preimages of an F̂ history gives P_F̂ of that history. The inequality is the
entropy of a function (Proposition A.7.7). ∎

This is Proposition A.6.2 for quantum records: refining a frame's records never
hides branching.

### Definition A.14.6 (comparison before the branch)

A resolved frame Â **adjudicates** options a and b when:
1. both are physically available continuations from the same state of Â;
2. neither continuation law is conditioned on an outcome that removes the
   alternatives being compared;
3. each option, together with the process, induces a law P^a_Â and P^b_Â on
   Â's recorded histories at the declared resolution.

The comparison is then

\[
\Delta_{\hat A}(a:b)=H\bigl(P^a_{\hat A}\bigr)-H\bigl(P^b_{\hat A}\bigr).
\]

**Remarks.**
- **No joint measurement is needed.** The two options are compared through
  their separate laws, as two kernels K_a and K_b are compared classically.
  Their instruments may be incompatible.
- **Options, not moments.** Conditions 1–2 are the quantum form of
  Definition A.9.2: compare options from the same present, before conditioning
  onto one branch removes the others.
- **Wigner's friend.** From the frame before the branch, "let the friend's
  record stand" and "reverse it, then record the interference observable" are
  two options with two complete laws. They are compared like any other pair.

*Verification:* V36 (the two options in a one-qubit Wigner protocol: 2 bits
and 0 bits).

### Proposition A.14.7 (when two frames have no common history)

Two frames' descriptions of one process can be merged into a single classical
history law only if some physically realizable instrument sequence has both
frames' instruments as coarse-grainings (Proposition A.14.5).

> There are processes for which no such sequence exists. In particular, let a
> qubit start in |+⟩. A frame whose interaction records Z assigns its records
> the law (½, ½). A frame that treats that interaction as coherent, and records
> X, assigns (1, 0). No single law has both as marginals, because once Z is
> recorded, X has the law (½, ½).
>
> *In words:* frames that disagree about which interactions are records cannot
> share one history. Where that happens, the comparison between their
> descriptions is unresolved, not tied.

**Proof.** If the Z interaction is a record, the post-record states are |0⟩
and |1⟩, each with probability ½, and each gives X outcomes with probability ½.
The X marginal of any joint law in which Z is recorded is therefore (½, ½),
not (1, 0). ∎

**Remark.** The obstruction is local to the frames that disagree. Once one
frame's record is copied into an environment that the other frame interacts
with, the second frame must treat it as a record too, and a common law exists.
Extended Wigner's-friend scenarios show the obstruction cannot always be
removed (Frauchiger and Renner 2018; Bong et al. 2020).

*Verification:* V36.

### Proposition A.14.8 (records at spacelike separation)

Let the instruments of two frames act at spacelike separation. Then

> the joint law of their records does not depend on the order in which the
> instruments are applied.
>
> *In words:* no choice of how to slice spacetime into presents changes the
> joint probabilities of records made at spacelike separation. So their
> lushness does not depend on the slicing.

**Proof.** In a model with a tensor-product split, instruments on different
factors have the forms 𝓜_r ⊗ id and id ⊗ 𝓝_s, which commute as maps on the
joint state, whatever that state is. In quantum field theory, localized
measurement schemes for causally disjoint regions compose in either order
under causal factorization (Fewster and Verch 2020). ∎

**Source.** Fewster and Verch (2020); Haag (1996).

*Verification:* V27.

### Definition A.14.9 (continuation-equivalent presents)

Two presents x and y of a resolved frame are **continuation-equivalent** if
they give the same law of future records under every admissible intervention.

**Remarks.**
- **It merges presents, never recorded histories.** Continuation equivalence
  says when two starting points are the same. It never removes a record that
  occurred. A branch whose outcomes later have identical futures still
  contributes its full uncertainty, as an offset (Proposition A.5.1).
- **For a frame, not for the world.** A distinction to which no admissible
  interaction of a frame is sensitive is equivalent for that frame. Another
  frame may still be sensitive to it.

*Verification:* V33 (a recorded branch with identical futures keeps a constant
offset at every horizon); V35 (a sector the frame never touches changes its
lushness by zero).

### Definition A.14.10 (a resolved quantum comparison)

Write the profile of a comparison at horizon T as its observed part plus a
remainder: Δ(T′) = Δ(T) + R_{T,T′} for T′ ≥ T. The comparison is **resolved**
at T if a bound B_T from the physics of the case gives |R_{T,T′}| ≤ B_T for
every T′ ≥ T, |Δ(T)| > B_T, and the sign is stable across the admissible
resolutions and refinements. Otherwise it is unresolved.

**Remarks.**
- **This is Proposition A.12.1 for quantum frames.** A sign that has held
  through T is not a certificate. A bound is.
- **Recurrence.** A finite closed system can undo a record. Then no bound
  holds for every later T′, and the comparison stays unresolved. Open systems
  that decohere and forget can supply bounds that shrink with T.
- **Latent distinctions.** A coherent distinction that no record has yet
  exposed can change later records. Merging it early is an error; the bound
  must cover it.

*Verification:* V34 (a phase invisible to early records changes later ones);
V37 (in a two-qubit closed system the record forms and is undone every π/2).

## A.15 What the formal core does not contain

The results above are proved under their stated assumptions. Several things
the argument needs are not among them. They are listed so that no reader
mistakes their absence for an oversight.

- **The central thesis.** It is a correspondence: what is recognized as good
  corresponds to whole-field expansion, and what is recognized as harm to
  whole-field contraction. No theorem shows it. It is proved, or refuted, one
  class of cases at a time (chapter 11).
  - Whether the breadth that dominates is generative rather than pathological
    is one of those classes.
  - So is whether every material injury that matters ethically appears as a
    contraction (Definition A.9.2).
- **The compression boundary.** The measure is not injective: distinct
  developments can have equal lushness, as Theorem A.2.3 allows. Whether the
  structure it compresses away matters is part of the central thesis
  (chapters 4 and 32).
- **Organization against matched references.** Proposition A.11.1 states when
  an organized preparation wins. No theorem derives that any class of physical
  arrangements wins (chapters 13 and 15).
- **Compounding as a general law.** Sustained rate gains from organized
  enabling are model results. Their generality to cosmological physics is a
  conjecture (chapter 15) [P1].
- **The persistence relation as a law.** Proposition A.4.5 gives the identity
  behind it, and Proposition A.3.10 shows that approximating a structure's
  contribution by its uptime times its excess branching is exact up to the
  change in branching within each regime and an interaction term. That
  physical access, measured independently, sets the sign and size of the
  occupancy term, where the partition is one of access, is a model result
  (chapter 15).
- **Attribution among interacting channels.** Proposition A.3.10 splits a
  difference between declared regimes. When several channels interact, a
  canonical attribution of the difference among them is not formalized,
  beyond the two-factor identity (Proposition A.8.3).
- **Open-ended fields.** Where the state space grows or the law changes, rate
  and offset need not exist and the total need not converge. The object is
  then the profile over later sections (section A.1); no result here supplies
  a single number for it.
- **The second transition.** The capacity of a structure to alter the
  processes that generate future agency, its own or others' (chapter 26), is
  not formalized. The leverage classes (Definition A.3.11) describe possible
  consequences of it. They are signatures, not a definition, and none of them
  is necessary or sufficient for it.
- **Whether physics bounds every admissible resolution.** Finitely many
  stages, with finitely many alternatives at each, give finite lushness at any
  finite horizon (Corollary A.3.12). That every admissible resolution of a
  physical system with finite energy, over a finite time, is finite in this
  sense is conjectured, with physical information bounds as the proposed
  justification (Proposition A.5.3, scope). It is premise (F1) of Proposition
  A.5.3 and premise (E2) of Proposition A.7.11.
- **Infinite fields.** Proposition A.7.11 makes the whole-field comparison
  finite at each horizon when the options leave the exterior unchanged. A
  joint limit in space and time, and fields in which the options' influence
  is not confined to a region of finite entropy, are open.
- **Counterfactual access, and the correspondence of optimal controllers with
  maximum occupancy.** Both are model results without theorems (chapters 13
  and 26).
- **A single ranking across resolutions.** Rankings can change with the cadence
  and the grain (section A.6). Sign stability across admissible resolutions is
  a requirement placed on claims, not a theorem. So is the requirement that a
  declared resolution and horizon let every consequential distinction appear
  in the continuation structure (Theorem A.2.3, remarks).
- **Why ethical judgement tracks the unindexed comparison.** Definition A.7.8
  defines the comparison, and Corollary A.7.9 derives its subject. That
  ethical judgement tracks it, and prudence the indexed comparison, is part of
  the central thesis (chapter 10).
- **Physically instantiated frames.** What makes a frame physically
  instantiated is not formalized, either for valuers (Definition A.7.1) or for
  quantum record structures (Definition A.14.2). The admissible grain
  (Definition A.6.5) poses a third form of the same question. Chapter 9 gives
  a candidate, and chapter 33 the test program.
- **Value requires valuers.** It connects the physical to the moral: without
  a valuer there is no value relation. With valuers defined as physically
  instantiated frames, the priority of future valuing follows from it
  (chapter 9). It is neither a theorem of the measure nor a moral premise.
- **Foreclosure in physical laws.** Definition A.9.3 is exact for finite
  models. A version for laws that seldom assign probability exactly zero is
  open.
- **Irreversibility and orientation.** Proposition A.4.9 takes the hazard of
  regeneration as given. Its relation to the directional asymmetry of the
  dynamics is argued in chapter 28, not proved.
- **The prudential margin.** A margin set by the actor's model error is a
  policy, not a theorem (chapter 25).
- **A universal bound on branching.** Proposition A.5.3 holds given physical
  premises at a declared grain.
- **Comparisons between frames that disagree about records.** Section A.14
  constructs frame-relative quantum lushness and compares options before the
  branch. Two problems remain. Both concern frames inside the whole field, not
  the whole field itself, which contains every frame and every disagreement
  between them.
  - **Covariance.** That physically equivalent quantum reference frames give
    the same comparison, or at least the same sign, is conjectured, not
    proved. Spacelike reorderings are covered (Proposition A.14.8).
  - **Merging frames.** Where frames disagree about which interactions are
    records, no common history exists (Proposition A.14.7), and a joint
    classical history of their records is unresolved. Alternative
    interventions remain comparable from the frame they share before the
    branch (Definition A.14.6).
- **A justified matching rule** for comparing alternatives that differ in
  earlier architecture. Its relation to Theorem A.13.1 is not formalized
  (chapter 33).
- **Estimators with error bounds.** Every number in the manuscript is computed
  exactly, or to stated precision, in a small model. No estimator for realistic
  systems is analysed here.
