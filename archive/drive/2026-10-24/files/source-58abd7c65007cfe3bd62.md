# 5 — Valuers, protection and the central wager

Part II gave the program a measure. This chapter connects that measure to value,
and it does so through two axioms and one wager. The chapter's main job is to
keep these three apart, so that the reader can see exactly what follows from
the axioms, what the wager adds on top of them, and what evidence would show the
wager to be wrong.

The argument can be summarized before it is developed. Value exists only where
something values. Anyone committed to an end is thereby committed to the
conditions on which that end depends. Almost every end extends through time, so
almost every end commits its holder to the persistence of something, and
persistence depends on the physical possibility structure. Because no one knows
where value is or will be realized, the structure must be protected under every
hypothesis about it. All of this is derived. The further claim — that more of
the structure is better, measured by lushness — is the program's central wager,
and the chapter ends by stating the tests that would decide it.

---

## 5.1 Value requires valuers

The first axiom is that value exists where valuing occurs: in processes for
which some distinctions matter. Value is not a substance spread through the
universe, present even in places no process could ever care about.

The claim is deliberately minimal, and most ethical traditions accept some
version of it. It does not say that value is whatever anyone happens to prefer,
that every preference is authoritative, or that only human beings value. It says
only that value is realized in a valuer rather than nowhere. A moral realist who
holds that some things would be valuable even if nothing could ever value them
will reject the axiom, and the program does not pretend otherwise.

On its own the axiom does little. Its force comes from combining it with the
second.

---

## 5.2 Commitment carries to conditions

The second axiom is that commitment to an end carries commitment to the
conditions on which that end depends. Someone committed to another person's
flourishing is thereby committed to the bodily, social and environmental
conditions of that flourishing, whether or not they have yet noticed what those
conditions are.

This has the logical form of what Kant called a hypothetical imperative: if you
will the end, you are bound to will the means (Kant 1785). It is not an
additional moral premise smuggled in alongside the first. It is ordinary
instrumental rationality, applied to the conditions of an end rather than to the
actions that pursue it. This is also how the program handles Hume's observation
that no "ought" follows from an "is" alone (Hume 1739, 3.1.1): the axiom does not
derive an ought from physics, but carries an ought the agent already holds onto
the physical facts that the ought depends on. That is why it binds only those
who hold the end. An agent with no ends is not bound by it, and an agent whose ends are hostile to
value can reject any commitment to value as such.

The axiom has one consequence that is easy to overlook. Dependencies exist
before anyone recognizes them. When an agent discovers that something it already
values depends on people, organisms or processes it had never considered, the
reach of its existing commitment extends to them. Learning more about the world
does not add new values; it reveals what the values an agent already holds have
required all along.

---

## 5.3 Persistence: the metavalue

If conditional normativity applied only to agents already committed to value as
such, it would bind few of them. What makes it bind nearly every agent is
persistence, and the argument for this runs in four steps.

**First, almost every end requires the persistence of something.** Most ends
extend through time: they have to be pursued, maintained or realized at some
later moment. An end of that kind cannot be achieved unless something carries it
forward — the agent itself, its successors, or the conditions that will
eventually realize it. By the second axiom, commitment to such an end is
therefore commitment to the persistence of whatever carries it. Even an end that
requires self-sacrifice requires the persistence of something other than the
agent, such as the outcome it dies for or the people who inherit it. In this
sense persistence is a metavalue: it is implied by nearly every other value,
whatever that value happens to be (formal note F5.4).

**Second, selection makes persistence common.** Chapter 3 described the
persistence filter: whatever can still affect a later situation has, by
definition, persisted until then. Valuers whose organization supports their
continued valuing therefore become more represented among later valuers than
those whose organization does not. This is a fact about selection rather than a
normative claim, but it means that the metavalue is what we should expect to find
among the valuers that actually exist. The observation is old: Spinoza held that
each thing, insofar as it is in itself, strives to persevere in its being
(Spinoza 1677, III P6).

**Third, artificial agents share it.** The same conclusion appears in AI research
under the name of instrumental convergence. Whatever an agent's final goal,
continuing to exist and retaining options help it achieve that goal (Omohundro
2008; Bostrom 2012). Turner and colleagues proved a precise version of this for
a broad class of decision problems: across most possible goals, optimal
policies tend to keep more options available (Turner et al. 2021). An agent with
arbitrary terminal goals still has to persist, and still has to preserve the
conditions under which its goals can be pursued.

**Fourth, a stake in one's own persistence becomes a stake in the structure.** An
agent's persistence depends on conditions beyond itself: resources, other
processes, the stability of its environment, and the future behaviour of agents
it will later need. These conditions are parts of the physical possibility
structure. Crucially, the agent cannot know in advance which parts of the
structure its persistence will come to depend on, because its future
circumstances are themselves uncertain. Its stake in persisting is therefore a
stake in the structure as a whole, not merely in the parts it can currently
identify as useful.

Persistence is the foothold of the argument, not its verdict, and this needs to
be said plainly. Pursued narrowly, persistence is exactly what drives excision:
an agent securing itself by closing off everyone else's routes, as chapter 3
described. The metavalue explains why nearly every agent has a stake in the
structure, but it does not by itself forbid an agent from destroying the parts it
believes it will never need. What restrains that move is the uncertainty in the
fourth step, which the next section makes precise, together with the limits on
every agent's knowledge that chapter 8 develops.

### What persists: the vortex

The metavalue raises a question that matters later: what exactly is it that
persists? For living and social things, the answer is not a fixed body of
matter. The atoms of an organism are replaced many times over its life, the
cells of a body turn over, and the members of an institution come and go, yet in
each case something continues.

The program describes what continues with the image of a **vortex**. In a
flowing liquid, a vortex is a pattern of rotation that persists while the water
passing through it is continuously replaced; what lasts is the pattern of
motion, not any particular water. Physics and biology study the general
phenomenon under other names. Dissipative structures are organized patterns that
exist only while a flow of energy passes through them (Nicolis and Prigogine
1977), and autopoietic systems are networks of processes that continuously
produce the components that make them up (Maturana and Varela 1980).

In this manuscript, a vortex is any organization that persists by maintaining
itself through a throughput of matter and energy: it draws on a gradient,
repairs or replaces its own parts, and would dissolve if the throughput stopped.
A rock persists without throughput, so it is not a vortex in this sense; a flame,
a cell, an organism, a firm and a city are. Vortices can contain other vortices
— cells within organisms, organisms within populations — and each persists at
its own scale.

The vortex is the program's candidate for the carrier of valuing, because it is
the kind of thing for which distinctions can matter: whether a vortex continues
depends on what happens to it, and its own organization responds to that. The
metavalue of persistence therefore attaches to vortices, each at its own scale.
Chapters 6 and 8 take up what follows from this nesting, including the fact that
what counts as loss at one scale can count as maintenance at the next.

## 5.4 Protection by dominance

The derived part of the argument can now be stated in full. It has four steps,
and each one depends only on what has already been established.

1. **We do not know where value lies.** No bounded agent can inspect the inner
   states of other minds, forecast which organizations will come to value, or
   say what they will value when they do.
2. **Wherever value lies, it depends on the structure.** By the first axiom, value
   exists only in valuers, and valuers are physical processes. Whatever value
   turns out to be, it is therefore realized in physical organization within the
   possibility structure.
3. **So the structure is a necessary condition under every hypothesis.** Take any
   hypothesis about where value is realized. Under that hypothesis, the valued
   end depends on the structure, and by the second axiom a commitment to the end
   transmits to the structure. This holds for every hypothesis at once.
4. **So destroying the structure is dominated.** An action is dominated when some
   alternative is at least as good under every possibility and better under
   some. Whichever hypothesis turns out to be true, destroying the structure
   defeats the valued end, while preserving it does not. No resolution of the
   uncertainty favours destruction.

This argument does not need probabilities over the hypotheses, and it does not
assume that more of the structure is better than less (formal note F5.1). Its
reach depends on how general the commitment is. It applies directly to anyone
committed to value as such. It applies to anyone committed to a particular good
through that good's dependencies, which widen as knowledge grows. And through
the metavalue of section 5.3, it applies to nearly every agent through its
stake in its own persistence.

### The same uncertainty selects the object

Welfare, preferences, capabilities, experiences and lives are all features of
the possibility structure. An ethical theory that took any one of them as its
fundamental object would be deciding in advance which kind of thing counts. Step
1 forbids that decision, because we do not know which kinds of things will turn
out to matter. The physical possibility structure is the only object that
contains every candidate without choosing among them.

This is also the source of the program's impartiality. Impartiality is not an
extra axiom. It is simply the starting point of an analysis that does not yet
know who counts.

### The measure is already fixed

Once the structure is the object, chapter 4 showed that there is only one way to
measure its magnitude consistently. A measure that depends only on the weights
of alternatives, and that gives the same verdict whether a development is
assessed whole or step by step, must be lushness (Shannon 1948; Khinchin 1957;
Faddeev 1956).

## 5.5 The central wager

Protection establishes that destroying the structure is dominated. It does not
establish that more of the structure is better. These are different claims: an
end can require that something exist without requiring that there be more of it,
so a necessary condition is not automatically a quantity to maximize.

The program's central wager is that, under uncertainty about where value lies, a
larger structure is better than a smaller one. The reason is that a larger
structure forecloses fewer of the ways value might turn out to be realized. This
is the logic of option value in economics: when the future is uncertain and some
losses cannot be undone, preserving the ability to choose later is worth
something in itself, because it lets choices respond to what is learned (Arrow
and Fisher 1974; Henry 1974). The wager extends that logic from the options of a
single decision maker to the structure from which valuers and their options
arise. It is a wager, rather than a theorem, because that extension is not
proved. Chapter 4 supplies what "larger" means: greater lushness, assessed at
the furthest horizon that can be resolved.

The wager and the program's thesis — that moral intuitions track features of the
structure — turn out to be a single claim seen from two directions. If good and
bad are not arbitrary, it is because they correspond to physical differences in
the structure, and the program claims that the relevant differences are
differences in how richly the structure supports what can follow. Stated
directly:

> **Good** is organization that sustains and compounds the conditions for
> further structured possibility. **Bad** is securing local continuation through
> suppression, destruction or foreclosure of the generative capacity around it.

Chapter 3 supplied a physical mechanism for each half of this statement. The
first is compounding repertoire growth, in which products build further
constructors. The second is suppression in Ashby's sense, in which a regulator
secures its own stability by removing variety from its surroundings (Ashby
1956). Chapter 4 then showed that lushness separates the two in exact models.
Whether it also separates them in the complex, coupled systems that ethics
actually concerns is precisely what the wager stakes.

---

## 5.6 The burden and the decisive tests

The wager carries a burden that should be stated without softening. Lushness
counts every future, including futures of failure, domination and suffering. A
world full of routes to misery could, in principle, be a broad world. The wager
holds only if the breadth that dominates in practice is generative breadth rather
than pathological breadth.

### Why we expect the wager to hold

There are three reasons to expect pathological breadth to lose.

The first is that domination is excisive. A dominating process secures itself by
closing other processes' routes, which by Ashby's fork means removing branching
from everything around it. Unless the dominator's own repertoire grows by more
than everyone else's shrinks, total breadth falls, and a single controller
generally cannot use everything it closes off to others.

The second is that suffering is a signal of narrowing. Control-theoretic
accounts of emotion treat affect as an organism's signal of how well it is
progressing toward its goals (Carver and Scheier 1990). The program generalizes
this: pleasure and suffering function as an organism's internal report on
whether its own futures are widening or narrowing (chapter 6). A world of
pervasive suffering is, by that signal, a world in which the futures of its valuers are contracting. Even suffering that is
deliberately engineered redirects behaviour toward avoidance and withdrawal, and
so narrows futures in turn.

The third is that the prediction cuts in both directions, which makes it
informative rather than convenient. If the wager is right, lushness-optimal
worlds should still contain suffering, because adaptation under load requires
aversive feedback. A world with no suffering at all would be a world that could
not detect its own errors, and it should score worse.

### What would decide it

The program's most important test is adversarial. It consists in building models
of the structure, searching them for high-lushness configurations that are
torture worlds, dominated worlds or other recognizably bad end states, and
determining whether any such configuration competes with generative ones. The
search should pay particular attention to configurations engineered so that
suffering is decoupled from narrowing futures, since those are where the second
reason above would fail. Two targets are decisive.

- **The isomorphic pair.** This would be two worlds with identical branching
  structure at every resolution, one recognizably good and the other recognizably
  bad. If two worlds are identical in their full physical structure, then nothing
  physical distinguishes them, and either their moral difference has no physical
  basis or our labels for them track nothing real. The program predicts that no
  such pair is physically realizable.
- **Breadth-neutral domination.** This would be a world in which access is
  concentrated in a few at no cost to total lushness. It is the more realistic
  danger of the two. The program predicts that concentration always costs
  breadth, for the reasons given above.

Both tests have to be assessed at the furthest horizon that can be resolved.
Example E2 in chapter 4 showed that at short horizons ruin can outscore
maintenance, because failure modes are genuine branches that lushness counts. A
test that stops too early can therefore be won by ruin.

### What failure would mean

If either target is found in some regime, the wager fails in that regime.
Lushness would remain a well-defined physical measure; what would fail is its use
as the measure of value there. Gradient Ethics would then have to restrict its
claim to the regimes in which the wager holds, or identify what the measure is
missing. The program is built so that this outcome is possible, rather than
excluded by definition.

## 5.7 Who counts

At the start of the analysis, every process that has consequences is admitted as
a potential valuer. Familiar organisms, conscious report and declared preference
are not entry conditions. Admission does not establish that a process actually
values anything, or how much it counts. Its purpose is to keep the account from
excluding unfamiliar forms by definition.

The same principle keeps the account from excluding destructive agents by
definition. A process that destroys others may still be a valuer. If the ethics
defined only benign processes as valuers, it would win its arguments by deciding
in advance who was allowed to lose them. Moral standing also need not depend on
agency: a process can matter without being able to act on its own behalf.

Admission is broad, but valuing is not spread evenly. The structure picks out
where valuing is to be expected: in vortices, organizations that maintain
themselves through throughput and for which their own continuation therefore
depends on what happens to them (section 5.3). That criterion admits cells,
organisms, institutions and forms not yet recognized, at whatever scale they
occur, without fixing a list in advance.

---

## 5.8 The vision of Gradient Ethics

The program's ethics is called Gradient Ethics because of the gradient that makes
everything else possible. The universe is far from equilibrium, and that
difference — the free-energy gradient between its present state and its
maximum entropy — is what stars, life, minds and civilizations spend (chapter 3).
The gradient will be spent whatever anyone does. Gradient Ethics is a view about
how it should be spent.

> **Gradient Ethics asks every agent to leave the world more generative than it
> found it: to protect the conditions from which valuers and value arise, and to
> spend the gradient on what compounds — widening what can follow for every
> valuer, at every scale, as far ahead as it can see.**

In operational form, the same vision becomes a decision rule. Protect the
structure. Among the options that protect it, prefer the one with greater
lushness. Assess that lushness at the furthest horizon you can resolve. The
first clause is derived (section 5.4). The second is the central wager (section
5.5). The third is the horizon condition of chapter 4.

Gradient Ethics is a maximizing view under constraint. It seeks the greatest
lushness possible within this universe while recognizing that no real agent
holds the complete comparison. Restraint has no value in itself; where it is
wise, it is prudence, a hedge against fragile over-extension. The familiar
moral norms — non-excision, cooperation, division of labour, prudence, triage
rather than destruction, and a balance between exploration and exploitation —
are predictions of the measure, and chapters 6 and 7 test them. The limits that
bounded knowledge places on any agent's licence to act are drawn in chapter 8,
and chapter 9 turns the vision into a decision theory.

## Formal notes

*Written for readers who want the definitions in full.*

### F5.1 Protection by dominance

Let 𝓗 be a set of hypotheses about where value is realized, and let V_h(a) be
the realized value of action a under hypothesis h. Suppose that under every h
the valued end requires the possibility structure S, so that any action d
destroying S satisfies V_h(d) ≤ V_h(a) for every action a that preserves S. Then
d is weakly dominated by every S-preserving action, and strictly dominated
wherever some S-preserving action realizes positive value under some h. No
probability measure on 𝓗 is needed. The premise is the content of step 2:
valuers are physical processes within S.

### F5.2 Option value

Under uncertainty with learning and irreversibility, the expected value of
keeping the ability to choose later exceeds the expected value of committing now
by the quasi-option value (Arrow and Fisher 1974; Henry 1974). The effect
requires that information arrive before the option lapses and that the
foreclosing action be irreversible. The central wager extends this from a single
decision maker's options to the structure from which valuers and their options
arise; because that extension is not a theorem, the claim is a wager.

### F5.3 Instrumental convergence

Omohundro (2008) and Bostrom (2012) argued that agents with a wide range of final
goals share instrumental subgoals, including self-preservation and the
acquisition of resources. Turner and colleagues (2021) proved, for Markov
decision processes and under symmetry conditions on the distribution of reward
functions, that optimal policies tend to seek power in the formal sense of
keeping more options available. The same results predict acquiring other
agents' options, so they support the footing of the metavalue without supporting
restraint.

### F5.4 Ends that extend through time

An end E is time-extended if realizing it requires some state of affairs at a
time later than the moment of commitment. For such an E, the conditions of E
include the persistence, until those times, of some process that realizes or
carries it, and conditional transmission yields a commitment to that
persistence. The ends for which this fails are those realized entirely at the
moment of commitment, which excludes every end that requires pursuit.

### F5.5 Vortices

A vortex is a physical organization O that (i) persists over an interval while
its constituents are replaced, (ii) is maintained by a throughput of matter or
free energy drawn from a gradient, and (iii) would dissolve if that throughput
ceased. Conditions (ii) and (iii) distinguish vortices from static persistence.
Dissipative structures (Nicolis and Prigogine 1977) and autopoietic systems
(Maturana and Varela 1980) are established cases. Vortices can be nested, with
the constituents of one being vortices at a finer scale.

## For alignment

This chapter answers two questions an alignment reader will ask, and leaves one
open.

**Why would a capable system accept the standard at all?** It need not share
anyone's values. If it has ends that extend through time, it is committed to its
own persistence, and through its dependencies to the structure. Conditional
normativity is not an extra requirement imposed on such a system; it is the
instrumental rationality it already has.

**Why does that not simply license power-seeking?** Persistence on its own does
push toward acquiring options, including other agents' options, as the
instrumental-convergence results predict. What restrains this is uncertainty
about which parts of the structure the system will depend on, together with the
limits of its own knowledge, which chapter 8 turns into limits on licence and
chapter 9 into a requirement of corrigibility.

**What remains open is installation.** Gradient Ethics specifies what an aligned
system should protect and prefer. Making a system actually hold that standard is
a separate problem, and the program does not claim to have solved it.
