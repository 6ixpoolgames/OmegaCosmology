[Chapter 1](#chapter-1) asked who counts, and found that every standard of
good risks settling that question before it begins. [Chapter 2](#chapter-2)
built the object the rest of the manuscript studies: Omega, everything that
can follow from every present. The construction is physical. It tells us what
is possible, not what is good. Somewhere between those two questions lies the
philosophical foundation of the program: how can a physical consequence be a
consequence that matters?

This chapter develops [Overview section 2](#overview-2) by answering that
question in stages. Value requires valuers (3.1). Consequence connects them to
what follows (3.2). No option available now can alter what is already realized
(3.3), so better and worse require a comparison of accessible continuations
(3.4). Those futures are the substrate of value still to come (3.5). And every
physical system qualifies, with no special admission test (3.6). The chapter
locates value. It does not yet measure it. Why the comparison should cover the
whole field belongs to [chapter 4](#chapter-4); why widening that field should
count as good is the central thesis of [chapter 10](#chapter-10).

## 3.1 Where value can be found

**Without valuers there is no value. Valuers are what value is relative to.**

Ordinarily, an ethical theory begins with a kind of good and then asks who
may possess it. Pleasure belongs to those who feel it; preferences belong to
those who form them; flourishing belongs to creatures that can flourish.
The question of standing has already been partly answered by the choice of
good. The program begins a step earlier. Before deciding which developments are
better, it asks what must be true for a development to be better *for or of*
anything at all.

Physics describes how a present develops: what moves, what forms, what
breaks down, what becomes possible next. Where the law permits alternatives,
it describes those as well. Such descriptions can be extraordinarily rich
without containing an ethical ordering. Adding detail does not, by itself,
turn an account of what happens into one of what ought to happen. This is the
familiar gap emphasized by Hume (1739), and the program accepts it.

What is missing from the bare description is not necessarily a mind to watch
it. It is the relation in which a consequence can matter. A development is
better or worse only in relation to something it happens to, sustains,
transforms or brings into being. We call a physical bearer on that side of the
relation a *valuer*. The foundational principle is simple: value requires
valuers. The challenge for the rest of this chapter is to say what that
principle requires, without quietly limiting the answer to familiar kinds of
beings.

**Relational does not mean subjective.** One might think that if value is
relative to valuers, it is whatever they take it to be. It is not. The relation
holds between a physical system and the physical continuations of its present,
and those terms are fixed by the law and the present, whatever anyone believes.
A cell's physically available development can change when its supply is cut,
whether or not anything registers the change. To call the cell a valuer is not
to give it an opinion about the cut. It is to locate a physical system for
which consequences exist. The terms of the relation are objective physical
facts. Which ordering of their consequences holds is a further question. This
chapter does not answer it; [chapter 10](#chapter-10) proposes a physically
determined one.

**It is compatible with intrinsic value.** Moore (1903) asked us to imagine a
world of great beauty that no one will ever see, and judged that its existence
is still better than that of a heap of filth. If so, the objection runs, some
value needs no valuer. But Moore's world removes observers, not valuers in the
sense this chapter develops. Whatever value the beautiful world has, it has
because something exists for it to be the value of. A theory of intrinsic value
still places value in a bearer that exists, and that is all the principle as
stated here requires. This is compatibility with the principle, not with the
whole theory. That value runs through consequence (3.2) is a further claim, and
a theorist of intrinsic value may reject it. The disagreement should remain
visible. Denying that value needs an observer does not deny that it has a
bearer. But saying that it has a bearer does not settle what makes the bearer
good.

> **Plain-English definition 3.1: Value requires valuers**
>
> Value is a relation between something and what happens to it. Without
> something for value to be relative to, nothing is better or worse.

**It is not an axiom.** An axiom would be introduced as a starting stipulation.
Here the principle has an argument: value, if it is to mean anything in a
physical world, must be value relative to something in that world. By the end
of the chapter, the claim comes close to a modest one: physical value has
physical bearers. But its consequences are not modest. It asks whether we have
grounds to exclude any physical participant before the comparison has even
begun. The principle fixes where value can be. It does not yet say what is
good.

## 3.2 What connects valuers to value

**Consequence. What can matter to a valuer is what follows for it.**

A principle that value needs valuers says nothing yet about what a valuer is,
or about what in its situation can matter. There must be a connection between
the bearer and the development to which value might attach. That connection
is consequence: the dependence of what follows on what is present. Under the
law, a system's present constitution and relations help determine its course.
It can grow or break down, be supplied or cut off, carry on or come apart;
what happens through it can alter the developments of other systems in turn.

This is physical consequence whether anyone notices it or not. It becomes
relevant to value because it pertains to something. We need not first ask
whether that thing understands the consequence, can prefer one outcome, or
could have acted otherwise. Those are further capacities. Consequence is the
bridge from a bearer to what can matter for it. It does not make those further
capacities conditions of admission.

> **Plain-English definition 3.2: Consequence**
>
> The dependence of what follows on what is present: under the law, the
> present shapes what can come next.

> **Plain-English definition 3.3: Valuer**
>
> A physical system that takes part in consequential continuation. It need not
> experience, choose, persist or be a single unified thing.

> **In Omega**
>
> A valuer is a physical system whose development runs through Omega. Its
> futures are the part of Omega that follows for it, always counted with the
> whole present in view.

**Consequence is not branching.** Consequence means that what follows depends
on what is present. Branching means that more than one continuation can follow
one specified present. A system can take part in consequence without branching
at all: its course can be fixed and still depend on its present conditions.
This is why the criterion can be universal without demanding that every
valuer possess options or exercise choice. Being a valuer needs consequence.
Comparing better and worse needs something more: alternatives physically
accessible from the comparator's present (3.4). Confusing those requirements
would turn determinism into an artificial exception to valuerhood.

## 3.3 Why value faces the future

**What is already realized is common to every option. Only what follows can
differ.**

Why should value face the future rather than reside equally in every direction
of time? The answer is not that the past lacks value. It is that the present
marks a physical difference between what is settled and what remains
accessible.

Start from one present, specified as [chapter 2](#chapter-2) requires, and
consider the options physically open from it. They share that present and its
realized history. No choice among them can replace an event that has already
happened. That fact does not remove the event's significance. But the ways in
which the options differ must lie in what follows. The past can matter to their
comparison through what it leaves active: injury that can heal or worsen,
knowledge that can be preserved or lost, a relationship that can be repaired or
broken further. These are consequences still to come.

So value already realized is not value still open to realization. Suffering
that has happened was suffering; flourishing that has happened was flourishing.
Neither is made unreal by the passage of time. Their remaining effects,
however, enter the next comparison as part of the present. The distinction is
not between a valuable future and a valueless past. It is between realized
value and value that can still be gained or lost.

> **Plain-English definition 3.4: Value already realized**
>
> Value in the past and present. It cannot be gained or lost by choosing among
> options now, though its consequences can still be shaped.

**The asymmetry is one of access.** A past event may remain uncertain to an
observer: the present may fail to disclose which of several histories led to
it. That ignorance does not restore those histories as alternatives one can now
realize. Conversely, futures can remain accessible under a law whose statistics
do not change over time. What gives prospective value its direction is not how
much an observer knows. It is which consequences remain physically reachable.

## 3.4 Better and worse

**A continuation can be worse than another continuation of the same present.
Nothing has to be worse than nothing.**

An ethical sign is a sign of a difference. A comparison does not need a
quantity stamped onto each completed history. From one admissible present,
distinct accessible options may lead to different continuations and different
distributions of later possibility. One can be better or worse than another.
The comparison is real even though neither option carries an absolute negative
label. Naming the alternatives does not settle which is better; the central
thesis answers that question.

> **Plain-English definition 3.5: Narrowing and widening**
>
> One option narrows what follows, relative to another from the same present,
> when fewer futures are open under it. Widening is the reverse. Both are
> comparisons of accessible continuation, never levels.

Narrowing and widening are physical comparisons. The central thesis
([chapter 10](#chapter-10)) identifies them with losses and gains in
accessible value; this chapter does not assume that identification. The
distinction is essential. We can establish where a comparison must be made
without yet establishing its ethical verdict.

**No absolute negative is needed.** Some accounts give states a value of their
own below zero. A hedonic account does so naturally, and summed levels can then
make a life or a world worth less than nothing (Smart 1958). The argument need
not settle that dispute before asking what can be gained or lost from a
present. Its comparisons have signs relative to accessible alternatives. If
negative value of some other kind exists, the substrate argument remains
available. The measure built in Part II has a floor: a continuation fixed to
one outcome. That floor is a property of the measure, not a law about value.
Appendix E takes up the dispute in full, and [chapter 18](#chapter-18) tests
whether what we recognize as the badness of suffering corresponds to narrowing.

**Determinism is the limit case, and it is not a problem.** Specify a present
completely under a deterministic law, and exactly one continuation follows. The
consequences may be extensive. But that present has no competing continuation,
so no relative sign can be assigned. Its systems remain valuers: their
development still depends on the physical conditions present. Two different
deterministic outcomes come from different presents, not from a choice between
futures available to one. Comparing them as though they were competing
continuations would change the comparator and then conceal the change. The
framework does not make that comparison.

## 3.5 The substrate of value still to come

**The futures open from a present are where value can still be gained or
lost.**

The argument can now be assembled. Value requires valuers. Consequence
connects those valuers to developments that can matter. From a specified
present, only what follows is still available to become different. The
futures accessible from that present are therefore the substrate of value
still to come.

This substrate is not a catalogue of today's valuers extended into tomorrow.
Some systems will cease, some will change beyond recognition, and some that do
not yet exist may arise. Their relations will alter one another's futures. The
consequences of a present include these emergences and endings, and the further
consequences they cause. A fixed inventory of present identities would miss
much of what may become valuable. The continuation structure can be described
without that inventory.

> **In Omega**
>
> The substrate of value still to come, from a present, is the part of Omega
> that can follow from it.

Only futures that can actually be reached belong to the substrate. A
continuation the law does not permit adds nothing. Nor are all permitted
continuations equally accessible. The branching that a state can subsequently
open must be weighted by the probability of reaching that state. These
probabilities belong to the physical law, not to the analyst's preferences.
How to count the substrate while respecting both reach and likelihood is the
job of [chapter 5](#chapter-5). For now, the object to be counted is clear:
the effective multiplicity of physically accessible futures, including their
higher-order consequences.

**The metavalue.** Value still to come requires future bearers, but their mere
continued existence is not enough to distinguish the futures at stake. Under
universal admission (3.6), valuers persist almost anywhere physical
continuation persists. The consequential question is what that continuation
keeps possible. We call the conditions through which future value can become
accessible the metavalue. They include the emergence, recovery and
transformation of valuers.

This turns a familiar presumption about persistence into a conditional claim.
Continuing may preserve opportunities that an ending would foreclose. But a
structure may also last by preventing a more generative successor from arising.
Recovery may restore possibilities that maintenance cannot, and replacement may
open possibilities that survival would close. Persistence is worth what it
keeps open, compared with what would otherwise follow, and no more. So death,
dissolution and transformation are not good or bad in themselves. That depends
on how their consequences compare. [Chapter 7](#chapter-7) makes this exact.

> **Plain-English definition 3.6: The metavalue**
>
> The continuation of the conditions through which future value can become
> accessible, including the emergence, recovery and transformation of
> valuers. It is instrumental: lasting is worth what it keeps open.

**A reason, for agents.** None of this yet tells anyone what to do. A norm of
practical reason supplies a first step: whoever is committed to an end is
committed to the conditions it depends on (Kant 1785). An agent who values an
outcome has a reason to protect the physical conditions required for that
outcome to remain accessible. But the step is limited. An agent can value one
outcome without having undertaken to preserve every possibility of future
valuing. The broader ethical significance needs [chapter 4](#chapter-4)'s
argument about scope and [chapter 10](#chapter-10)'s thesis about what is at
stake in that scope.

This reason addresses agents, which can act on reasons. It does not attribute
such commitments to every physical system that qualifies as a valuer. Nor is it
a blanket command to preserve whatever currently exists. On the framework's
account, keeping a channel for value open can sometimes require maintenance,
sometimes repair, and sometimes allowing one structure to give way to another.

## 3.6 Who counts

**Anything that takes part in consequential continuation is a valuer.**

We can now return to the question with which [chapter 1](#chapter-1) began.
Who counts? If we answer with a list of favored kinds of beings, we have
already imported a theory of what makes those kinds special. The preceding
argument offers a criterion before such a list is drawn. If a valuer is what
consequence pertains to, whatever physically participates in consequential
continuation qualifies. That is a claim about minimal standing, not a claim
that every participant has the same future or the same influence on others.

**Argument 3.1 (universal admission).**
- **(P1)** Value is relative to valuers (3.1).
- **(P2)** What makes something a valuer is that consequence pertains to it:
  what follows depends on its present and runs through it (3.2).
- **(P3)** Every physical system takes part in consequential continuation.
- **(P4)** Once participation in consequential continuation is admitted, no
  further property has been shown necessary for minimal standing as a valuer.
- **(C)** Every physical system is a valuer.

The conclusion follows from P2 and P3. P1 says why valuerhood matters at all.
P2 is the substantive philosophical bridge: it treats participation in
consequence as sufficient for minimal standing. P3 makes the conclusion
universal. P4 is not another logical step needed to force the conclusion. It is
the defense of P2 against restrictions. The program's norm is that physics, not
the theorist's preferences, decides what counts as a physical distinction. An
additional boundary on standing requires a reason.

Rival criteria have arguments, and they should be met on their own terms.
Experience, agency, life, persistence and organization have each been proposed
as the line, and each has defenders. They can describe genuine and important
differences among physical systems. The question P4 puts to each is narrower:
once consequence is admitted, what shows that the additional property is
necessary for *minimal* standing, rather than for a richer capability or
different kind of life? Such boundaries may be useful for science and
practice. But their usefulness does not by itself give them the authority to
exclude everything beyond them from value. Many also introduce difficult
questions of where a system begins, ends or remains the same over time.
The framework's comparison need not wait for those questions to be
settled.

So a particle in a gas is a valuer, and so is a cell, a person, an institution,
an artificial agent, a short-lived eddy, a scattered population, and the
universe as a whole. Nothing on that list needs permission from a higher class
of beings. Equally, a mathematical description is not a new physical valuer. A
total, average or parity computed from the world describes physical systems; it
is not another system. And existence alone does not qualify. A structure with
no consequential continuation at all, if there could be one, would not count
merely because it could be said to exist. The criterion is participation, not
the word *exists*.

**Separate questions.** Being a valuer settles minimal standing; it is not a
certificate of sophistication. It is not agency, which is physically
implemented control over consequence ([chapter 25](#chapter-25)). It is not
identity, which is the continuity of a pattern across presents
([chapter 22](#chapter-22)). It is not consciousness, which the measure can
distinguish only through the difference it makes to what follows
([chapter 18](#chapter-18)). A system that changes what follows under a
given comparison is a *deformer*. A deformer is a valuer like any other, but a
valuer need not change the field measurably to count. These questions remain
important. They simply cannot do the work of admission without an argument of
their own.

**Universal standing does not mean equal consequences.** Being a valuer settles
that consequence pertains to something. It does not settle how much is at
stake. A molecule in a gas and a living cell differ in what their continuation
holds and in what it opens for everything around them, and the effective count
of Part II reads that difference from the physics. No valuer is assigned a
weight, and no ledger of valuers is kept: [chapter 4](#chapter-4) explains why
none is needed. The differences come in tiers: branching supplied by thermal
motion; structures that persist and sustain further branching; structures that
build further structures; and processes whose effects propagate through many
generations of possibility. These tiers describe mechanisms that must be tested
under the same physical law. They are not ranks of standing.

**The thermal baseline is taken on.** Because every physical system counts, a
gas counts, and every comparison is made against a world that still branches.
The framework cannot announce that the branching of disorder is worthless
because it does not resemble life or thought. That would undo universal
admission at the moment the mathematics became inconvenient. Whether
organization opens more than disorder, and when, must be answered under the
same physics. [Chapter 15](#chapter-15) asks that question directly.

## 3.7 What the argument rests on

**The framework adds no axioms of its own.** That is not a claim that nothing
is being asked of the reader. The argument has a foundational principle, norms
of reasoning, physical premises and a central thesis. Each carries a different
burden of justification. Stated together, they are:

- **The foundational principle:** value requires valuers, with consequence as
  its content (3.1, 3.2). Argued.
- **Two norms of reasoning,** borrowed, not invented: instrumental consistency
  (3.5); and no distinction without justification (3.6).
- **Physical premises,** open to empirical challenge: development is oriented;
  a native law fixes what can follow, with objective probabilities where
  physics supplies them; presents can be specified; consequences stay within
  causal reach ([chapter 2](#chapter-2)).
- **One thesis,** stated and defended later: that the expansion of the
  substrate, measured over the whole field, is the expansion of accessible
  value ([chapter 10](#chapter-10)).

**What this chapter does not claim.**
- That value can be proved from physics. Physics supplies the terms of the
  relation; the principle is argued.
- That intrinsic value is impossible.
- That being a valuer requires experience or agency, or that experience and
  agency do not matter.
- That valuers carry weights.
- That negative value cannot exist.
- That widening the future is good. That is the thesis, earned case by case.

*Nihilism, briefly.* If nothing matters because nothing is watching, the
argument has slipped an observer into the definition of value without defending
the requirement. A world without watchers is not therefore a world without
consequential physical systems. The framework locates the relevant relation
within physics, and asks its further ethical question there. That is a reply to
one route to nihilism, not a declaration that the central thesis is proved.
[Chapter 29](#chapter-29) takes the issue up in full.
