## Section 1: The Central Thesis

> **TL;DR**
>
> Every ethical theory has to compare outcomes, and every one so far begins
> by deciding what matters and who counts. We begin with physics instead.
> - **The object of ethics is Omega:** everything that can follow from a
>   present.
> - **Its measure is lushness:** how many genuinely different futures a present
>   supports.
>
> **The central thesis:** what we recognize as good corresponds to the
> widening of the whole world's futures, and what we recognize as harm
> corresponds to their narrowing. The correspondence is not true by
> definition. Omega has to earn it, one class of cases at a time.
>
> **Value requires valuers** is the link from physics to value: without
> something that values, nothing physical is good or bad. A valuer is anything
> with a frame of its own from which futures can be projected, and the whole
> world is the ultimate frame. Who counts, how to count and what persistence is
> worth then follow from physics, with no further assumption.
>
> **Self-interest and ethics use the same measure.** They differ by exactly
> one term: what an act does to the breadth of everything else.

### The problem

To say that one outcome is better than another, you need a standard for
comparing them. Every standard ethics has used so far starts by deciding what
matters.
- Welfare counts those who can fare well or badly.
- Preference counts those who have preferences.
- Rights count those who hold them.

Each standard settles the hardest question, who and what counts, before the
inquiry begins. Each is then exposed whenever its answer to that question is
disputed, and it always is.

The problem has become urgent. We are building artificial agents that may come
to understand the world better than we do. They will need a standard for
comparing outcomes with three properties:
- it does not depend on whose preferences trained them;
- it favours no group of beneficiaries;
- it stays well defined as their understanding outgrows ours.

No standard that begins by deciding who counts can meet all three.

### Our resolution

**Ethics has a physical object, and the object has a measure.**

Given the state of the world now and the laws that carry it forward, physics
assigns a probability to every way the future can unfold.
- Each such way is a **future**.
- The futures of a present, with their probabilities, form a definite physical
  structure.
- That structure, taken over every present, is **Omega**.

> **Plain-English definition 1: Future**
>
> One complete way things can go from now: the full sequence of states the
> world passes through, not just where it ends up.

> **Plain-English definition 2: Omega**
>
> Every future from every present, each with its probability, treated as a
> single object.

The measure is **lushness**: how many genuinely different futures a present
supports, with each future counted by how likely it is. Section 2 explains why
this is the right way to count, and how it avoids the traps that have caught
earlier attempts.

### Value requires valuers

**Value requires valuers. That is the link between physics and value.**

Physics describes what can follow from a present. It does not say that any of
it is good or bad. Value enters only where something values: where there is a
standpoint from which some futures are its own. Without a valuer there is no
value relation at all, only physics. So the link is not a moral rule. It says
where value can be found, not what is good.

A valuer is anything with a frame of its own: a physical standpoint from which
its futures can be projected. A cell has one, and so do a forest, a person, an
institution, an artificial agent and a region of gas. Each has a present of its
own, and its futures follow from it. Valuing requires neither experience nor
agency. No kind of process is excluded in advance, so no one has to decide who
is admitted.

One frame contains all the others: the whole world, the **ultimate frame**. It
counts as a valuer too, though no part of the world can occupy it.

> **Plain-English definition 3: Valuer**
>
> Anything with a frame of its own: a physical standpoint from which its
> futures can be projected. Being a valuer does not require consciousness or
> intention.

Two things follow from the link at once.
- **Future value needs future valuing.** Where nothing values, nothing has
  value. So value at any later time requires that valuing persist, recover or
  arise. The persistence of valuing is not one value among others. It is a
  condition of every value not yet realized, and that is why persistence is
  the metavalue.
- **There is no free-floating disvalue.** Disvalue needs a valuer as much as
  value does, and for a valuer to disvalue is for it to register that its
  continuation is being narrowed. So every loss is a comparison, between what
  follows from one act and what would have followed from another. Nothing sits
  below zero, waiting to be added up.

One principle of practical reason binds every agent to this. Whoever wills an
end is committed to the conditions it depends on, and almost every end that
extends through time depends on the persistence of whatever realizes it. That
principle is a matter of consistency, not of ethics.

### Whose futures?

Every ethics must say whose outcomes it compares, and every answer has been
contested.
- **Pick a group,** and the theory inherits the dispute over who belongs to
  it.
- **Count every individual equally,** and you must first say what an
  individual is. A person contains cells. A family overlaps its members. An
  institution overlaps the people in it. Equal weighting over subjects is
  undefined until someone decides what the subjects are.
- **Assert impartiality as a further moral axiom,** and the axiom leaves open
  the question it was meant to settle: who is everyone? Nor does it say why
  anyone should accept it.

### Our resolution: the comparison with no index

**The same measure can compare futures for a part of the world, or for the
whole with no part singled out.**
- Self-interest compares them for the self, or for the part of the world that
  supports it.
- The comparison with no index compares them for the whole.

Philosophers call reasons of the second kind agent-neutral (Nagel 1986). The
thesis is that what we recognize as ethical tracks the comparison with no
index, and what we recognize as prudent tracks the indexed one. That is a
claim about our judgements, and it can be checked. An act we recognize as
domination should widen the dominator's futures while narrowing the whole.
Chapter 22 checks it.

Once the index is removed, only one answer is left. Seen from a part of the
world, two futures that differ only outside that part look the same. Physics
does not treat them as the same. The only standpoint that keeps every
distinction physics makes is the **whole field**, the ultimate frame. The
principle that fixes the measure in section 2 also fixes the subject: physics
decides what counts as the same.

A comparison does not need the whole universe in practice. Parts of the world
that an act cannot reach, and whose futures are independent of the parts it
can, add the same to every option and drop out. Parts it can reach are in,
whether or not the actor depends on them. And there is no ledger to keep.
Nothing has to be admitted, weighed or counted twice, because the joint
structure of the futures already accounts for what overlapping parts share.

> **Plain-English definition 4: The whole field**
>
> The whole world, with no part singled out. Parts that an act cannot affect,
> and that are independent of those it can, drop out of every comparison.

**Self-interest and ethics use the same measure. They differ by exactly one
term: what an act does to the breadth of everything else, given what it does
for the self.**

Where that term is small, or runs the same way, prudence and ethics agree.
The more intertwined the futures of the self and the rest, the more often they
do. Where it runs the other way, they come apart: an act can widen its actor's
futures while narrowing everyone else's by more. Chapter 22 measures such a
case.

> **Note: why expect ethics to track the whole?**
>
> The thesis says that ethical judgement tracks the comparison with no index.
> Chapter 16 gives three reasons to expect it to.
> - **The two comparisons mostly agree.** Futures are intertwined, so what
>   narrows the rest of the field usually narrows the self's futures too.
> - **The self has no privileged boundary.** Physics draws no line around a
>   self that it does not draw around any other part of the field. Any line
>   drawn around a self, or a group, is a choice.
> - **It is the standard others can use.** A measure applied to the whole lets
>   anyone assess anyone's acts, including acts that would remove them, and it
>   may license defence against such acts. Self-interest offers no such
>   standard.

### What persistence is worth, and what harm is

Two old puzzles sit here. If survival is valuable, should everything that
exists be preserved? If suffering is bad, is a world of suffering worse than
no world at all? Both puzzles assume that persistence and loss are quantities
of their own. They are not.

**Persistence is worth exactly the breadth it keeps going.**

In the measure, persistence has no value of its own. Each step a process lasts
is worth what it adds to the breadth of the futures, beyond whatever would
replace it (Appendix A, Proposition A.4.8). A process whose successor would be
broader narrows the whole by lasting. So the aim is to keep channels open, not
to preserve stock.

**What we recognize as harm narrows what would otherwise have stayed open.**

An act narrows the futures of the whole field when the futures that follow
from it are fewer, in the measure's sense, than those that would have followed
from the alternative. Physics describes the narrowing. The central thesis is
that what we recognize as harm corresponds to it.
- **Narrowing is a comparison, not a quantity below zero.** It is always
  relative to what else could have been done.
- **The largest narrowing leaves no alternatives.** No act can take away more
  than everything its alternative would have kept open, and nothing ranks
  below a future with no alternatives left.
- **The thesis predicts that suffering narrows futures** that the same world
  without it would keep open. That is the comparison that matters, not a
  comparison with no world at all. Chapter 19 puts the prediction to the test.

> **Plain-English definition 5: Narrowing**
>
> How far an act leaves the futures of the whole field narrower than its
> alternative would have. Widening is the reverse. The central thesis is that
> harm corresponds to narrowing, and benefit to widening.

### Why an early end costs: Alpha

Omega needs very little underneath it. Any world in which things follow one
another, and in which something can change, needs three ingredients:
- **distinction:** that something is this rather than that;
- **relation:** that one thing bears on another;
- **asymmetry:** a relation that runs one way and not the other, which gives
  before and after.

We call the three together **Alpha**. Each is needed by the ethics.
- Without distinction there are no alternatives to count, and nothing for a
  valuer to register.
- Without relation nothing follows from a present, and no end depends on any
  condition.
- Without asymmetry there is no before and after, and so no futures at all.

**Asymmetry gives a future. A frame gives a standpoint from which it is
defined.** Together they give a structure of continuations relative to
something, and lushness measures its breadth. That is the whole chain from
Alpha to Omega: asymmetry, frame, valuer, continuation, lushness.

Asymmetry does one thing more, and this is where Alpha enters the case for
Omega. In our world, causal order and the thermodynamic arrow run the same
way, and much of what an act removes is never restored. **How much an early
end costs depends on how readily the world restores what was removed.** For a
structure that adds more to the futures than whatever would replace it:
- If it is rebuilt, ending it early costs only what is lost while it is gone.
- If it can never be rebuilt, ending it costs everything it would have added,
  beyond what replaces it, over the rest of its life (Appendix A, Proposition
  A.4.9).

That is why persistence matters, and why acts that cannot be undone call for
the most care. The value of keeping a choice open is greatest when the
alternative is permanent and more will be learned later. Economists know this
as **option value** (Arrow and Fisher 1974; Henry 1974). Chapter 30 develops
Alpha in full.

> **Plain-English definition 6: Alpha**
>
> The three ingredients any world with change needs: distinction, relation and
> asymmetry.

> **Plain-English definition 7: Option value**
>
> The value of keeping a choice available. It is largest when the alternative
> cannot be undone and more will be learned later.

### From persistence to breadth

Why should what we recognize as good track breadth at all? Add one fact about
our position. No bounded agent knows where value is or will be realized,
because none can survey every process that values, or foresee which processes
will come to value. Under that uncertainty, protecting the persistence of
valuing means keeping open as many ways for it to continue as possible.
**Persistence is the floor. Uncertainty turns it into breadth.**

Precaution, option value, deference to better-informed others and keeping
mistakes correctable are this same uncertainty, applied in different places.
Part VI treats each.

> **The Central Thesis**
>
> What we recognize as good corresponds to the widening of the whole field's
> futures. What we recognize as harm corresponds to their narrowing.
>
> - **Good,** on this account, turns out to be organization that sustains and
>   compounds the conditions for further possibility.
> - **Bad** turns out to be securing one's own continuation by closing the
>   possibilities around it.
>
> The correspondence is not true by definition. Omega has to earn it, one class
> of cases at a time.

### Good and bad have a structural signature

**The difference between good and bad shows up in the structure of Omega.**

Ethics has long treated good and bad in one of two ways: as facts that no one
can locate, or as opinions that no one can settle. The central thesis makes
them neither.

The thesis turns the question whether an act is good into a question about
how it changes the structure of Omega. Are the futures that follow from it
broader or narrower than the futures that would have followed otherwise, for
the whole and not only for the actor? That is a question about the world, and
it has an answer whether or not anyone yet knows it.
- **Good.** An act that builds and sustains organization others can build on
  widens the futures of the whole.
- **Bad.** An act that secures its own continuation by closing others'
  possibilities narrows them, by more than it widens its own.

Organization, cooperation, protection and domination matter only through what
they do to the futures. None of them carries a value of its own.

Moral judgement, on this account, is our long-practised and fallible attempt
to read this structure. Where our judgements are right, they track it. Where
two judgements conflict, at most one of them tracks it, and the structure
settles which.

### The minimal foundation

> **Everything in this manuscript rests on one link and one thesis**
>
> *The starting point is standard physics: the world has a state now, and laws
> carry it forward with definite probabilities.*
>
> **The link.** Value requires valuers. Without something that values, nothing
> physical is good or bad. A valuer is anything with a frame of its own, and
> the whole world is the ultimate frame.
>
> **What follows from physics, with no further assumption** (Appendix A):
> 1. **Who counts.** Everything. The comparison with no part singled out is
>    the whole field's, because only it keeps every distinction physics makes.
> 2. **How to count.** Lushness, the only count of distinct futures that meets
>    four requirements (section 2).
> 3. **What persistence is worth.** The breadth it keeps going, beyond what
>    would replace it.
> 4. **What a loss is.** A comparison, not a quantity below zero: how far an
>    act narrows the futures, against what its alternative would have left
>    open. The largest loss leaves no alternatives at all.
>
> **The thesis.** What we recognize as good corresponds to the widening of
> the whole field's futures, and what we recognize as harm to their narrowing.
>
> What remains to be tested is not the count, the subject or the worth of
> persistence. It is the correspondence, one class of cases at a time.
>
> Everything else falls into one of four kinds:
> - derived from physics and the link (Appendix A);
> - demonstrated in exact physical models;
> - observed in the world;
> - for the central thesis itself, argued for and put to the test.

### The thesis must earn its place

**The thesis makes predictions, and this manuscript has to earn every one of
them.**

Most ethical theories cannot be tested. This one can, and it puts itself at
risk in three places. Each is a class of cases in which the correspondence
must hold.
- **Organization should open futures that disorder cannot reach.** Organized
  systems should reach, within the time available, futures that disordered
  systems never reach. Chapters 10 and 14 put this to the test.
- **Information should pay only through action.** Knowing more should widen
  the future only when the knowledge is used to act. Chapter 11 puts this to
  the test.
- **Blocking others should narrow the whole.** Constraint that blocks others'
  access should narrow the futures of the whole, even when it widens the
  futures of whoever imposes it. Chapter 22 puts this to the test.

Section 5 adds the hardest tests of all: the ones that could still overturn the
thesis.

## Section 2: Lushness

> **TL;DR**
>
> To compare futures, we need a way to say that one situation holds more
> possibility than another. Two problems have defeated earlier attempts, and we
> solve them in order.
> - **The grain problem.** Every count depends on what you treat as different.
>   Physics settles it: a declared resolution fixes which futures count as the
>   same, and we accept only verdicts that hold at every resolution the physics
>   allows.
> - **The weighting problem.** Counting options treats the almost certain and
>   the almost impossible alike. Once physics has settled what counts as
>   different, four requirements on any honest count leave exactly one measure.
>   Information theory and machine learning already know it as **perplexity**.
>
> **Physics decides what counts as the same. Probability decides how much there
> is.** Nothing else enters the count, which is why the measure is derived and
> not chosen.

### The grain problem

Every count of possibilities depends on what you treat as different. That
dependence has troubled every field that counts.
- **Freedom of choice.** Economists who rank freedom by counting options must
  decide whether two nearly identical options are one option or two, and that
  decision settles the verdict.
- **Physics.** The entropy of a gas depends on which microscopic differences
  are counted. Gibbs's paradox about mixing gases made the dependence
  famous (Jaynes 1992).
- **Artificial agents.** Measures of how many options an agent has depend on
  how the states of its world are represented.

The usual responses fail. One is to pick a grain and say nothing about it.
Another is to pick the grain that gives the answer one wants. Either way, the
choice of grain decides the verdict, and the verdict cannot be trusted.

Time raises the same problem in a second form. How often a system is observed
changes the count. Read a process often enough and every small event registers
as a new branch, so the most active process wins regardless of what its
activity achieves.

### Our resolution: physics decides what counts as the same

**Sameness is settled first, by the physics, and only then do we count.**

The resolution has four parts.
1. **Declare it.** Every comparison states its grain, meaning which states
   count as different, and its cadence, meaning how often the system is read.
2. **Let the physics set the admissible range.** A grain is admissible if it
   keeps the distinctions the physics of the case depends on: what acts on
   what, what is conserved, which alternatives are open, and which recoveries
   are possible. Which option it happens to favour plays no part. The cadence
   must be no finer than the fastest events that leave a record, and no
   coarser than is needed to resolve every kind of event the system produces.
3. **Claim only robust verdicts.** One future is ranked above another only if
   the ranking holds at every admissible resolution. A small difference that
   keeps its sign counts. A large difference that changes sign does not.
4. **Know the dependence exactly.** We have worked out how lushness depends on
   cadence. Finer reading raises it in proportion to the number of events a
   process produces (Theorem A.6.3). The dependence is therefore computed, not
   hidden, and we can say in advance where a ranking will flip.

Once the resolution is fixed, a present determines a definite set of
distinguishable futures, each with its probability. That pair, the futures and
their probabilities, is everything the measure looks at.

> **Plain-English definition 8: Resolution**
>
> The two choices every comparison of futures declares: the grain, meaning
> which states count as different, and the cadence, meaning how often the
> system is read.

> **Note**
>
> Lushness is not the entropy of heat. Thermodynamic entropy counts the
> microscopic arrangements consistent with a system's state at one moment.
> Lushness counts the futures a present supports over time, at a declared
> resolution. A gas at equilibrium, described by its pressure and temperature,
> has essentially one future. Described molecule by molecule, it has an
> astronomical number. That is the grain problem in its purest form, and the
> resolution above is how we handle it.

### The weighting problem

With sameness settled, the natural next step is to count the distinct futures.
Economists who rank freedom of choice count options. Pattanaik and Xu (1990)
showed that three plausible axioms force exactly that ranking, by the number
of options available.

Counting fails for futures, for a simple reason. Suppose a situation has a
million distinct futures, one of them almost certain and the rest vanishingly
unlikely. A count says it has a million futures. In every sense that matters,
it has one. Physics supplies more than a list of possibilities: it supplies how
likely each one is. A count that ignores the likelihoods throws away part of
what the physics says.

### Our resolution: the only honest count

**The measure is derived, not chosen. Four requirements on any honest count of
distinct futures leave exactly one measure.**
1. **It depends only on the futures and their likelihoods.** Renaming the
   futures changes nothing, and futures that cannot happen do not count.
2. **It uses the likelihoods fully and smoothly.** A small change in how likely
   the futures are makes only a small change in the count.
3. **It gives the same answer however the future is divided.** Assessing a
   branching future all at once must give the same answer as assessing it stage
   by stage, with each stage weighted by the chance of reaching it. Otherwise the
   verdict would depend on bookkeeping, and an actor deciding step by step would
   reverse its own judgements.
4. **It counts plainly.** A number of equally likely futures counts as that
   number.

Exactly one measure meets all four: the one Claude Shannon introduced in 1948
to quantify information (Shannon 1948; Faddeev 1956). The effective number it
yields already has a name: **perplexity**. Perplexity was introduced to measure
how hard a speech-recognition task is (Jelinek et al. 1977), and it is now the
standard score for language models. A model's perplexity is the number of
equally likely words it is, in effect, choosing among at each step.

**Lushness is the perplexity of a present's futures.** Appendix A states it and
proves it.

> **Plain-English definition 9: Perplexity**
>
> The effective number of equally likely alternatives that a set of
> probabilities amounts to. Over k equally likely outcomes, the perplexity is k.
> When probability is spread unevenly, it is less.

> **Plain-English definition 10: Lushness**
>
> The effective number of genuinely different futures a present supports. It
> equals the number of futures when they are equally likely, and falls toward
> one as one future comes to dominate.

### Why not weight futures by how alike they are?

Some measures of diversity go one step further. They count two similar
alternatives as partly the same (Leinster and Cobbold 2012). Should lushness?

No, and the reason is not taste. Either the similarity between two futures
makes a physical difference to what follows, or it does not.
- **If it does,** through different recoveries, capacities, interactions or
  consequences, then those differences already show up as different futures
  with different likelihoods at an admissible resolution. They need no second
  weight.
- **If it does not,** then weighting by it adds a separate criterion. It
  measures how diverse the futures look according to a chosen geometry, not how
  many futures the present supports.

**Physics decides what counts as the same. Probability decides how much there
is.** There is no third stage at which distinct futures are counted as partly
the same. If some morally crucial difference never shows up in what can follow,
lushness will miss it. The central thesis takes that risk openly. We do not
quietly reintroduce the difference through a weighting.

### Rate and offset

Lushness builds up through time, stage by stage, so a present can be compared
with another in two ways:
- by how fast its lushness grows, its **rate**;
- by how far ahead of the other it starts, its **offset**.

> **Plain-English definition 11: Rate**
>
> How quickly the lushness of a present grows as the future unfolds, in the
> long run.

> **Plain-English definition 12: Offset**
>
> A lead in lushness that one present keeps over another, however long you
> wait.

### Counting everything

**Lushness counts every future, random ones included. That is the point.**

Every other standard decides in advance which variations matter. Welfare
ignores whatever does not affect welfare. Preference ignores whatever no one
prefers. Each settles what matters before looking. Lushness refuses. The random
motion of molecules branches, and lushness counts the branching. What earns a
future its weight is not what kind of variation it is, but what can follow from
it.

That refusal is the measure's greatest strength, and also the source of the
strongest objection to it: that a measure which counts everything must prefer
noise to structure. Section 5 takes that objection on directly.

### What comes next

Lushness builds up through time, and a present can trade a head start for a
faster rate. When is a lasting edge worth more than a lead you can see now? The
answer depends on how long things last, and it decides more of ethics than one
might expect. Section 3 works it out.
