VALUERS AND VALUE-BEARING CONTINUATION
v2.5 checkpoint
Current through 21 September 2026

CURRENT STATUS

A valuer is a persistent process for which differences among possible continuations matter from within the organization of the process itself.

Valuerhood is not defined by external approval, raw complexity, causal power, or mere persistence.

The current structural target is a system that:

- persists as an organized process;
- differentiates among possible continuations;
- uses those differences in its own regulation, action, correction, reconstruction, or value contact;
- has endogenous stakes in what happens next.

A compact working definition is:

    an emergent valuer is a persistent pattern that endogenously
    differentiates among possible continuations and uses those
    distinctions to organize future-directed behavior.

Valuerhood is the bridge between neutral consequence and value-bearing consequence.


1. WHY VALUERS MATTER

Physical consequence is broader than value.

A hurricane can transform an ecosystem.

A dam can redirect a river.

A computer optimizer can restructure an economy.

These are large deformations of continuation.

But value enters only when there are systems for which differences among those continuations matter.

The program therefore treats value as valuer-relative without reducing it to arbitrary preference.

The question is:

    What physical organizations instantiate their own orientation
    among possible continuations?


2. THIN AND STRONG VALUER CRITERIA

A thin candidate is:

    a coherent bounded physical process whose interaction with
    its environment contributes to maintaining its organization.

This includes many self-maintaining systems and is probably too permissive.

A stronger criterion is:

    distinctions among reachable continuations affect the process's
    own organization, policy, correction, reconstruction, or value contact.

This adds endogenous future sensitivity.

The stronger condition is closer to the active target.


3. ORIENTATION LOCUS

An orientation locus is a persistent pattern whose internal distinctions systematically alter its own continuation.

Examples might include:

- a regulatory network;
- an adaptive organism;
- an autonomous agent;
- a distributed control organization.

Orientation alone does not settle valuerhood.

A thermostat distinguishes hot from cold in a control loop, but whether that is enough for genuine endogenous valuation is precisely the demarcation problem.

The project therefore studies a graded structural ladder rather than assuming a trivial threshold.


4. A STRUCTURAL LADDER

A useful hierarchy is:

    passive response
        -> regulation
        -> adaptive regulation
        -> model-mediated control
        -> future-sensitive policy
        -> self-modifying future orientation.

Valuerhood may emerge over a region in this space rather than at one sharp line.

Relevant dimensions include:

- persistence;
- feedback;
- temporal integration;
- policy flexibility;
- self-maintenance;
- future modeling;
- memory;
- learning;
- self-reference;
- correction;
- integration of competing priorities.


5. ENDOGENOUS STAKES

The phrase "matters to the system" needs a physical interpretation.

One candidate is that different future states alter the system's own:

- viability;
- regulatory state;
- policy;
- memory;
- reconstruction;
- expected continuation;
- error signal;
- learned model;
- access to reward or aversive structure.

This creates endogenous stakes.

The system is not merely pushed through different states by external dynamics.

Its own organization treats the differences asymmetrically.


6. VALUE CONTACT

Value contact is a deliberately broad term for a system's capacity to encounter, register, create, compare, or be affected by what matters within its own organization.

Possible realizations include:

- reward and punishment signals;
- homeostatic error;
- pain and pleasure;
- goal discrepancy;
- preference change;
- social evaluation;
- learned significance;
- anticipated loss.

The exact relation between value contact and consciousness remains open.

The term allows the structural problem to be posed before assuming one theory of phenomenology.


7. VALUERHOOD AND AGENCY

Agency and valuerhood overlap but are not identical.

Agency asks:

    Does internal policy steer continuation?

Valuerhood asks:

    Do distinctions among continuations matter to the organization itself?

A powerful automated controller can exhibit high agency and deformation breadth while having uncertain or absent valuerhood.

A conscious animal can plausibly be a valuer while possessing modest external causal power.

Thus:

    agent != valuer

as a type distinction, even when many organisms are both.


8. VALUERHOOD AND SELF-MAINTENANCE

Self-maintenance is strong evidence for endogenous orientation because the system acts to preserve conditions of its own continuation.

Examples include:

- metabolism;
- homeostasis;
- immune function;
- repair;
- resource acquisition;
- error correction.

But self-maintenance alone may still be too weak.

Simple biochemical systems can preserve themselves without rich future differentiation.

The current program therefore looks for a combination of maintenance, integration, adaptive control, and continuation-sensitive organization.


9. VALUERHOOD AND TEMPORAL DEPTH

A valuer is typically extended across time.

Its current state reflects:

- past records;
- present regulation;
- anticipated continuation.

Temporal depth matters because value-bearing organization often depends on delayed consequence.

Examples include:

- hunger driving future resource seeking;
- fear preparing for anticipated threat;
- planning trading present cost for future benefit;
- learning altering behavior based on remembered outcomes.

The longer and richer the temporal integration, the more clearly the system behaves as though alternatives matter to its continuing organization.


10. VALUERHOOD AND FLEXIBILITY

A fixed reflex can be continuation-sensitive without supporting much policy variation.

Flexible systems can:

- change strategies;
- learn new distinctions;
- update models;
- transfer learning across contexts;
- revise priorities.

Policy flexibility may therefore be an important dimension of valuerhood.

It is not yet assumed to be strictly necessary.

A deeply integrated but relatively fixed system could still instantiate genuine endogenous stakes.


11. CONSCIOUSNESS AS A POSSIBLE SUFFICIENT ROUTE

One active hypothesis is that phenomenal consciousness, if it emerges from integrated temporoperceptual future-oriented control, may be sufficient evidence of valuerhood.

The reasoning is structural.

A conscious controller appears to integrate:

- asynchronous sensory signals;
- bodily state;
- memory;
- affect;
- prediction;
- action selection.

Phenomenal valence is particularly suggestive because pain, hunger, fear, relief, attraction, and reward are already asymmetrically oriented toward continuation.

Thus a plausible conjecture is:

    consciousness
        -> integrated endogenous orientation
        -> valuerhood.

The converse is not assumed.

Nonconscious valuers may exist.


12. VALUERHOOD BEYOND CONSCIOUSNESS

Possible nonconscious valuer candidates include:

- bacteria with adaptive chemotaxis;
- distributed colonies;
- autonomous artificial systems;
- regulatory networks;
- self-maintaining organizations.

The criterion is structural rather than anthropomorphic.

A system qualifies only if its organization genuinely differentiates continuation in a way that guides its own future-directed behavior.


13. VALUERHOOD AND CAUSAL POWER

Standing is not proportional to causal influence.

A system can have:

    low causal power
    high confidence of valuerhood.

Another can have:

    enormous causal power
    low confidence of valuerhood.

Examples of the second class include:

- large infrastructure;
- powerful optimization systems;
- natural disasters.

These can still be enormously important because valuer-bearing continuation depends on them.

That importance is dependency-derived rather than evidence that the infrastructure itself is a valuer.


14. DEPENDENCY-DERIVED IMPORTANCE

Many non-valuers are essential to valuers.

Examples include:

- oxygen supply;
- water systems;
- power grids;
- food webs;
- critical software;
- habitats;
- institutions.

Their significance can be represented through their effect on valuer-bearing continuation.

If removing D collapses large regions of valuer continuation, then D has high derived importance.

This allows the framework to recognize vital infrastructure without assigning it intrinsic valuerhood.


15. VALUERHOOD DOES NOT FIX MORAL SIGN

A valuer can act destructively.

A conscious organism can reduce ecosystemic continuation.

A powerful society can foreclose other valuers.

A valuer can even undermine its own long-term continuation.

Therefore:

    valuerhood != positive contribution.

The sign and structure of an action must be evaluated separately.


16. INDIVIDUATION

A difficult question is how many valuers exist in one physical system.

Candidates include:

- one organism;
- multiple subsystems;
- a colony;
- a social group;
- a distributed AI;
- an institution.

Valuer individuation should depend on independent organization of continuation-sensitive stakes, not on labels or headcount alone.

This becomes especially difficult when systems are tightly integrated.


17. COPIES AND REDUNDANCY

Suppose several physically distinct systems instantiate highly similar value-bearing trajectories.

At the physical consequence level, some structure may be redundant.

At the phenomenal or standing level, numerical multiplicity may still matter.

This is an important open problem.

The framework therefore distinguishes:

- redundancy in consequence geometry;
- multiplicity of valuer loci;
- moral aggregation.

They should not be collapsed into one count.


18. DISTRIBUTED VALUERS

A distributed system may instantiate a higher-level valuer if continuation-sensitive organization exists at the collective level and is not reducible to any one component.

Possible candidates include:

- colonies;
- teams;
- firms;
- institutions;
- artificial multi-agent systems.

The burden is to identify:

- collective memory;
- collective policy;
- integrated error signals;
- persistence;
- reconstruction;
- future orientation.

Mere coordination is not enough.


19. UNCERTAIN VALUERHOOD

Real systems may be ambiguous.

Examples include:

- simple invertebrates;
- organoids;
- novel AI systems;
- damaged nervous systems;
- distributed computational systems.

The theory can represent epistemic uncertainty as

    p(V | E),

where E is available evidence.

This does not make valuerhood subjective.

It separates uncertainty about classification from the underlying physical fact.


20. GRADED OR PHASE-LIKE VALUERHOOD

Valuerhood may be:

- categorical;
- graded;
- vector-valued;
- phase-like.

A useful parameter space could include:

    theta_V =
        (persistence,
         endogenous feedback,
         temporal integration,
         future modeling,
         policy flexibility,
         self-maintenance,
         recursive adaptation,
         priority integration).

The valuer regime would then occupy a region

    Theta_V.

This is likely more realistic than forcing all systems into a single scalar threshold too early.


21. VALUE-BEARING CONTINUATION

Once a candidate valuer V is identified, its continuation can be analyzed as a valuer-indexed field.

Relevant variables include:

- current records;
- available actions;
- resource budget;
- repair possibilities;
- horizon;
- uncertainty;
- fact language;
- other interacting valuers.

The important object is not simply "the future."

It is the structure of continuation relative to a situated valuer and its endogenous stakes.


22. CONTINUATION GRADIENTS

A valuer induces an ordering or directional structure over some aspects of continuation.

This can be represented schematically by a gradient-like object:

    g_V

indicating how changes in state or policy alter conditions relevant to the valuer's own organization.

The term gradient is broad.

In some models it may be:

- a utility gradient;
- a viability gradient;
- a preference order;
- a partial order;
- an error signal;
- a vector field;
- a set of constraints.

No universal scalar utility is assumed.


23. RECOVERY

For a valuer, damage is not exhausted by immediate state change.

A key question is whether lost conditions can be restored.

Recovery depends on:

- allowed repairs;
- resources;
- time;
- information;
- remaining control channels.

The difference between temporary impairment and nonrecoverable loss is central to later ethical analysis.


24. VALUE AND LUSHNESS

Lushness describes the richness of consequential possibility.

Valuerhood identifies loci for which continuation differences matter.

These are related but distinct.

A physically lush universe with no valuers would contain differentiated possibility but no instantiated value-bearing perspective.

A universe with valuers but extremely narrow continuation may contain value with little room for development, correction, or diversification.

The broader program studies how valuer-bearing continuation can remain open, generative, compatible, and recoverable.


25. CURRENT RESEARCH TARGET

The immediate valuer problem is empirical and formal:

    Which physical organizations instantiate endogenous continuation-sensitive stakes?

A mature theory should classify systems without relying on:

- species labels;
- human resemblance;
- raw complexity;
- causal power;
- declared goals alone.

The next step is multi-valuer structure: what happens when several valuers occupy overlapping continuation and their requirements interact.
