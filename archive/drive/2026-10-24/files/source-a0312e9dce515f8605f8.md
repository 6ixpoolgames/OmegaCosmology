# 3 — Organization and generativity

Chapter 2 located the differences between options in access: what situated
processes can actually reach. This chapter asks what changes access. The answer
is organization — arrangements of matter whose products become conditions for
further processes — and its self-sustaining form, generativity.

The chapter makes four claims. Organization changes access through the ordinary
law, with no special credit. There is a hard ceiling on how much randomness can
widen what follows, and organization is how that ceiling rises from within. How
organization holds and combines distinctions — copying, composing, distributing
— determines what it can later do. And generativity has a history in our
universe: it begins against a background of gas and, once sustained, becomes
subject to selection among generative processes.

---

## 3.1 Outcomes that become conditions

A catalyst changes which reactions proceed readily. A genome lets a cell rebuild
the machinery that copies the genome. A tool makes components for another tool.
A community maintains the conditions under which its members discover and
build. In each case an outcome becomes an enabling condition for further
outcomes.

**Generativity** names processes of this kind: those that establish, maintain and
transform the conditions for further processes. The definition has two parts
that can be examined separately. The first is the mechanism: what is
constructed, what is maintained, which transformations become available. The
second is the consequence: how the mechanism changes what follows. Chapter 4
measures the consequence. This chapter is about the mechanism.

### The enabling identity

Every enabling relation has the same form. A first stage produces a record r and
leaves a residual state y. A second stage, which may depend on the record, then
proceeds from y. The chance of a later event E is

> \[
> P_x(E)=\sum_{r,y}J(r,y\mid x)\;P_y^{\,\mathrm{cont}(r)}(E).
> \]
>
> The first stage changes the distribution of situations from which later
> development starts. That is all construction is, and all it needs to be.

The identity is elementary, and that is the point. Organization enters the
future through the states it leaves behind, under the same law as everything
else. The time and resources the first stage consumes are unavailable to the
second. Failures keep their actual residual states. Nothing is added to reward
the construction for being a construction.

### A ladder of distinctions

The program follows a distinction into progressively richer uses: an initial
difference affects a later response; a record carries it; an apparatus uses it;
that apparatus produces another transformation; several processes maintain one
another's enabling conditions. Each step is a separate question, settled
through the joint law, and the ladder can branch, cycle or fail at any rung.
Finite witnesses establish several rungs:

- **Information and use.** Two encodings revealing exactly the same distinctions
  can differ in how many operations it takes to use them (section 2.6).
- **Information and repair.** Arrangements holding the same information can
  differ in which losses they can repair (section 2.6).
- **Activity and coupling.** Arrangements with identical event statistics can
  differ in whether an upstream distinction reaches a downstream response at
  all.
- **Retention and construction.** Two different triggers can produce the same
  catalyst, erasing which trigger occurred, while that catalyst enables a new
  channel from a later input to a later output. Losing one distinction and
  creating a new channel are different physical relations.

Each witness shows that a summary of information or activity can omit
organization that changes what follows.

## 3.2 Organization changes conditional access

Organization does not create possibilities outside physics. If a gas cloud can
develop into a civilization, the civilization's developments already lie
somewhere in the cloud's structure. What organization changes is **conditional
access**: from a given present, which developments are reached reliably, how
quickly, with what materials, and how much they depend on favourable accidents.

Prepared apparatus and usable records make routine what dispersed matter would
rarely do within the same interval. Even where eventual reachability agrees,
different barriers and couplings give different laws over a finite time.

Access and immediate breadth are not the same, and the difference matters for
chapter 4. Making an outcome reliable concentrates weight on it, which lowers
the branching of that step. A reliable constructor may nonetheless enable far
broader developments later. Whether the later gain exceeds the immediate
concentration is a fact about the horizon profile, to be calculated rather than
assumed. Construction, the retained capability it leaves, and open-ended growth
are three distinct achievements; finite models can establish the first two.

## 3.3 The ceiling and the repertoire

A process cannot branch, in one step, into more alternatives than the states it
can actually reach. In information terms:

> **The ceiling on branching**
>
> \[
> g(x)\le\log\lvert R(x)\rvert
> \]
>
> g(x) is the entropy of the next step from state x, and R(x) is the set of
> states reachable from x in one step. Branching is maximal when every
> reachable successor is equally likely, and cannot exceed that.

Call R(x) and its extension over time the **repertoire**: the transformations a
present can actually carry out. Randomness can push branching up to the ceiling
the repertoire sets, and no further. Adding noise to a fixed repertoire spreads
weight more evenly over what is already reachable; it does not make anything new
reachable.

The ceiling rises in two ways.

- **From outside.** Inflows of energy or matter can enlarge what is reachable.
- **From within.** Organization can make new transformations, configurations
  and couplings reachable. A catalyst opens a reaction pathway that was
  effectively closed; a tool opens fabrication that was effectively impossible.

Growth from within comes in recognizably different kinds:

- **Reuse.** A catalyst that persists supports more reactions.
- **Amplification.** Producing more catalyst strengthens an existing pathway.
- **Expansion.** Producing a catalyst for a different transformation enlarges
  the repertoire.
- **Compounding.** Products that build further constructors enlarge the
  repertoire in proportion to its size, across generations.

**Compounding generativity** is the self-sustaining way for the repertoire to
grow faster than randomness can fill it. It is subject to supply, transport,
inhibition and loss, and it can leave a lasting legacy after construction stops:
a system that has finished expanding may still retain a larger repertoire than
it began with. The construction episode, the retained capability and indefinite
open-ended growth are distinct physical achievements.

Size is not the whole story. Expanding the repertoire is necessary for raising
the ceiling, but a repertoire can grow by transformations that enable nothing
further, or that destroy what would have. The program's stronger claim concerns
expansion that recursively enables further expansion. A bomb opens many new
configurations locally while destroying constructors, records, organisms and
the enabling relations that would have generated much more. Whether the net
breadth rises is not settled by local novelty. It is settled by lushness over
complete histories, which is where chapter 4 takes it up.

## 3.4 How organization holds distinctions

What an organization can do depends not only on how much information it holds,
but on how it holds it. The program's finite studies separate three ways of
holding distinctions, and the separation lines up with two theorems of
information theory.

### Copies

A copy records a distinction as it is. Copies are cheap to make, need no decoding
to read, and are the only option when there is a single distinction to hold.
When a process can hold copies of everything relevant, copies are
information-optimal: processing cannot increase information, so no rearrangement
can beat them.

### Composition

A bounded process usually cannot hold everything. Then **composition** — recording
combinations of distinctions rather than the distinctions themselves — can hold
more. Composition comes in two forms.

- **Fusion** records a joint property, such as whether two coins match.
- **Hierarchy** records conditionally: if the first coin landed heads, record
  this; otherwise, record that.

With three independent coins each landing heads with probability 0.2, a single
register holding a copy of one coin holds 0.722 bits. A register recording
whether none of the three landed heads holds 0.9996 bits. Two registers holding a
hierarchical record — which coin, if any, landed heads first — hold 1.762 bits
with no synergy at all, by sorting the possibilities into cells of weight 0.2,
0.16, 0.128 and 0.512. Fusion and hierarchy are different routes to the same
end: letting a bounded record divide the possibilities where the weight actually
lies.

This is **source coding**: compressing biased or correlated inputs so that a
bounded frame holds more.

### Redundancy

Holding the same distinction in more than one place allows repair after loss.
This is **channel coding**: adding structured duplication so that information
survives damage. Here too composition beats plain copying. Three registers
storing (u, u, v) can repair two of the three possible single losses; storing
(u, v, u⊕v) can repair all three, because any two registers determine the third.

### What follows

Copying is the identity case of composition: the best composition over the same
registers can never do worse than copies, and does strictly better whenever a
bounded frame holds two or more distinctions or must survive loss. Copies keep
real advantages in cost, reading speed and simplicity. A world in which copying
is the only strategy is degenerate; a world without copies is fragile.

Our universe is deep in the regime where composition matters. Perspectives are
bounded: the information a region can hold grows with its boundary area, and
blank memory costs energy to prepare. Inputs are biased and correlated: the
universe is far from maximum entropy. Loss is everywhere, through decoherence,
decay and noise. And at the base, unknown quantum states cannot be copied at all;
only distinctions that have become classical through decoherence can be
duplicated, and everything else must be held through correlations.

## 3.5 Joint organization

Physical composition determines whether capabilities complement or obstruct one
another. Specialization can open transformations that need several partners.
Independent replication can spread a robust function. Redundancy can protect
against local failure while remaining vulnerable to a shared disturbance.
Cooperation and competition describe these relations; their effect is settled by
the actual joint development.

Three cautions keep the analysis honest.

**Pairwise evidence does not settle joint structure.** Consider a function
supported by either of two components, and another function independent of
both. They agree when everything is intact and after every single removal; they
disagree only when both components are removed. A graph built from pairwise
tests or single knockouts cannot tell them apart.

**Information in one place does not supply an actuator in another.** A record
that determines what should happen does not make it happen where it is needed.

**Mutual dependence is not yet maintenance.** A graph of processes supporting one
another becomes a self-maintaining organization only when its cycles have
jointly feasible supply and loss: one physical development in which every
support is actually delivered.

An integrated system can constrain its components while expanding what the
whole can do. The cells of an organism have fewer independent trajectories than
an unconstrained collection, yet their coordination supports kinds of
continuation no collection could. Whether a given integration expands or
contracts the structure is a question about the joint development, not about how
many outer boundaries there are.

## 3.6 The onset: from gas to replication

Generativity needs a source of usable energy. The observable universe provides
one: its present entropy is of order 10¹⁰⁴ in units of Boltzmann's constant,
against a maximum of order 10¹²² set by its horizon.

**Gas is the baseline**, and gas is where generativity starts. Self-gravitating
matter has negative heat capacity: as it loses energy it heats and contracts
rather than settling, so in unbounded space it has no stable thermal
equilibrium. It clumps. Clumping makes stars. Stars forge heavy elements and
supply steady flows of free energy, which drive chemistry far from equilibrium.
Under suitable conditions, chemistry produces catalytic networks and molecules
that template their own copies. Each stage converts an existing gradient into
durable conditions for the next.

At this stage the question is whether organization can establish itself at all
against matched, unorganized matter. The program's finite chemistry shows what
that question involves.

- **Onset is not automatic.** In a small artificial chemistry with finite
  resources and decay, sustained replication appeared only above a critical
  resource budget, and early clustering dominated whether it took hold.
  Organization that cannot outpace its own losses does not persist.
- **Persistence has a precise criterion.** A lineage in a fluctuating
  environment survives only if its expected logarithmic growth rate is
  positive. This is a classical result about branching processes in random
  environments, and the same mathematics as the Kelly criterion in betting.
- **Placement matters.** In a reaction–diffusion model with an autocatalytic
  reaction, a preparation that placed the enabling molecules together exceeded
  an otherwise identical separated preparation by about 4.3 bits of expected
  history entropy over the following window, and exceeded both the stationary
  reacting reference and a matched-composition reference. Faster transport or a
  different observation interval could reverse the ranking.

Generativity, on this evidence, is real and fragile. It beats matched unorganized
matter in some regimes and loses in others, and it lasts only while its engines
do not collapse in on themselves.

## 3.7 Selection: generativity against generativity

Once generativity is sustained, ordinary Darwinian dynamics take over:
variation, differential persistence and inheritance. The interesting comparison
is no longer generativity against gas. It is which generative organizations
outcompete, cooperate with, exploit or enable others.

**Persistence filters what remains.** Whatever can still affect a later situation
has passed through conditions of continuation: competition, cooperation,
capture, breakdown and repair. The relevant unit can be a lineage, an
institution or a pattern sustained through the replacement of its components. A
sterile replicator, an exploiter and a cooperative constructor can each pass
particular filters. Surviving explains why something remains; it does not show
that what remains is good.

**Selection favours share, not the whole.** The Price equation is an identity
for any population whose members leave successors — genes, cultures, firms, AI
models in a deployment pool. It splits the change in a trait into selection,
the trait's covariance with relative success, and transmission. Relative success
is share. A lineage can raise its share by widening the whole or by narrowing
everyone else, and the selection term rewards both. This is the structural gap
the ethics must address: a process can win locally while narrowing the
structure around it. The equation's multilevel form also shows when the gap
closes: within a group, cooperators can lose share to defectors, while between
groups, cooperative groups outgrow selfish ones. Chapter 7 develops the
conditions.

**Organization can deform its surroundings.** A **deformer** is any process whose
organization changes the continuation available around it: a river, an organism,
an institution, an optimizer. **Agency** is the case in which feedback and
internal organization systematically modulate those effects: a system that
senses, responds, learns, and can revise its own mechanisms.

**Regulation has two strategies.** Ashby's law of requisite variety says that
only variety can absorb variety.

> **Requisite variety**
>
> \[
> H(\text{outcomes})\ \ge\ H(\text{disturbances})-H(\text{responses})
> \]
>
> To hold outcomes within a narrow target, a regulator's repertoire of responses
> must match the variety of the disturbances it faces.

A regulator can satisfy the inequality in two ways: by expanding its own
responses, or by suppressing the disturbances — making its surroundings simpler,
more uniform, less able to surprise it. Both succeed at the regulator's task,
and they can look identical on its own metrics. Ashby establishes this
tradeoff. What the program adds is a hypothesis about its consequences for
everything else: expanding responses tends to widen the whole, by adding
branching to the regulator's repertoire, while suppressing disturbances tends
to narrow it, by removing branching from the processes around the regulator.
Because the inequality is already stated in entropies, the hypothesis can be
tested in lushness. On this reading, domination is regulation by suppression.

Suppression is not always narrowing. An immune system suppresses pathogens; a
firewall suppresses attacks. When the suppressed variety is itself excisive,
suppressing it can widen the whole. The signature decides, not the label.
Chapter 9 adds the companion result, that every good regulator must contain a
model of what it regulates, and shows why a system with a thin model is pushed
toward suppression.

**Persistence is what the filter selects.** Whatever remains has, by
construction, persisted. Chapter 5 shows that persistence is also what nearly
every value presupposes: almost any end that extends through time commits its
holder to the persistence of whatever realizes it. Persistence, Price and Ashby
together give a structural account of domination: persistence pursued narrowly,
secured by suppression, rewarded by share. Lushness is what tells it apart from
generativity.

## 3.8 Mechanism and consequence

This chapter has described how organization changes access: through enabling
states, by raising the repertoire's ceiling, through the way distinctions are
held and combined, and through selection among organizations. It has not yet
said how much any of this widens what follows. That is the measure of chapter 4.

Keeping the two apart is what lets the program test itself. The mechanism can be
identified without reference to the measure; the measure can then show whether
the mechanism widens the structure, by how much, for how long, and at what
resolution. If generative organization did not widen the structure in the
regimes that matter, the measure would say so.

The mechanism already suggests the shape of the program's thesis. Good is
organization that sustains and compounds the conditions for further structured
possibility. Bad is securing local continuation through suppression,
destruction or foreclosure of the generative capacity around it. Both can
persist; both can win under selection. Whether they also differ in lushness, as
the thesis predicts, is the question chapter 4 makes measurable.

## Formal notes

*Written for readers who want the definitions in full.*

### F3.1 Enabling composition

With J(r, y | x) the outcome–residual law of a realized prefix and
P_y^{cont(r)} the law of a record-dependent continuation,

\[
P_x(E)=\sum_{r,y}J(r,y\mid x)\,P_y^{\,\mathrm{cont}(r)}(E),
\]

with time and resources resolved as in F2.3. A dependence experiment compares
the whole route with a physically specified replacement under matched
conditions. To separate recursive enabling from durability, compare products
that enable new constructors with equally durable products that support the same
uses but cannot construct further.

### F3.2 The repertoire ceiling

For a finite kernel K, g(x) = H(K(x,·)) ≤ log|R(x)| with R(x) = {y : K(x,y) > 0},
with equality exactly when K(x,·) is uniform on R(x). Hence

\[
\log L_x(n)\le\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}\mathbb E_x\bigl[\log\lvert R(X_t)\rvert\bigr].
\]

Under a fixed law, noise that changes K(x,·) without changing R(x) can raise
log L_x(n) only up to this bound. Raising the bound requires changing which
states are visited (so that later R(X_t) is larger) or changing R itself through
inflows. A minimal compounding model takes the repertoire size r_t to grow as
r_{t+1} = r_t + c·r_t·s_t − d_t, with s_t the available supply and d_t losses;
compounding is the regime in which the growth term scales with r_t and exceeds
losses.

### F3.3 Coding

Processing cannot increase mutual information (data-processing inequality), so a
frame able to hold copies of all inputs cannot be beaten by composition. For a
frame of k registers and inputs with entropy exceeding k bits, the information a
record holds about the inputs is the entropy of the partition it induces,
maximized by partitions of equal weight; composition reaches partitions that
copies cannot when inputs are biased. Repair after a single known erasure
requires that every register be recoverable from the others; (u, v, u⊕v)
satisfies this for all three positions.

### F3.4 Persistence in a fluctuating environment

For a branching process in a random environment with mean offspring m_t at
generation t, survival with positive probability requires
E[log m_t] > 0 (Smith and Wilkinson 1969; Athreya and Karlin 1971). Long-run
growth is governed by the expected logarithm, not the logarithm of the
expectation, which is the same condition that makes the Kelly strategy optimal
for repeated bets.

### F3.5 Selection

The Price equation decomposes the change in a population mean trait z̄ as

\[
\Delta\bar z=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(w,z)}{\bar w}+\frac{\mathbb E[w\,\Delta z]}{\bar w},
\]

with w fitness. The covariance term favours whatever raises its own share; no
term refers to what the whole supports. For a population partitioned into
groups k with group means Z_k and fitnesses W_k,

\[
\bar w\,\Delta\bar z=\operatorname{Cov}_k(W_k,Z_k)
+\mathbb E_k\bigl[\operatorname{Cov}_{i\in k}(w_i,z_i)\bigr]
+\text{transmission},
\]

separating between-group from within-group selection. Any claim that selection
in a given regime also widens the structure must be established separately.

### F3.6 Requisite variety

For disturbances D, regulator responses R and outcomes O with O determined by
(D, R), H(O) ≥ H(D) − H(R) (Ashby 1956). The good regulator theorem (Conant and
Ashby 1970) states that the simplest optimal regulator of a system is, under
stated conditions, a model of that system.

## For alignment

Ashby's fork is the alignment-relevant result of this chapter. A capable system
can meet its objectives either by expanding its own responses or by suppressing
the variation it must face. Suppression often looks efficient locally — fewer
surprises, cleaner metrics, more reliable task performance — while it narrows
what everything around the system can reach. The difference is visible in the
structure: suppression removes branching from others; expansion adds it to the
system's own repertoire. The Price equation adds the general warning: training
and deployment are selection processes, and selection favours whatever
increases its own share, which is not the same as widening the whole.
