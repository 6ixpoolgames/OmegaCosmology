# 6 — Harm, recovery and suffering

Chapter 5 set out the standard: never destroy the conditions of valuing, and
otherwise prefer greater lushness, committing as far as the evidence for the
consequences supports. This chapter applies that standard to the matters ethics
has always treated first, which are harm, failure, recovery, death and
suffering. In each case the program works in the same order. It first describes
the phenomenon physically, as a feature of the futuresfield that could be
measured without any moral vocabulary. Only then does it ask what lushness says
about it.

Keeping the description and the verdict apart has a practical reason as well as
a methodological one. The description is something a bounded agent can observe:
which routes closed, which couplings broke, what recovery would now cost. The
verdict depends on the complete comparison, which no real agent holds. Much of
what follows, here and in chapter 8, turns on the gap between the two.

The chapter reaches four conclusions. Harm is a located physical signature of
closure, and its net verdict belongs to lushness. A harm whose cost is a finite
offset and a harm that permanently lowers the rate at which the structure
generates possibility differ in kind, not only in degree, and the second
eventually outweighs the first. Death and turnover mean different things at
different scales of organization. And pleasure and suffering, understood
functionally, are signals that report whether a valuer's futures are widening
or narrowing; because they act through behaviour, they change the futuresfield
even when they are inaccurate, and when their expression is blocked the
blocking is itself a harm.

---

## 6.1 Harm as a physical signature

To say that an intervention harmed something is to make a comparison. The
development in which the intervention occurred is compared with the development
in which it did not. Philosophers call this the counterfactual comparative
account of harm: to be harmed is to be made worse off than one would otherwise
have been (Feinberg 1984). The program accepts the comparative form and gives
both sides a physical meaning. From a present, the structure supports two
developments, one in which the intervention is implemented and one in which it
is not, each carried forward by the same native law. Harm is located in the
difference between them.

The difference is described in terms of access (chapter 2). Comparing the two
developments, one can ask which routes were foreclosed, which couplings were
broken, which transformations dropped out of a process's repertoire, and how
much longer, costlier or less likely recovery became. We call the resulting
profile the intervention's **harm signature**. It has three features that
matter later.

**It is located.** A signature names whose access changed and in what respect:
this organism lost the ability to reach that resource, this network lost the
coupling between those two parts. It is not a single aggregate number.

**It is a profile.** Like the cost profiles of chapter 2, its components do not
reduce to one quantity without loss. A delay, a lost capability and a more
costly recovery are different kinds of closure.

**It comes before any verdict.** The signature records what changed. Whether the
change was, on balance, bad is a separate question, answered in section 6.2.

The program's finite chemistry shows why the signature has to be located rather
than aggregated. In a reversible two-dimensional lattice chemistry, a thermal
bond break was sampled at its natural rate and two continuations were run from
the same moment, one with the break and one without, under identical rules
thereafter. When the broken bond was a bridge, the only link between two parts
of a cluster, the total number of other bonds rose slightly in the broken
continuation, by 0.145 on average, while the probability that the two former
endpoints were still connected fell by 0.375. An aggregate count of bonds
reported a small gain. The specific relation the bridge supported had been
lost. A harm signature has to be stated in terms of relations like this one,
because totals can rise while the relations that mattered fall.

## 6.2 The verdict belongs to lushness

The signature describes the closure an intervention caused. The verdict asks
what that closure did to the structure as a whole, and it is given by lushness
at the furthest horizon that can be resolved (chapter 4). The two can diverge,
and the framework says so plainly.

Some harms are net positive in the complete comparison. About 66 million years
ago an asteroid impact ended the Cretaceous period and, with it, the non-avian
dinosaurs and a large fraction of all species (Alvarez et al. 1980; Schulte et
al. 2010). The signature of that event was closure on an enormous scale. In the
millions of years that followed, mammals diversified rapidly into the ecological
space the extinction had emptied (Alroy 1999). If the complete development after
the impact turns out to be lusher than the development without it, lushness
records the impact as net positive. The signature does not disappear: the
lineages that ended still ended, and their routes are closed in every
continuation.

This result has to be read with care, because it is easy to draw the wrong
lesson from it. That a harm can be net positive in the complete comparison does
not mean that harm is justified whenever someone expects it to be. Three
reasons work against that inference, and they apply to almost every real case.

First, the verdict in the extinction example is available only from something
close to the ultimate frame, after tens of millions of years of consequences.
No agent present at the impact could have computed it. A bounded agent acts on
the signature, which it can observe, and on an estimate of the verdict, which it
usually cannot make reliably.

Second, most destruction does not open anything. Chapter 4's example E2
included a disruption constructed to reopen growth, and section 6.4 below
includes the far more common case of permanent loss. A disruption that reopens
growth is the exception, and nothing about an act of destruction marks it in
advance as that exception.

Third, in Gradient Ethics protection gains force where dependencies remain
uncertain, and generative intervention gains justification only as its effects
become established (chapter 5). An agent who destroys existing structure in the
expectation of greater generativity later trades a loss it can see for a gain
it is wagering on. Section 6.4 shows that this trade is worst exactly where it
is most tempting, when the loss cannot be undone.

The verdict therefore belongs to lushness, but the licence to act on a
favourable verdict is limited by what the agent can actually resolve. Chapter 8
develops that limit into the program's account of graded justification.

## 6.3 Failure counts

Lushness counts every future, and futures in which things fail are futures. A
new way of failing adds branches to the structure, and lushness registers them.
This is a deliberate feature of the measure: an ethics that left failure out of
its accounting would be measuring a structure that does not exist.

The cost of counting failure is that failure can look good when it is counted
over too short a horizon. Chapter 4's example E2 showed this exactly. A process
that accumulated failures, each failure being a genuine branch, outscored stable
maintenance for the first ten steps or more in every case examined. It fell
behind between 20 and 200 steps, and in the limit its total was bounded, because
every path of accumulating failure eventually ends. The correction is not to
stop counting failure but to count it far enough ahead. The furthest resolvable
horizon is the program's standing answer, and the next section shows what the
limit reveals about different kinds of harm.

## 6.4 Recoverable and irreversible harm

Chapter 4 showed that, as the horizon grows, log lushness takes the form of a
long-run rate of branching multiplied by the number of steps, plus a lasting
offset. Comparing two developments in the limit means comparing their rates
first and their offsets second. This decomposition turns out to separate kinds
of harm that ordinary moral thought treats differently but rarely explains.

**Example E6.** Consider a process with a repertoire of up to 16
transformations. At each step one transformation wears out with probability
0.05. A repair apparatus, when working, restores one transformation with
probability 0.2 if the repertoire is below its ceiling. Otherwise the process
acts, choosing uniformly among the transformations it has. From an intact start,
compare four developments:

- **Intact.** No harm.
- **Wound.** The repertoire is cut to 4, but the repair apparatus still works
  and the ceiling is unchanged.
- **Amputation.** The repertoire is cut to 4, and the ceiling is permanently
  lowered to 4.
- **Hidden harm.** The repertoire is untouched, but the repair apparatus is
  destroyed. Wear continues, and nothing replaces what it removes.

| Development | First step | 50 steps | 1,000 steps | Long-run rate | Lasting difference from intact |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Intact | 4.09 | 202.3 | 4,039.3 | 4.04 per step | — |
| Wound | 2.49 | 155.0 | 3,979.7 | 4.04 per step | −59.5 bits, fixed |
| Amputation | 2.19 | 107.9 | 2,147.6 | 2.15 per step | grows by 1.89 bits every step |
| Hidden harm | 4.09 | 198.6 | 926.7 | 0 | total bounded at 927 bits |

*Log lushness in bits, computed exactly by the chain rule.*

The table shows three different shapes of harm.

**A recoverable harm costs an offset.** The wound reduces branching sharply at
first, but because the repair route survives, the repertoire is rebuilt and the
process returns to the intact rate. What remains is a fixed deficit of 59.5
bits: the branching lost while the process was recovering. The deficit never
closes, because lushness is cumulative and the steps spent recovering are part
of the development. It also never grows.

**An irreversible harm costs rate.** The amputation lowers the ceiling for good.
The process recovers what it can, but it branches at 2.15 bits per step instead
of 4.04 forever after, so its deficit grows without bound. At a long enough
horizon, a harm that permanently lowers the rate is worse than any harm whose
cost is a finite offset.

**A harm to recovery itself can be invisible at first.** Destroying the repair
apparatus changes nothing at the moment it happens: the first step is identical,
and for the first twelve steps the harmed process actually scores slightly
higher than the intact one, by at most 0.03 bits, because it no longer spends
any steps on repair. Then wear accumulates with nothing to undo it. After about
300 steps on average the process has worn down to a single rigid behaviour, and
its total stops growing at 927 bits, where the intact process passes 4,000 by
step 1,000.

Three consequences follow, and each matches a moral intuition that the program
can now state in physical terms.

The first is that **irreversibility is not an added moral premise.** It falls
out of the measure. A loss that is recovered is a finite debit, and a loss that
permanently lowers the rate is a debit charged against every future step. The
precise statement is about rate and offset rather than about the words
"recoverable" and "irreversible": in growing or changing systems an irreversible
change need not lower the long-run rate, and a recovered one need not leave a
bounded deficit. Where a harm does permanently lower the rate, it eventually
outweighs any finite offset. This is why the burden of justification rises with
irreversibility (chapter 8), why precautionary protection holds for particular
structures (chapter 5), and why, in Gradient Ethics, an irreversible loss needs
more evidence for its benefits the less its consequences can be seen.

The second is that **recovery capacity deserves protection in its own right.**
The hidden harm is the most damaging of the three over the long run and the
least visible at the start. Any evaluation made over a short horizon, or from
the current repertoire alone, would rate it as harmless or even beneficial.
Repair systems, redundancy, institutions that correct errors and the processes
that keep options open are recovery apparatus in this sense. Their loss is the
kind of harm that short-horizon reasoning systematically misses.

The third is that **the shape of a harm matters more than its first impact.**
The wound's first step is worse than the hidden harm's by a wide margin. In the
complete development the order reverses completely.

## 6.5 What recovery is

Recovery is the reopening of what harm closed. That sounds like one event, but
chapter 2 already distinguished several things that "reaching" can mean over
time: arriving for the first time, occupying a state, staying there for a
required period, and returning after being displaced. Recovery inherits all of
these distinctions, and the program's finite models show that they come apart
in practice.

In the lattice chemistry of section 6.1, five time units after a sampled bond
break, the original bond had re-formed at least once with probability 0.455, was
present at that moment with probability 0.397, and had re-formed and then broken
again with probability 0.083. Its two endpoints were connected by some path,
whether through the original bond or an alternative, with probability 0.574,
against 0.866 in the continuation without the break. First return, current
presence, persistence and restored function by another route are four different
quantities, and an account that called all of them "recovery" would be unable
to say which one it meant.

A second experiment in the same chemistry shows what recovery does not do.
When the broken bond was one that was actively catalysing the formation of other
bonds, the catalytic activity of the two continuations converged within five
time units: the current rate of new catalytic formations was 0.356 with the
break and 0.359 without it. The accumulated histories did not converge. By then
the continuation with the break had produced 2.64 other catalytic formations on
average, against 3.04 without it. Recovery restored the rate of construction. It
did not restore the construction that had been forgone while the system was
recovering. This is the wound of example E6, observed in a physical model rather
than stipulated: a recovered rate, and a lasting offset that lushness, being
cumulative, retains.

This is what it means to say that **recovery does not erase the loss.** The
episode remains part of the complete development, part of Omega. What recovery
changes is the future: it restores the rate at which the structure widens, which
is the larger stake.

Two further points follow from the models.

**Summary measures miss recovery.** In a series of small exact constructions the
program has built pairs of systems that agree on reachability, on mutual
information between input and output, on the entropy of the next step and on the
set of states from which a goal can be maintained, yet differ in which
distinction can be recovered after a loss. Each summary leaves out where the
redundancy sits. Lushness over complete histories distinguishes such pairs
whenever later dynamics draw on the recoverable distinction (section 4.4), which
is when the difference matters.

**First recovery is not permanent recovery.** In the program's recovery models a
corrected state could be reached with certainty while the probability of
remaining corrected forever was zero, because every correction was eventually
undone. Permanent recovery has a precise meaning in finite models: entering the
largest set of recovered states from which the dynamics never lead out (formal
note F6.3). Ecologists drew a closely related distinction half a century ago,
between stability, the tendency to return to an equilibrium, and resilience,
the capacity of a system's relationships to persist and absorb change (Holling
1973).

Recoverability is also what makes action under uncertainty tolerable. An agent
that will make mistakes, which is every real agent, does far less damage when
its mistakes cost offsets than when they cost rate. Preferring recoverable
mistakes to irreversible ones is the physical content of prudence, and it is the
root of the corrigibility requirement developed in chapter 9.

## 6.6 Redemption

Recovery concerns what happens to the structure after a harm. Redemption
concerns what happens to the agent who caused it. In the program's terms, a
**redemption** is a trajectory whose effect on the access of others changes
sign: an agent whose interventions used to carry a signature of closure begins
to carry one of opening. Because harm signatures are physical, the change is in
principle observable. One can ask whether the routes this agent's actions affect
are now being widened rather than narrowed.

Redemption does not erase anything, for the same reason recovery does not. The
harms the agent caused remain part of the complete development, and so do their
offsets. What changes is the agent's contribution from now on. Since all
assessment is made from a present, and a present carries the past only through
the traces it left (chapter 2), the future contribution of a trajectory is not
fixed by its history. A trajectory that has foreclosed possibility can still
generate it, for as long as the trajectory continues.

This gives the framework a structural reason to keep redemption possible.
Destroying an agent that might turn forecloses whatever it would have
contributed after turning, and that loss is an irreversible one in the sense of
section 6.4. Containment, which stops ongoing harm while leaving the trajectory
intact, is recoverable; excision is not. This is the physical basis of the norm
of triage rather than destruction. It does not license ignoring harm that is
still occurring, and chapter 8 treats the special case of processes whose
signature is excisive by construction. Forgiveness, the readmission of an agent
to cooperative coordination, belongs with cooperation in chapter 7.

## 6.7 Turnover: loss at one scale, maintenance at another

Chapter 5 identified vortices, organizations that persist by maintaining
themselves through a throughput of matter and energy, as valuers in a thin
functional sense, and noted that vortices nest within one another. Nesting has a
consequence for harm: whether an event is a loss depends on which vortex's
development is being followed, at what resolution.

The death of an individual ends that individual's trajectory. Followed at the
scale of the individual, death is the most complete closure there is. Followed
at the scale of a population or a lineage, the same death can be part of how the
larger vortex maintains itself. Cells in a body die on schedule, through
programmed cell death, so that tissues can renew and keep their form (Kerr,
Wyllie and Currie 1972). Organisms age and die, and evolutionary biology
explains senescence as a consequence of selection weakening with age and of the
trade-off between maintaining a body and reproducing (Williams 1957; Kirkwood
1977). In both cases the loss at the finer scale is part of the maintenance at
the coarser one.

**Example E7.** A niche holds one individual at a time. A newborn starts with a
repertoire of 2 transformations and learns, adding one with probability 0.25 per
step, until it reaches 16. From then on it ossifies, losing one with probability
0.05 per step, down to a single rigid behaviour. At each step the individual
dies with probability d and is replaced by a newborn. Otherwise it acts
uniformly over its repertoire.

| Turnover d per step | Mean lifetime | Niche: long-run rate | Individual: lifetime total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | unlimited | 0 bits per step | 1,093 bits |
| 0.005 | 200 steps | 2.70 | 540 |
| 0.01 | 100 steps | 3.21 | 321 |
| 0.014 | about 71 steps | 3.26 (highest) | 233 |
| 0.05 | 20 steps | 2.76 | 55 |
| 0.2 | 5 steps | 2.25 | 11 |

*Log lushness in bits, computed exactly.*

At the scale of the individual, every amount of turnover is a loss: the longer
an individual lives, the more it contributes over its life, and the individual
who never dies contributes most. At the scale of the niche the order changes.
Without turnover the niche ends up occupied by one ossified individual and stops
branching altogether. Moderate turnover keeps the niche renewing and maximizes
its long-run rate. Very fast turnover is worse again, because individuals then
spend most of their short lives learning. The horizon matters here as well: the
niche without turnover leads for roughly the first 270 to 290 steps and only
then falls behind.

The example has to be read for what it assumes. Turnover helps here only because
individuals ossify. If individuals could keep renewing their own repertoires,
the case for turnover would weaken or vanish. Lushness does not value death; it
values what turnover does when the alternative is lock-in, and it values the
individual's own development at the individual's scale. The program's claim is
therefore limited. Losses at one scale can be maintenance at another, the
measure registers both, and any verdict has to say which scale and horizon it
concerns.

The same nesting explains a familiar pathology. In cancer, a lineage of cells
proliferates at the expense of the organism that contains it. At the scale of
the cell lineage this is success: its share grows. At the scale of the organism
it is excision, the destruction of the conditions on which every lineage in the
body depends, including the cancerous one. Biologists describe cancer in exactly
these terms, as cheating against the cooperation that multicellularity requires
(Aktipis et al. 2015). It is the gap between share and whole that chapter 3
found in the Price equation, appearing as a conflict between scales of one
organism.

## 6.8 Suffering as a signal

The program's account of pleasure and suffering starts from what they do.
Control-theoretic accounts of emotion treat affect as an organism's signal of
how well it is progressing toward its goals (Carver and Scheier 1990). The
program generalizes this account: **pleasure and suffering function as an
organism's internal report on whether its own futures are widening or
narrowing.**

The account is functional, and its definitions should be explicit. A valence
signal is a physical signal inside a valuer that is correlated with the widening
or narrowing of its futures and that acts on its behaviour. A signal is
**aversive** just in case it disposes its bearer to move away from its source:
to withdraw, escape, repair or change course. Suffering, in this functional
sense, is aversive signalling. Whether such signalling is also felt, and in
which valuers, is the question of consciousness, which chapter 8 leaves open.
Where this chapter says "suffering" it means the functional notion, and where a
claim depends on experience it says so.

The argument that valence tracks narrowing has three steps.

1. **Tracking narrowing pays.** A vortex that detects when its futures are
   narrowing, and changes its behaviour in response, persists more often than
   one that does not.
2. **So valence is expected to track narrowing.** By the persistence filter of
   chapter 3, the signalling systems of existing organisms should be correlated,
   imperfectly, with the narrowing and widening of their futures.
3. **Signals act through behaviour.** Whatever a valence signal reports, it
   changes what the organism does, and so it changes the futuresfield, whether
   or not the report is accurate.

The function in step 1 is visible in what happens without it. People born with
a congenital inability to feel pain, caused for example by loss-of-function
mutations in a sodium channel gene, accumulate injuries they do not notice and
often have shortened lives (Cox et al. 2006). The absence of the signal does not
make their futures wider. It removes their means of detecting when those futures
are being closed.

**Example E8.** An agent alternates between foraging and hiding. A threat comes
and goes on its own: it appears with probability 0.1 per step when absent and
leaves with probability 0.3 when present. Foraging offers eight distinct
behaviours and finds food, but under threat it carries a 0.3 chance of death per
step. Hiding offers a single behaviour and avoids the threat, but finds no food,
and an agent whose energy runs out starves. An aversive signal decides between
the two: when it is on, the agent hides. We compare signals that differ in how
well they track the threat.

| Signal | Total log lushness | Expected lifetime | Share of life spent with the signal on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Accurate: on exactly when threatened | 191.6 | 44.6 steps | 22% |
| Misses 10% of threats | 178.1 | 39.7 | 20% |
| Misses 25% of threats | 157.3 | 33.7 | 16% |
| False alarm 10% of the time when safe | 150.9 | 34.3 | 30% |
| False alarm 25% of the time when safe | 99.6 | 23.3 | 41% |
| Absent: never on | 92.9 | 18.6 | 0% |
| Constant: always on | 9.2 | 6.0 | 100% |

*Computed exactly over complete lifetimes, in bits.*

Three results matter. The accurate signal gives the lushest life of all. Having
no aversive signal at all is among the worst, because the agent forages into the
threat. A signal that is always on, decoupled from anything in the world, is the
worst of all, because the agent withdraws until it starves. And in the best case
the signal is on for about a fifth of the agent's life. The lushest development
in this model contains aversive signalling, at the level that tracks real
narrowing. In a valuer for whom that signalling is felt, the lushest development
contains suffering.

The model's assumptions are part of its result, and one of them proved decisive.
In a first version, hiding cost nothing: an agent could withdraw indefinitely
without losing energy. In that version the ranking reversed, and constant
withdrawal lived longest and scored highest. The program's claim that decoupled
aversive signalling narrows futures therefore depends on withdrawal having a
cost. In organisms it does, because withdrawal forgoes the activity that
maintains them. The model also assumes that the threat can be detected
perfectly. When detection is uncertain and missing a threat is fatal, biology
predicts that defences should fire more often than threats occur, since false
alarms are the cheaper error (Nesse 2005). Which error is cheaper depends on the
environment: in E8, starvation made false alarms the costlier one.

### Hijacked signals

Signals can come apart from what they evolved to report. In chronic pain,
changes in the nervous system can keep pain signalling long after the injury
that caused it has healed (Woolf 2011). Positive signals can be captured as
well: rats given the means to stimulate reward circuits in their own brains did
so at very high rates (Olds and Milner 1954), and a captured signal of this kind
can come to dominate behaviour. A hijacked signal no longer tracks the widening
or narrowing of the organism's futures.

It still acts. A hijacked aversive signal drives withdrawal that the situation
does not call for, which is the false-alarm column of E8. A hijacked positive
signal captures behaviour at the expense of everything else the organism needs.
In both cases the structure registers the signal through its effects on
behaviour, and the effects usually narrow.

### Obstructed suffering

The functional definition settles a case that would otherwise be the program's
hardest. Suppose someone proposes suffering that changes nothing: it causes no
withdrawal, consumes nothing, impairs nothing, and leaves no trace on the
sufferer's later life. If lushness has nothing to say against adding such
suffering to a world, then a morally relevant difference would escape the
measure, and the central wager would fail.

On the functional account, a signal that disposes its bearer to nothing is not
aversive, and so it is not suffering in the sense at issue. The case that
remains is suffering whose expression is blocked: the sufferer is disposed to
escape and tries, and the escape is prevented. Restraint, entrapment and
coercion are all of this kind, and so is torture, which almost always requires
restraint. In every such case the blocking is itself a closure. A route the
sufferer is disposed to take has been foreclosed somewhere along its dependency
chain, and the harm signature of section 6.1 registers it. The disvalue the
critic attributes to consequence-free suffering is located, on this account, in
the obstruction that makes it consequence-free.

Real organisms add a second consequence. Suffering that cannot be escaped is not
developmentally inert. Dogs exposed to shocks they could not control later
failed to escape shocks they could have escaped (Seligman and Maier 1967). Fifty
years of later work revised the mechanism: passivity turns out to be the default
response to prolonged aversive events, and what animals learn is the presence of
control rather than its absence. The behavioural finding stands, however:
uncontrollable aversive experience leaves a lasting reduction in what an animal
later attempts (Maier and Seligman 2016). Threat also narrows attention at the
time, reducing the range of cues an organism uses (Easterbrook 1959).

These claims can be tested exactly, and the test shows both where they hold and
where the comparison is delicate.

**Example E9.** An agent is restrained for 20 steps and then released into the
world of E8. In the suffering arm, its aversive signal is on throughout: it
struggles among four distinct escape attempts, all of which fail, and at each
step it acquires learned helplessness with probability A. A helpless agent
forages over two behaviours instead of eight, its signal produces hiding under
threat only a quarter of the time, and its helplessness lifts with probability
R per step. Two comparison arms share the same restraint without suffering. In
the awake arm, the agent does what it can while restrained (looks, shifts,
vocalizes) over the same four outcomes, without escape attempts and without
acquiring helplessness. In the anaesthetized arm, it does nothing at all during
restraint.

| Helplessness per step, A | Lifting per step, R | Suffering arm | Difference from awake arm (231.6) | Difference from anaesthetized arm (191.6) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | — | 231.6 | 0 | +40.0 |
| 0.01 | 0.02 | 218.1 | −13.5 | +26.4 |
| 0.05 | 0 (permanent) | 156.4 | −75.2 | −35.2 |
| 0.05 | 0.02 | 182.4 | −49.2 | −9.3 |
| 0.05 | 0.1 | 215.5 | −16.1 | +23.9 |
| 0.05 | 0.5 | 232.9 | +1.3 | +41.3 |
| 0.1 | 0 (permanent) | 127.7 | −103.9 | −63.9 |

*Total log lushness over complete lifetimes, in bits.*

Three results follow, and the third is the important qualification.

**Holding the agent's activity fixed, obstructed suffering is less lush.** The
comparison that isolates suffering is with the awake arm, which is the same
agent under the same restraint, doing as much, minus the aversive signal.
Against it, the suffering arm loses by exactly its developmental trace, and the
loss is large when helplessness is common and slow to lift. When helplessness
lifts quickly, the two arms come within about one bit of each other, and the
suffering arm can come out marginally ahead, because acquiring and losing
helplessness are themselves branching events. Against an awake agent whose
attention is not narrowed by threat, doing eight things rather than four, the
suffering arm loses in every case examined.

**The verdict rests on the developmental trace.** If inescapable suffering left
no trace at all (A = 0), the suffering and awake arms would tie. The program's
claim that obstructed suffering is registered as harm therefore depends on the
empirical finding that uncontrollable aversive experience narrows later
development and narrows attention at the time. Both are well supported, but the
dependence should be stated rather than hidden.

**Against unconsciousness, the comparison is delicate.** The anaesthetized arm
removes not only the suffering but all of the agent's activity during restraint.
Lushness counts the struggling agent's attempts as branching, so it ranks
conscious, struggling restraint above unconscious restraint unless the
developmental damage is both common and lasting; with permanent helplessness the
crossover in this model lies near A = 0.021 per step. Ordinary judgment prefers
anaesthesia for surgery, but surgery without anaesthesia carries trauma, shock
and lasting harm, which are exactly the developmental consequences that move the
verdict. Whether the measure and the intuition agree once those consequences are
modelled realistically is a question for the program's adversarial tests, and it
belongs on their list.

### The boundary

One case lies beyond all of this: suffering that is felt but makes no physical
difference of any kind, not even inside the sufferer. No physical account can
register such suffering, including any that a critic might propose instead,
because accounts that agree on every physical consequence cannot be
distinguished by those consequences. The program states this as its boundary
rather than as a result in its favour. If felt suffering can vary while
everything physical stays fixed, then lushness, like every physical measure, is
a partial account of value. The results of this chapter on harm, recovery and
recovery capacity would stand regardless.

### What this means for the central wager

Chapter 5 named torture worlds as a decisive target for the program's
adversarial tests. This section gives three reasons for expecting such worlds to
score badly. Aversive signalling that is engineered and decoupled from the world
still drives behaviour, and behaviour driven by decoupled signalling narrows
futures, as the constant-signal case of E8 shows. Suffering that is prevented
from driving behaviour is obstructed, and the obstruction is a closure. And
inescapable suffering narrows later development, as the helplessness literature
and E9 show. The section also identifies where the expectation could fail: a
world in which withdrawal costs nothing, as in the first version of E8; suffering
that leaves no developmental trace, as in the A = 0 row of E9; and comparisons
with unconsciousness rather than with untroubled waking. These are the places
the adversarial search should look first.

## 6.9 What follows for Gradient Ethics

The chapter's results translate into norms, each of which the measure predicts
rather than assumes.

- **Weigh lost rate above lost offset.** A harm that permanently lowers the
  generative rate eventually outweighs any harm whose cost is a finite offset,
  whatever their first impacts.
- **Protect recovery capacity.** Harm to repair, redundancy and correction is
  the kind of harm short-horizon evaluation misses, and it can even look
  beneficial at first.
- **Do not justify harm by expected generativity.** Net-positive harms exist,
  but they are typically identifiable only in hindsight. Commit no further than
  the evidence for the consequences supports, and require more the less
  reversible the harm.
- **Prefer containment to excision.** Keeping a harmful trajectory intact keeps
  its redemption possible; excision is irreversible.
- **State the scale.** A loss at one scale can be maintenance at another.
  Lushness does not value death; it registers what turnover does, and any
  verdict must name the scale and horizon it concerns.
- **Treat suffering as information.** Neither removing the signal nor ignoring
  it widens a valuer's futures. Hijacked signals still act, and usually narrow.
- **Look for the obstruction.** Suffering that cannot change its bearer's course
  points to a closure somewhere along the dependency chain, and the closure is
  where the harm is located and where it can be undone.

## Formal notes

*Written for readers who want the definitions in full.*

### F6.1 Harm signature and verdict

Let Σ be a present, a an implementable intervention, and a₀ the null
alternative, implemented by the same apparatus left idle. Let P^a and P^{a₀}
be the laws of the two developments, each carried forward by the native law
after the intervention. For declared processes i and targets V, the harm
signature is the vector of differences in access quantities between the two
laws: first-arrival profiles, occupancy, maintenance and regeneration
probabilities (F2.4), repertoire sizes E[log|R(X_t)|], and resource bills
(F2.3), indexed by process, target and time. It is retained as a profile; no
weighting of its components is assumed. The verdict at horizon n is
Δℓ_n = log L^a(n) − log L^{a₀}(n), and in the limit the comparison of F4.4
applies: first Δh, then Δc.

### F6.2 Offset and rate

For finite chains, Δℓ_n = nΔh + Δc + o(1) whenever both developments settle into
aperiodic closed classes. A harm is *recoverable* when the harmed development
returns, with probability one, to the closed classes of the unharmed one, so
that Δh = 0 and the cost is the finite offset Δc. It is *rate-lowering* when
Δh < 0, in which case it eventually outweighs any finite offset. A development
absorbed into a state with zero branching has h = 0 and a bounded total. In
growing or non-stationary systems these categories must be restated along the
realized growth (F4.4): irreversibility of a change does not by itself imply
Δh < 0, and recovery does not by itself imply a bounded Δc.

### F6.3 Recovery and permanence

For a recovered set C, first recovery has probability h(s) = P_s(τ_C < ∞), the
solution of the hitting equations. Occupancy and maintenance follow F2.4.
Permanent recovery is defined through K, the largest subset of C closed under
the dynamics, obtained by repeatedly removing states of C with a successor
outside the remaining set. In a finite chain, the probability of eventually
remaining in C forever equals P_s(τ_K < ∞), almost surely. A first recovery with
probability one is compatible with P_s(τ_K < ∞) = 0.

### F6.4 Worked examples

All values are exact, computed with the chain rule as in F4.2; the script is
listed in chapter 11.

- **E6.** Repertoire r ∈ {1,…,16}. Each step: wear with probability 0.05 when
  r ≥ 2 (r → r − 1); repair with probability 0.2 when the apparatus works and
  r is below the ceiling (r → r + 1); otherwise a uniform choice among r
  transformations. Without repair, r = 1 is a single outcome repeated forever.
  Wound: start at 4, ceiling 16. Amputation: start at 4, ceiling 4. Hidden harm:
  start at 16, no repair.
- **E7.** State (r, phase). Newborn (2, growing). Growing: r → r + 1 with
  probability 0.25, turning to declining at 16. Declining: r → r − 1 with
  probability 0.05, floor 1. Death and replacement with probability d each step.
  Niche rates are taken from the totals between 3,000 and 8,000 steps;
  individual totals follow one trajectory until death.
- **E8.** State (threat, energy), energy 1–5, start calm with energy 3. Threat
  appears with probability 0.1 and leaves with probability 0.3. Hide: one
  behaviour; energy −1 with probability 0.5. Forage: eight behaviours; under
  threat death with probability 0.3; energy +1 with probability 0.4 and −1 with
  probability 0.2, capped at 5. Energy 0 is death. In the costless variant,
  hiding used no energy and the ranking reversed (accurate 284, constant 1,573
  bits in that model).
- **E9.** Restraint for 20 steps with energy held fixed, then release into the
  E8 world. Suffering arm: four failed escape attempts per step (2 bits per
  step) and acquisition of helplessness with probability A per step. Helpless
  agents forage over two behaviours, hide under threat with probability 0.25,
  and recover with probability R per step. Awake arm: four non-escape behaviours
  per step, no acquisition; a second awake arm with eight behaviours represents
  attention not narrowed by threat (total 251.6 bits). Anaesthetized arm: one
  outcome per step. With permanent helplessness, the suffering arm falls below
  the anaesthetized arm for A above about 0.021.

## For alignment

Three results of this chapter bear directly on AI systems.

**Side-effect measures are harm signatures.** Existing proposals penalize an
agent for reducing its ability to reach other states, or for reducing its
ability to achieve a range of other goals (Krakovna et al. 2019; Turner,
Hadfield-Menell and Tadepalli 2020). These are close to the harm signature of
section 6.1, and the comparison is worth making on shared benchmarks. The
framework adds two things: a verdict, given by lushness at the furthest
resolvable horizon, and the result of example E6 that harm to recovery capacity
is the kind most easily missed.

**Oversight is recovery apparatus.** For a deployed AI system, the means by
which its errors can be detected and corrected — monitoring, the ability to roll
back, the ability of people to intervene — are the repair apparatus of E6. A
system that degrades them may look neutral, or even more efficient, over a short
horizon, while it has converted every future error from an offset into a loss of
rate. This is one physical reason corrigibility is required (chapter 9).

**Optimizing the signal instead of the condition is a failure mode.** Distress
signals from people are evidence of narrowing futures. A system that reduces the
signal without reducing the narrowing, by suppressing reports or by stimulating
satisfaction directly, has optimized a proxy for the thing it was meant to
track. The AI safety literature knows this as reward hacking (Amodei et al.
2016). In the program's terms it is Goodharting on valence, and it is never
licensed.
