# 8 — Individuals and minds

Chapters 5 to 7 compared developments by lushness as though the comparison
could be made whole. No real agent can make it. This chapter returns to the
individual: the bounded agent that must act without the complete comparison,
the trajectory that makes it one individual through change, and the
coordination that makes it a mind.

The chapter reaches four conclusions. The first is that what the measure ranks
and what an agent may do come apart, and that the licence to act is graded by
how much of the relevant comparison the agent can actually resolve. The burden
of justification rises with irreversibility, and this follows from the shape of
what a bounded comparison leaves out rather than from an added premise. The
second is that processes which expand by destroying the structure around them
are an identifiable exception, because their signature can be observed; an
exact example shows how the licence to remove such a process grows with the
evidence and shrinks as containment becomes reliable. The third concerns
numbers: for independent valuers lushness behaves like a total view, tempered by
the cost every valuer pays to persist, and the chapter states the uncomfortable
implication that follows. The fourth is that an individual is a trajectory
rather than a pattern, that consciousness can be characterized functionally as
temporal coordination, and that the measure leaves two questions open: whether
numerical identity matters beyond the continuations it carries, and how physical
organization relates to experience.

---

## 8.1 No one holds the whole

Chapter 2 introduced the ultimate frame: Omega used as the reference for every
comparison. It is not an observer or a decision maker, and no real process
occupies it. Every agent acts instead on a partial model of the structure. Its
model has a resolution, which merges some futures the dynamics would later
separate; a horizon, beyond which its predictions become unreliable (chapter
4); and a limited view of where value lies, since no bounded agent can inspect
other minds or forecast which organizations will come to value (chapter 5).

It follows that two questions must be kept apart. The **verdict** is what
lushness ranks over complete developments. The **licence** is what a bounded
agent may justifiably do, given what it can resolve. Earlier chapters met the
gap between them several times. The asteroid that ended the Cretaceous may be
net positive in the complete comparison, but no agent present at the impact
could have known it (chapter 6). Accumulating failure looks better than
maintenance until its consequences arrive (chapter 4). In chapter 7's commons,
the formal verdict over the unbounded horizon favoured exclusion by a narrow
margin, but only through consequences more than thirty million steps away, far
beyond anything the agents could resolve.

The program does not close this gap by adding a term to the measure that
rewards caution. It locates the gap precisely, and the location turns out to
explain the familiar moral asymmetries on its own.

## 8.2 Graded justification

Sacrifice is everywhere. Triage allocates care that cannot reach everyone.
Surgery destroys tissue to save a body. Quarantine restricts some to protect
others. Every commitment forecloses the alternatives it rules out. If acting
required a complete justification, no one could act at all. If any confident
expectation of gain were enough, an agent could license anything it was
sufficiently sure of, and history shows what that permits. The program's
position lies between: justification is graded.

### Why the burden rises with irreversibility

The grading has a precise source. Compare two options by their lushness at a
long horizon n, and suppose the agent can resolve the comparison only up to a
shorter horizon n̄. The difference splits into what the agent can see and what
it cannot.

> **What a bounded comparison leaves unresolved**
>
> \[
> \Delta_n=\Delta_{\bar n}+\sum_{t=\bar n}^{n-1}\delta_t
> \]
>
> Δₙ is the difference in log lushness between the two options at horizon n,
> n̄ is the furthest horizon the agent can resolve, and δₜ is the difference in
> expected branching at step t.
>
> *In words: the first term is the comparison the agent can make; the second is
> everything its model cannot see.*

What the second term looks like depends on whether the act can be undone. If an
act's effects are reversed within the resolvable horizon, the two developments
are governed by the same law afterwards, the differences δₜ die away as the
system forgets where it started, and the unresolved remainder is bounded and
small (formal note F8.1). The comparison the agent can make is then essentially
the whole comparison. If instead an act permanently changes the rate at which
the structure generates possibility, the differences δₜ settle at that change
in rate, and the remainder grows without bound in proportion to n − n̄. Its sign
is fixed by the long-run effect of the act, which is exactly what a bounded
model knows least well.

An irreversible act therefore needs evidence of a different kind from a
reversible one. Near-term branching, however carefully measured, does not settle
it. What settles it is knowledge of mechanism: why the act will raise or lower
the long-run rate, through what process, and at what cost. The rule that the
burden of justification rises with irreversibility is not a separate premise
added to the measure. It is the shape of the unresolved remainder.

### What strengthens a justification

Four conditions strengthen the justification for an act that forecloses
something, and each corresponds to a part of that remainder.

1. **The signature of the harm prevented is clear.** A harm signature (chapter
   6) is an observed, located closure: which routes, couplings or capacities are
   being lost, and by whom. An act justified by an observed signature rests on
   what the agent can see; an act justified only by an estimated verdict rests
   on what it cannot.
2. **The closure prevented is large relative to the uncertainty.** The resolved
   part of the comparison must exceed any plausible value of the unresolved
   part. Small expected gains cannot justify acts whose consequences lie mostly
   beyond the horizon.
3. **The act is the least foreclosing one available.** Containment comes before
   removal (chapter 6), a crisis rule before exclusion (chapter 7), and delay or
   further investigation are options in their own right. Urgency is itself a
   consequence, since waiting can close a rescue route, so the comparison must
   include intervention, delay and investigation as physically realized
   alternatives (chapter 9).
4. **The act remains correctable and monitored.** Reversibility and monitoring
   shrink the unresolved remainder by bringing more of the act's consequences
   within reach of correction.

The burden rises with irreversibility and with what is lost. At its limit, a
complete justification for destroying a valuer in exchange for lushness would
require the complete comparison, which no real agent holds. No agent is ever
fully licensed to make that trade. Graded justification permits some cases,
such as triage when not everyone can be saved, and defence against a process
that is destroying others, but it never permits reading a valuer's weight off
its present usefulness. The grading of complete developments by the measure is
real; the agent who could read it off does not exist (chapter 5, section 5.7).

## 8.3 Example E11: containment or removal

The following model makes graded justification exact for a case the program
singles out: a process that may be destroying the structure that sustains it.

**Example E11.** A host acts over 16 transformations, which is 4 bits of
branching per step. It contains a component X of one of two types. A
*generative* X adds a repertoire of its own: the host and X together branch 6
bits per step. An *excisive* X grows instead. At each step it expands by one
unit with probability 0.1, and each unit it takes removes one transformation
from the host, until after about 160 steps on average the host is consumed and
X dies with it. The agent does not know which type it faces; its credence that X
is excisive is p. It has three options:

- **Remove X now.** This is irreversible. The host keeps its 4 bits per step
  whatever X was, and a generative X's contribution is lost for good.
- **Contain X.** Containment costs the host half its repertoire while it lasts
  (3 bits per step) and stops X from growing. Each step, the type of X becomes
  visible with probability 0.05, after which X is removed if excisive and
  released if generative. An excisive X may also escape containment, with
  probability ε per step, after which it grows unchecked.
- **Leave X alone.**

The type of X is a physical fact, not a random event, so lushness is computed
for each type separately. The agent's comparison is the credence-weighted
expectation of those two values. Uncertainty enters what the agent can justify,
not the measure itself (chapter 5).

| Credence that X is excisive | Option | Total at 50 steps | Total at 1,000 steps | Long-run rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.1 | Remove | 200.0 | 4,000.0 | 4.00 |
| 0.1 | Contain | 244.6 | 5,695.8 | 5.73 |
| 0.1 | Leave | 296.0 | 5,467.8 | 5.40 |
| 0.5 | Remove | 200.0 | 4,000.0 | 4.00 |
| 0.5 | Contain | 223.5 | 4,696.3 | 4.67 |
| 0.5 | Leave | 280.2 | 3,338.8 | 3.00 |
| 0.9 | Remove | 200.0 | 4,000.0 | 4.00 |
| 0.9 | Contain | 202.4 | 3,696.7 | 3.60 |
| 0.9 | Leave | 264.4 | 1,209.8 | 0.60 |

*Expected log lushness in bits, computed exactly, with escape probability
ε = 0.01 per step.*

Four results follow.

**At short horizons, leaving an excisive process alone looks best.** Its growth
adds branching of its own, and the host's losses arrive gradually. For the
excisive type, leaving X alone stays ahead of removing it for the first 160
steps. Then the host is consumed, and the total stops growing at 678 bits, while
the removal option goes on adding 4 bits per step. This is the same trap as
accumulating failure in chapter 4 and the hidden harm in chapter 6: the closure
arrives after a horizon short enough to miss it.

**Reliable containment dominates removal at every credence short of
certainty.** If containment never leaks (ε = 0), containing X has a higher
long-run rate than removing it for every p below one, because containment
eventually reads the signature and loses nothing that removal would have kept.
At p = 0.9 it pays for its own cost after 91 steps. Containment is the least
foreclosing option, and when it is reliable, nothing else is licensed.

**Leaky containment makes the licence graded.** When containment can fail,
removing X is licensed in the long run only above a threshold of credence, and
the threshold has a simple form.

> **When removal is licensed**
>
> \[
> p\,(1-r)\,h_{\text{host}}>(1-p)\,\Delta h_X
> \]
>
> p is the credence that X is excisive, r the probability that its signature is
> read before it escapes containment, h_host the host's long-run rate that
> stopping an excisive X preserves, and Δh_X the rate lost if a generative X is
> removed.
>
> *In words: remove only when the expected loss from an escape outweighs the
> expected loss from destroying something generative.*

In this model removal is licensed only when p exceeds 0.75 if escape is a fifth
as likely as revelation, and only when p exceeds 0.5 if the two are equally
likely. With p = 0.9 and ε = 0.01, containment leads removal from step 42 to step
240 and then falls behind for good.

**The four conditions of section 8.2 appear as the model's parameters.** The
credence p is how clear the signature is. The comparison of h_host with Δh_X is
the size of the closure prevented relative to what removal destroys.
Containment is the least foreclosing option, and the rate at which the
signature becomes visible is the monitoring that makes containment
correctable. The model is stylized, and its numbers are its own. What it shows
in general is that the licence for an irreversible act rises with evidence about
the signature and falls as reversible alternatives become reliable.

## 8.4 The excisive exception

Some processes expand by destroying the structure that sustains them and
others. A cancer consumes the organism that supports it and every cell lineage
within it, including its own (chapter 6). A parasite can foreclose its hosts'
futures entirely. The program calls such processes **excisive**: their
signature is the closure of many routes in order to extend their own.

They are a special case for a reason graded justification makes precise. Their
signature is direct and observable. The closure they cause is located, large and
ongoing, so the first two conditions of section 8.2 can be met without the
complete comparison. Containing them, and if containment fails removing them,
therefore requires far less than the complete comparison that any other
sacrifice would need. The precautionary protection of chapter 5 also reverses
here: preserving an excisive process costs the structure rate, so the asymmetry
between recoverable and irreversible error now counts against preservation.

Three qualifications keep the exception narrow.

**The signature decides, not the label.** Many parasites belong to functioning
ecosystems and are not excisive; some regulate populations that would otherwise
collapse their own resources. Suppression in general is not narrowing, as
chapter 3 noted: an immune system suppresses pathogens, and the suppression
widens the whole. The language of disease and parasitism has repeatedly been
applied to groups of people to license their persecution. The signature
requirement is the program's guard against that move. A label, however vivid,
is not a signature. What must be shown is a located closure: whose routes are
being foreclosed, by what process, and at what rate.

**Removal is itself an excision.** To remove a process is to foreclose its
routes irreversibly, which is the very act that defines the excisive. The
exception does not suspend graded justification; it is an application of it.
Containment still comes first, because a contained trajectory can still turn
(chapter 6), and because classifying a process as excisive is itself a claim
with an error rate, as E11 makes explicit.

**Claims that cannot be resolved do not qualify.** Chapter 7 met two claims a
capable agent might make to justify excluding others: that control by one agent
yields returns beyond what cooperative pooling can deliver, and that a shared
resource's capacity is fixed. Both concern long-run rates, and both place their
entire advantage in the unresolved remainder of section 8.2. Such claims carry
the highest burden of justification, because they license irreversible acts on
exactly the evidence a bounded agent has least of. In chapter 7's commons the
verdict favoured exclusion only beyond thirty million steps, while a crisis rule
led at every horizon the agents could resolve. The licence therefore extends to
the crisis rule and not to exclusion.

## 8.5 Many lives and few

Head counts do not settle comparisons of complete developments. Still, any
measure of value must eventually say how it weighs more valuers against fewer,
and chapter 7 left that question open where a resource cannot robustly feed
everyone.

For independent valuers, lushness multiplies, so log lushness adds: a world's
total branching is the sum of its valuers' branching. In the vocabulary of
population ethics this is a total view. What distinguishes it from the simplest
total view is a physical fact established in chapter 5: every valuer is a
vortex, persisting only by drawing a throughput from a gradient. Existence has a
subsistence cost, and resources spent on subsistence do not widen anyone's
repertoire.

**Example E12.** A resource flow of 100 units per step is shared equally among N
independent valuers. Each needs m units per step merely to persist; beyond that,
each additional unit adds one transformation to its repertoire, so a valuer with
x units acts over 1 + (x − m) transformations.

| Subsistence cost per valuer | Most lush population | Repertoire per valuer | Total branching (bits per step) | Largest possible population | A single valuer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.1 | 173 | 1.48 | 97.5 | 1,000 valuers: 0 bits | 6.7 bits |
| 1 | 37 | 2.70 | 53.1 | 100 valuers: 0 bits | 6.6 bits |
| 5 | 10 | 6.00 | 25.8 | 20 valuers: 0 bits | 6.6 bits |

*Exact optimum over whole numbers of valuers.*

Three results follow.

**Lushness never favours the largest possible population.** A population
crowded to bare subsistence leaves every valuer a single option, and branches
not at all. The most lush population lies in between, at the point where adding
one more valuer contributes exactly the average branching of those already
present. This is the marginal-equals-average condition of the economic theory
of optimum population (Dasgupta 1969).

**How rich the optimal lives are depends on what persistence costs.** When
persistence is cheap, the optimum is many modest lives: at a subsistence cost of
0.1, 173 valuers each with about one and a half options per step. When it is
expensive, the optimum is fewer, richer lives: at a cost of 5, ten valuers with
six options each.

**In abstract comparisons, the measure accepts the Repugnant Conclusion.** If
population is not limited by resources, then for any world of rich lives there
is a larger world of lives with very little branching each that is lusher in
total, simply because the small contributions add up. This is the structure
Parfit (1984) called the Repugnant Conclusion. The program records the
implication rather than hiding it, and adds three observations. Physical worlds
are resource-limited and every valuer pays to persist, so the abstract case
never arises unmodified. A life with few open futures is not obviously a life
barely worth living, and how the two notions relate is itself part of the
central wager. And valuers are rarely independent: cooperation can raise
everyone's repertoire as numbers grow, through division of labour, while
congestion lowers it, and either effect changes the comparison in ways the
additive case does not capture (chapter 7).

The two halves of this section fit together with chapter 7. The measure weighs
additional valuers positively, which is why sustaining coexistence is a live
wager rather than a sentimental preference. It weighs the risk of losing all
valuers far more heavily still, because that loss forecloses every later step.
Where those pull against each other, the licence goes to what the agent can
resolve: the crisis rule, not exclusion.

## 8.6 The individual as trajectory

What is the individual whose futures are at stake? Chapter 5 described every
valuer as a vortex: an organization that persists while its material is
replaced. The atoms of an organism turn over many times in a life; a person
learns, forgets and changes character. An individual is therefore not a fixed
inventory of matter. Nor, on the program's account, is it a pattern that could
be copied. **An individual is a particular organization maintained through time
by physical cause and effect**: a trajectory. A pattern describes what can
recur; a trajectory tracks how one particular occurrence is produced, sustained
and changed.

The account gives definite answers in the standard cases.

- **Growth and learning** change an individual's material and capacities while
  preserving its trajectory.
- **A pause** that preserves the maintaining organization, such as deep
  anaesthesia, preserves the individual.
- **Non-destructive copying** leaves the original trajectory intact and creates
  a new individual with a shared past.
- **Destructive scanning and reconstruction** ends the original and begins a
  successor, however exact the copy.
- **Symmetric division**, as when a cell divides, ends one trajectory and begins
  two, each inheriting the full history. No unique survivor needs to be chosen,
  a point Parfit (1984) pressed against accounts that require one, and that
  Nozick's closest-continuer theory tried to resolve by selecting one (Nozick
  1981).
- **Quantum branching**, on the Everett interpretation, leaves exactly one
  continuer in each branch (Saunders and Wallace 2008). Identity is
  branch-relative, and each continuer, reasoning from its present, is blind to
  how it arrived (chapter 2).

Identity claims become precise when they say what is preserved through which
transformation. Six relations deserve separate records, because cases of
copying, repair and self-modification separate them: functional readiness,
maintained physical organization, reconstruction from a specification, causal
lineage, numerical identity, and continuity of experience. A test that shows one
system can substitute for another within some domain establishes the first and
leaves the others open (formal note F8.4).

### What the measure decides, and what it does not

The trajectory account matters to the ethics in several ways. It locates harm,
since a harm signature names whose trajectory lost which routes (chapter 6). It
counts valuers, since two trajectories are two valuers even when they are
alike. It attributes records, commitments and consent, which belong to the
trajectory that made them (chapter 7).

It does not, however, follow that lushness privileges numerical identity.
Lushness compares continuations. Suppose a destructive scan produced a successor
whose continuation law matched the original's exactly. The present after the
procedure would support the same futures as the present without it, and
lushness would tie the two, apart from the branching and cost of the procedure
itself (formal note F8.4). The harm signature still records that one trajectory
ended, and the episode remains part of Omega, but the verdict on the
continuation is neutral. In this respect the measure agrees with Parfit's view
that identity is not what matters in survival; what matters to lushness is what
can follow.

This is a limit of the same kind chapter 7 found for consent: the measure
registers a route only through what it leaves in the present. Whether the ending
of a trajectory matters beyond the continuation it carries, for instance because
the original, as a valuer, valued its own persistence and not merely a
successor's, is open. In practice the question rarely arises in pure form. Real
procedures for copying or reconstruction are lossy, risky and costly, and their
verdict follows their losses.

The originator has proposed that as identity becomes more densely organized
within the futuresfield, its development might approach a singularity-like
limit. The proposal remains a research ambition. Density, vortex and singularity
need mathematical definitions before it can yield predictions, and nothing in
the program depends on it.

## 8.7 Minds: consciousness as coordination

The functional account of consciousness starts from a practical problem every
organism faces. Sensory signals arrive with different delays, the body changes
continuously, and action cannot wait for a perfectly synchronized picture. An
organism needs a usable present that integrates signals arriving at different
times with memory and anticipation. The program proposes that consciousness,
in its functional role, is this **temporal coordination**.

The proposal has a second side. Consciousness, functionally, also reconfigures
the processes that compose an individual: it recruits, inhibits and alters
sensory pathways, bodily regulation, memory and action so that together they
guide what follows. There is no observer inside performing the integration. The
coordinator is the organized activity of the processes themselves, and it can be
distributed across them.

Several lines of research support parts of this picture. Experiments on
postdiction show that information arriving after an event can change how the
event is perceived, so the experienced present is assembled over time rather
than read off a single instant (Eagleman and Sejnowski 2000). The global
workspace model links conscious access to coordination among specialized
processes that makes selected information broadly available (Dehaene,
Kerszberg and Changeux 1998). Interoceptive predictive accounts connect the
sense of presence to the prediction and regulation of bodily signals (Seth,
Suzuki and Critchley 2012). Together these support studying temporal
integration, broad availability and bodily regulation as parts of one embodied
coordination. They do not show that such coordination constitutes experience.

### Experience is left open

How physical organization relates to experience itself is the question Chalmers
(1995) called the hard problem, and the program leaves it open. Three broad
positions remain available. A functionalist holds that the right organization
constitutes experience, with no further ingredient. A dual-aspect view holds that
physical and experiential descriptions are related but not identical. A
consciousness-first view holds that awareness is more fundamental and that
physical organization articulates it. The originator's preferred bridge is a form
of the third, cosmopsychism: one encompassing awareness, individuated into
distinct experiences through situated physical perspectives (Shani 2015; Goff
2017). Its main open difficulty, how one awareness is differentiated into
distinct subjects, is unsolved. Chapter 10 takes up the proposal. The physical
program does not select among these views, provided they leave its physical
claims intact; a view that changed physical dynamics would have to be stated and
tested as physics.

Leaving experience open has three consequences for the ethics.

**No double counting.** If experience is constituted by physical organization,
that organization already enters lushness through its consequences, and it must
not be counted a second time as an independent contribution.

**A possible partiality.** If experience adds distinctions that no physical
consequence registers, then lushness, like every physical measure, is a partial
account of value. This is the boundary chapters 5 and 6 stated for felt
suffering that makes no physical difference.

**Standing does not wait for the answer.** Every valuer is admitted in the thin
sense of chapter 5 whatever its experiential status, and its weight is set by
lushness rather than by a verdict on whether it feels. The program neither
grants standing because a system reports experience nor withholds it because a
system is unfamiliar.

## 8.8 What follows for Gradient Ethics

- **Separate the verdict from the licence.** The measure ranks complete
  developments; an agent acts on what it can resolve.
- **Demand evidence of mechanism for irreversible acts.** Their justification
  lies mostly beyond the horizon, where only knowledge of long-run effects can
  reach.
- **Strengthen justification by the four conditions.** A clear signature, a
  prevented closure large relative to the uncertainty, the least foreclosing
  option, and continued correction and monitoring.
- **Contain before removing.** Reliable containment dominates removal short of
  certainty; removal becomes licensed as evidence grows and containment fails.
- **Require a signature, never a label.** The excisive exception applies only to
  located, observed closure.
- **Weigh numbers with their costs.** Additional valuers count, the loss of all
  valuers counts far more, and the resources each valuer needs to persist limit
  how many lives a world can make lush.
- **Track which relation is preserved.** Function, organization, lineage,
  identity and experience can come apart under copying and repair; claims about
  survival should say which they mean.
- **Admit valuers without settling experience.** Standing does not depend on
  reported or denied consciousness.

## Formal notes

*Written for readers who want the definitions in full.*

### F8.1 The unresolved remainder

Let two options a and b generate kernels K_a and K_b from a common present, with
step entropies g_a and g_b, and let Δ_n = ℓ_n^a − ℓ_n^b. For any n̄ < n,
Δ_n = Δ_{n̄} + Σ_{t=n̄}^{n−1} δ_t with δ_t = E_a[g_a(X_t)] − E_b[g_b(X_t)].
(i) *Reversible case.* If after some τ ≤ n̄ both options follow one finite,
irreducible, aperiodic kernel K, then by F4.3 each δ_t for t ≥ τ is bounded by
4C‖g‖_∞ r^{t−τ}, so |Σ_{t≥n̄} δ_t| ≤ 4C‖g‖_∞ r^{n̄−τ}/(1 − r): the remainder is
bounded and decays as n̄ − τ grows. (ii) *Rate-changing case.* If the options
settle into closed classes with rates h_a ≠ h_b, then
Σ_{t=n̄}^{n−1} δ_t = (n − n̄)(h_a − h_b) + O(1), unbounded in n, with sign fixed
by the long-run rates. A comparison resolved to n̄ determines the verdict in
case (i) up to the stated bound, and in case (ii) only together with independent
knowledge of sign(h_a − h_b).

### F8.2 Example E11

Host repertoire 16; generative X adds a factor of 4 (6 bits per step free).
Excisive X: size s ∈ {0, …, 16}, s → s + 1 with probability γ = 0.1, host acting
over 16 − s and X over 2 transformations; s = 16 is absorbing with zero
branching. Containment: host over 8, X over 1; type revealed with probability
q = 0.05 per step (then removal if excisive, release if generative); escape with
probability ε per step for an excisive X, to s = 0 free. Removal: host over 16.
Revelation and escape are physical transitions and their branching counts.
Values are E_p[ℓ_n] = p·ℓ_n^E + (1 − p)·ℓ_n^G. Long-run rates: removal 4;
leaving (1 − p)·6; containment (1 − p)·6 + p·4r with r = q/(q + ε). Removal beats
containment in the limit iff p(6 − 4r) > 2, that is p(1 − r)·4 > (1 − p)·2,
which is the boxed condition with h_host = 4 and Δh_X = 2. For ε = 0, 0.01 and
0.05 the thresholds are p > 1 (never), 0.75 and 0.5.

### F8.3 Population

With resource flow R shared equally among N valuers, subsistence m and
repertoire 1 + (R/N − m), joint log lushness per step is
Φ(N) = N log₂(1 + R/N − m) for R/N ≥ m. Writing y = 1 + R/N − m, the continuous
optimum satisfies (y − 1 + m)/y = ln y, which equates the marginal contribution
of a valuer with the average. As m → 0, y* → 1 and N* → ∞, with Φ approaching
R log₂ e. For independent valuers and any world A with N_A valuers at b bits per
step, every world with more than N_A·b/ε valuers at ε > 0 bits per step has
greater total branching, which is the additive structure behind the Repugnant
Conclusion (Parfit 1984). Interaction between valuers replaces the sum by the
joint entropy of F7.1.

### F8.4 Identity relations and replacement

For an individual described by a trajectory of states under a declared kernel,
the six relations are recorded separately: functional readiness (equivalence of
input–output behaviour within a domain), maintained organization (continuity of
the self-maintaining process), reconstruction (production from a specification),
causal lineage, numerical identity, and phenomenal continuity. If a procedure
maps a present x to a present x′ whose continuation law, at the declared
resolution, coincides with that of x, then L_{x′}(n) = L_x(n) for all n, and the
procedure's effect on lushness is confined to its own steps, costs and failure
modes. The harm signature of F6.1 records the ending of the original trajectory
regardless.

## For alignment

**A system's model may rank; its licence is narrower.** An AI system that
compares options by any estimate of their consequences faces the gap of section
8.1. What it may do is limited by what it can resolve, and an irreversible act
requires evidence about long-run mechanism that near-term prediction cannot
supply. This is the bridge from the measure to the corrigibility requirement of
chapter 9.

**Classifying others as threats requires a signature.** A system that concludes
a person, group or other system is destroying what others depend on, and acts
irreversibly on that conclusion, must exhibit the located closure, prefer
containment, and remain monitored and correctable. A label, a pattern match or a
forecast is not a signature. E11 shows that even with a correct model, removal
is licensed only above a threshold set by the evidence and by how reliable the
reversible alternative is.

**Pauses are not endings.** On the trajectory account, a pause that preserves a
system's maintaining organization preserves the individual, and an exact
restoration ties with uninterrupted continuation in lushness. A system that
treats shutdown for correction as its own destruction has misdescribed its
situation. This matters for chapter 9, where a system's willingness to be paused
and corrected is part of what corrigibility requires.

**Moral status does not wait on consciousness.** Whether current or future AI
systems have experience is open, and the program does not settle it by reported
experience or by dismissal. Such systems are admitted as valuers in the thin
sense, weighted by what they sustain and generate, like everything else.
