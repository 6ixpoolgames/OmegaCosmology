[Chapter 1](#chapter-1) asked who counts, and found that every standard of
good settles the question before it begins. [Chapter 2](#chapter-2) built the
object the rest of the manuscript studies: Omega, everything that can follow
from every present. Nothing in that construction says that any future is
better than another. This chapter says where, in a world described entirely by
physics, better and worse can be found, and what can bear them. It develops
[Overview section 2](#overview-2). Its argument runs in one line: value
requires valuers (3.1); consequence connects them to what follows (3.2); so
value faces what is still accessible (3.3); better and worse are comparisons
between continuations (3.4); the futures open from a present are the substrate
of value still to come (3.5); and anything that takes part in consequence is a
valuer (3.6). It does not argue that the comparison covers the whole world;
[chapter 4](#chapter-4) does. It does not claim that widening futures is
good; that is the central thesis of [chapter 10](#chapter-10).

## 3.1 Where value can be found

**Without valuers there is no value. Valuers are what value is relative to.**

Take two ways the next hour could go for a river after a storm. In one it
floods the town on its bank; in the other it stays in its channel. Physics can
describe both completely: where the water goes, what it moves, what it leaves.
The description contains no word for better or worse. Adding more physical
detail adds more description and still no ordering. This is an old point about
the gap between what is and what ought to be (Hume 1739), and the program
accepts it.

So where does better and worse come from? It comes from the fact that the
flood happens to something. It matters to the town, to the people in it, to
the fields downstream. Remove everything the outcome could happen to, and the
two hours differ physically and in no other way. Value is a relation: it holds
between something and what happens to it. We call whatever stands on the near
side of that relation a valuer, and the principle that value needs one, value
requires valuers.

**Relational does not mean subjective.** One might think that if value is
relative to valuers, it is whatever they take it to be. It is not. The relation
holds between a physical system and the physical continuations of its present,
and both are fixed by the law and the present, whatever anyone believes. A
cell's futures narrow when its supply is cut, whether or not anything registers
the change. Relational value is value fixed by the structure of the world and
located at a place in it.

**It is compatible with intrinsic value.** Moore (1903) asked us to imagine a
world of great beauty that no one will ever see, and judged that its existence
is still better than that of a heap of filth. If so, the objection runs, some
value needs no valuer. But Moore's world removes observers, not valuers in the
sense this chapter develops. Whatever value the beautiful world has, it has
because something exists for it to be the value of. A theory of intrinsic value
still places value in a bearer that exists. The principle asks no more than
that.

> **Plain-English definition 3.1: Value requires valuers**
>
> Value is a relation between something and what happens to it. Without
> something for value to be relative to, nothing is better or worse.

**It is not an axiom.** An axiom is accepted without argument. This principle
is argued: largely from what value is, and, by the end of the chapter, it comes
close to the claim that value in a physical world has a physical bearer, which
almost no one who accepts a physical world denies. We call it the foundational
principle. It fixes where value can be. It does not say what is good.

## 3.2 What connects valuers to value

**Consequence. What can matter to a valuer is what follows for it.**

A principle that value needs valuers says nothing yet about what a valuer is,
or about what in its situation can matter. The answer is consequence: the
dependence of what follows on what is present. Under the law, the present
state of the world shapes what comes next, and through that shaping things can
happen to a system: it can grow or break down, be supplied or cut off, carry on
or come apart. Consequence is meaningful only because it pertains to something,
and what it pertains to is a valuer.

> **Plain-English definition 3.2: Consequence**
>
> The dependence of what follows on what is present: under the law, the
> present shapes what can come next.

> **Plain-English definition 3.3: Valuer**
>
> A physical system that takes part in consequential continuation. It need not
> experience, choose, persist or be a single unified thing.

> **In Omega**
>
> A valuer is a physical system whose development runs through Omega. Its
> futures are the part of Omega that follows for it, always counted with the
> whole present in view.

**Consequence is not branching.** Consequence means that what follows depends
on what is present. Branching means that more than one continuation can follow
one specified present. A system can take part in consequence without any
branching at all: its course can be fixed and still depend on its present
conditions. Being a valuer needs only consequence. A comparison of better and
worse needs more, because it needs alternatives (3.4). Keeping the two apart
matters at once, in the case of determinism.

---

## 3.3 Why value faces the future

**What is already realized is common to every option. Only what follows can
differ.**

Value faces the future for a reason, not by stipulation. Stand at any present
and consider the options open from it. Every one of them shares the same past
and the same present. Whatever value those carry, they carry it equally under
every option, so no choice between options can turn on them. A comparison
between options can only ever see what follows.

This does not make the past worthless. Suffering and flourishing that have
already happened were real, and their effects persist in the present and shape
what can follow. The point is narrower. Value already realized is not value
still open to realization. We say it is already realized, and that its
remaining consequences still belong to the future.

> **Plain-English definition 3.4: Value already realized**
>
> Value in the past and present. It cannot be gained or lost by choosing among
> options now, though its consequences can still be shaped.

**The asymmetry is one of access.** A past event can be uncertain, in the
sense that the present leaves open which of several pasts led to it, without
becoming open to change. A future can be open to change even when nothing about
its statistics distinguishes it from the past. What sets the direction of value
is not how much is known about either side. It is which side can still be
reached.

---

## 3.4 Better and worse

**A continuation can be worse than another continuation of the same present.
Nothing has to be worse than nothing.**

Value enters where the continuations of one present can differ. If one option
leads to a world in which a forest regrows and another to one in which it does
not, the first can be better or worse than the second, and the sign of that
difference is real. A loss is a narrowing of what follows, relative to what else
could have followed.

> **Plain-English definition 3.5: Narrowing and widening**
>
> One option narrows what follows, relative to another from the same present,
> when fewer futures are open under it. Widening is the reverse. Both are
> comparisons, never levels.

**No absolute negative is needed.** Many accounts of value give some states a
value of their own below zero. A hedonic account, on which valuing is feeling,
does so naturally: feelings come with signs, so a painful state is bad at a
level, and summed over a life or a world, those levels can produce totals below
zero. A life or a world can then be worth less than nothing, and its not
existing can rank above its existing. Smart (1958) drew this out of negative
utilitarianism: a ruler able to end all sentient life painlessly would, on that
view, be obliged to.

The argument of this chapter needs no such level. A sign appears only when one
continuation of a present is compared with another. If negative value of some
other kind exists, the argument goes through unchanged; and if, in the case of
the measure built in Part II, what looks like negative value turns out always
to be a narrowing of the field, the framework explains why. The measure has a
floor, a continuation fixed to one outcome, but that is a property of the
measure, not a law about value. Whether what we recognize as the badness of
suffering is a narrowing is tested in [chapter 18](#chapter-18), not settled
here.

**Determinism is the limit case, and it is not a problem.** Specify a present
completely under a deterministic law, and exactly one continuation follows.
There is nothing to compare it with, so nothing can carry a sign: from that
standpoint, no value is gained or lost by any comparison, because there is no
comparison to make. Its systems are still valuers, since their course still
depends on their present. Two different deterministic outcomes come from two
different presents. They are not alternatives open to one present, and
comparing them as though they were is not a comparison the framework makes.

## 3.5 The substrate of value still to come

**The futures open from a present are where value can still be gained or
lost.**

Put the last three sections together. Value is relative to valuers;
consequence is what connects them to what follows; and only what follows can
differ between options. So the futures accessible from a present, with
everything that can follow in them and the valuers they contain, are the
substrate of value still to come. They include the emergence of new valuers and
new kinds of value, as well as the fate of those that exist now.

> **In Omega**
>
> The substrate of value still to come, from a present, is the part of Omega
> that can follow from it.

Only futures that can actually be reached belong to the substrate. A
continuation the law does not permit adds nothing, and one that is permitted
but very unlikely contributes less than one that is likely. How to count the
substrate, with reach and likelihood both taken into account, is the job of
[chapter 5](#chapter-5). For now it is enough to say what is being counted: an
effective number of accessible futures.

**The metavalue.** Value still to come needs valuing still to come: something
for it to be relative to when it arrives. That makes the continuation of
valuing a condition of every value not yet realized. We call it the metavalue,
and two qualifications keep it honest. First, valuing can continue by recovery
or by something new arising, not only by what exists now carrying on. Second,
persistence is worth what it keeps open, compared with whatever would replace
it, and no more. A structure that lasts by holding the world in a narrower
course than its successor would open narrows the future by lasting. An ending
is not bad in itself; it depends on what it forecloses and what follows. [Chapter
7](#chapter-7) makes this exact.

> **Plain-English definition 3.6: The metavalue**
>
> The continuation of valuing, as the condition of every value not yet
> realized. It is instrumental: lasting is worth what it keeps open.

**A reason, for agents.** None of this yet tells anyone what to do. That step
comes from a norm of practical reason: whoever is committed to an end is
committed to the conditions it depends on (Kant 1785). An agent who values
anything is committed, by consistency, to the conditions through which value
can arise and continue, and the substrate is those conditions. The norm
connects valuing to its conditions; it does not by itself say how far the
concern extends or how much is at stake. [Chapter 4](#chapter-4) argues for
its scope, and [chapter 10](#chapter-10) states what the measure claims about
its magnitude. The reason addresses agents, which can act on reasons; it does
not attribute commitments to everything that counts. And what it supports is
keeping the channel open, not preserving everything: sometimes what exists
should be maintained, and sometimes reorganized or replaced because that opens
more.

## 3.6 Who counts

**Anything that takes part in consequential continuation is a valuer.**

We can now answer the question chapter 1 found every standard settling before
it begins. If a valuer is what consequence pertains to, then whatever takes part
in consequential continuation is a valuer.

**Argument 3.1 (universal admission).**
- **(P1)** Value is relative to valuers (3.1).
- **(P2)** What makes something a valuer is that consequence pertains to it:
  what follows depends on its present and runs through it (3.2).
- **(P3)** Every physical system takes part in consequential continuation.
- **(P4)** A criterion of admission beyond consequence needs its own
  justification, and no such criterion has been justified.
- **(C)** Every physical system is a valuer.

The work is done by P2 and P4. P2 is the content of the foundational
principle. P4 is a norm of reasoning the program uses throughout: physics, not
the theorist, decides what counts as one thing and what counts as different, and
a further distinction needs a reason. Experience, agency, life, persistence and
organization have each been proposed as the line. Each draws a line physics
draws only by degrees, or not at all, and each inherits the dispute over where
it falls. Most also bring in a theory of what makes something the same thing
over time, which would have to be settled before anything could be compared.

So a particle in a gas is a valuer, and so is a cell, a person, an institution,
an artificial agent, a short-lived eddy, a scattered population, and the
universe as a whole. A summary computed from the world, such as a total, an
average or a parity, is not: it is a description of physical systems, not one
of them. And existence alone does not qualify. A structure with no
consequential continuation at all, if there could be one, would not be a valuer
merely because it could be said to exist.

**Separate questions.** Being a valuer is a matter of standing. It is not
agency, which is physically implemented control over consequence
([chapter 25](#chapter-25)). It is not identity, which is the continuity of a
pattern across presents ([chapter 22](#chapter-22)). It is not consciousness,
which the measure can distinguish only through the difference it makes to what
follows ([chapter 18](#chapter-18)). Something that changes what follows under
a given comparison, a deformer, is a valuer like any other; but a valuer need
not change the field measurably to count.

**Valuers are not equal, and carry no weights.** Being a valuer settles that
consequence pertains to something. It does not settle how much is at stake. A
molecule in a gas and a living cell differ in what their continuation holds and
in what it opens for everything around them, and the effective count of Part II
reads that difference from the physics. No valuer is assigned a weight, and no
ledger of valuers is kept: [chapter 4](#chapter-4) explains why none is needed.
The differences come in tiers: branching that thermal motion supplies;
structure that persists and sustains further branching; structure that builds
further structure; and processes whose consequences reach through many further
generations of possibility. The tiers describe mechanisms. They are not ranks
of standing.

**The thermal baseline is taken on.** Because every physical system counts, a
gas counts, and every comparison is made against a world that still branches.
The framework does not exclude disorder by declaring it irrelevant. Whether
organization opens more than disorder, and when, is a question the measure has
to answer under the same physics, and [chapter 15](#chapter-15) asks it.

## 3.7 What the argument rests on

**The framework adds no axioms of its own.** What it needs, stated in one
place:

- **The foundational principle:** value requires valuers, with consequence as
  its content (3.1, 3.2). Argued.
- **Two norms of reasoning,** borrowed, not invented: instrumental consistency
  (3.5); and no distinction without justification (3.6).
- **Physical premises,** open to empirical challenge: development is oriented;
  a native law fixes what can follow, with objective probabilities where
  physics supplies them; presents can be specified; consequences stay within
  causal reach ([chapter 2](#chapter-2)).
- **One thesis,** stated and defended later: that the expansion of the
  substrate, measured over the whole field, is the expansion of accessible
  value ([chapter 10](#chapter-10)).

**What this chapter does not claim.**
- That value can be proved from physics. Physics supplies the relata; the
  principle is argued.
- That intrinsic value is impossible.
- That being a valuer requires experience or agency, or that experience and
  agency do not matter.
- That valuers carry weights.
- That negative value cannot exist.
- That widening the future is good. That is the thesis, earned case by case.

*Nihilism, briefly.* An argument that nothing matters because nothing is
watching loses its middle step here. Value is a relation within physics, and
its terms exist whether or not anything watches. [Chapter 29](#chapter-29)
takes the argument up in full.
