## 1. Voice

The manuscript has one voice: Parfit's precision, carried in the manner of the
great accessible philosophers. **Chapter 3 r3 (Value requires valuers) is the
reference chapter**; when in doubt, read a page of it.

**The reader knows nothing of the framework** [112]. Write for someone
meeting it for the first time, not for a collaborator. Information density is
a virtue in discussion with someone steeped in the topic; on the page it
overwhelms the target reader. Prefer one idea per sentence and one step per
paragraph, even at the cost of length. The STE influence (see the changes from
r21) exists for this reason. Two habits to avoid:
- **Attractor words**, which readers recognize as machine-written and which
  arrive without being earned: "ledger", "tapestry", "landscape", "delve",
  "crucial", "robust", "nuanced", "underscore". Use the plain description
  ("scoring each valuer separately and adding the scores").
- **Clipped dramatic reveals**: a run of short sentences that builds to a
  twist ("That is the real defect. Double counting is a symptom. The disease
  is …"). State the point plainly by default. An occasional memorable line
  can stay when its meaning is precise and the reader has the ideas it
  compresses; chapter 4 keeps that very line, with "ledger" used once, at the
  originator's request [113].

**These are defaults, not laws.** Apply the voice where it serves the passage;
break any rule in this section when breaking it makes the writing better. A
proof sketch, a definition, a status table or a dense technical passage may
want a plainer register than an argument does; a moment in an argument may
want a rule broken for emphasis. The test is the reader, not the rule.
("Writing and its rules need breaking", working position [105].) The hard
constraints are elsewhere and do not bend: the status discipline (section 2),
the terms (section 3), and what the originator has ruled out by name.

**The house voice** (working position [104]):
- **The reader's question first.** A section opens with the question a reader
  would actually ask, in plain words, then answers it ("Why should value face
  the future rather than reside equally in every direction of time?"). Do not
  open with the conclusion and then defend it like a proof.
- **Connected argument.** Paragraphs argue in sentences that hand off to one
  another (so, but, conversely, the answer is not … it is …). Bold run-in
  labels on every paragraph and stacks of bare assertions are the dry style to
  avoid.
- **Physical instances, not analogies.** Examples are cases of the claim
  itself: an injury that can heal or worsen, knowledge kept or lost, a
  relationship repaired or broken further. They make a point land without
  borrowing from another domain.
- **Concede, then press.** Grant the strongest part of an objection before the
  turn ("Suffering that has happened was suffering … Neither is made unreal by
  the passage of time.").
- **End on a turn.** Sections close on a short line that turns the argument
  ("The criterion is participation, not the word *exists*."). The line
  carries argument, not ornament.
- **Confident about scope.** Say what is and is not shown, without apology
  ("That is a reply to one route to nihilism, not a declaration that the
  central thesis is proved.").
- **Watch for:** sentences overloaded with qualifications (split them);
  repeating a point within a section; quietness where a short declarative or a
  direct "we" would serve.

- **Plain declarative sentences.** Each claim is stated precisely enough to be
  wrong. A paragraph makes one step.
- **Explicit arguments.**
  - Arguments that carry weight are numbered.
  - Their premises are named and listed.
  - The text says which premise does the work.
- **No needless hedging (rulings 59 and 60).** Use the stronger phrasing
  wherever it can be justified, and qualify a claim only as far as its
  support requires. A qualification that changes nothing the reader would do
  or believe is cut.
- **Bold headlines, argued asterisks (ruling 68).** A TL;DR bullet or a
  section headline states the claim as strongly as it can be defended. Its
  qualifications are argued in the body, under the claim, not folded into
  the headline.
- **Grounded, and the narrative first (rulings 68 and 69).** Omega is a
  physical object, and the prose treats it as one: prefer a concrete
  statement to an abstract restatement, and cut sentences that repeat a point
  at a higher level of abstraction. Not every result needs a physical
  example. Use one where it makes the point land, and keep the argument
  flowing. Accessibility is the aim; background goes in Primers and Notes,
  not into the main line.
- **Objections at full strength.** An objection is stated in its best form
  before it is answered. If it is not answered, the text says so.
- **Worked examples, not analogies.** Analogies are avoided because most are
  bad, not because analogy as such is ruled out [103]; an analogy that survives
  scrutiny may stay, but the default is a physical instance. The usual failure
  is a comparison across domains: setting the case under discussion beside
  another to show that it is "the same". In the measurement and test chapters,
  an example is a specified physical model with numbers that can be recomputed
  (Appendix B), and thought experiments are allowed only when they can be
  written as such a model. In the philosophical chapters (3, 4, 10), an
  example is a physical instance described in words, with no numbers.
- **The reader's question is not a rhetorical question.** Opening a section
  with the question a reader would ask, and then answering it, is the house
  voice. What the rule below forbids is a question left standing in place of
  an argument.
- **No ornament.**
  - No rhetorical questions used in place of arguments.
  - No "clearly", "obviously", "of course", "profoundly" or "deeply".
  - Short memorable lines appear only when they clarify.
- **Person.**
  - "We" for the reasoning ("we now show").
  - "The program" for the research enterprise.
  - "This manuscript" for the text. Never "I", and never "the book".
- **Terms.** A technical term is defined at its first use and keeps exactly
  one sense afterwards.
- **Omega is the object of study (ruling 34).** The manuscript studies the
  completed physical possibility structure. The ethics and its alignment
  relevance fall out of it. Each new concept is placed in Omega when it
  arrives, in an "In Omega" box. The correspondence framing still holds: Omega
  yields the ordering, and that what we recognize as good tracks it is the
  thesis.
- **Alpha is quarantined (rulings 77 and 78).** A TL;DR mentions it only
  where the section has earned it, the Overview being the likeliest place,
  and then says that the argument does not need it. Outside Part VIII,
  mention it at most in passing, with a pointer to chapter 28 and a sentence
  saying the argument at hand does not need it. Chapter 28 makes the
  cosmology's case in full. The aim is a smaller attack surface; the
  cosmology is not disowned.
- **Cosmic nihilism (ruling 35). It is answered by argument, never by
  rhetoric. Grant the nihilist's premises: no value built in, nothing
  watching, everything ends. Then show that the structure is not flat:
  - value is a relation within physics (value requires valuers);
  - organization reaches futures that disorder never does;
  - a finite history still differs in total lushness (Corollary A.3.9);
  - the ultimate frame is a standpoint needing no observer.

  State the limit honestly: the premises leave the ordering standing, and
  whether that ordering is value is the thesis. The main treatment goes in
  chapter 30. Brief mentions, a sentence or two each, go where the point is
  topical.

## 2. Status

Every substantive claim has one of six statuses. In prose, the status is
carried by the verb.

| Status | What it is | Verbs and forms allowed |
|---|---|---|
| D, definition | a stipulation | "we define", "is defined as" |
| T, theorem | proved in Appendix A, or cited with a source | "proves", "it follows", "Theorem A.4.2 shows" |
| M, model result | shown in a named model, within its scope | "in model C.n, …", "the model shows"; never "proves", "establishes" or "demonstrates" without the model's name |
| E, empirical correspondence | observed in published work | "corresponds to", "is observed in", with a citation |
| N, normative premise | assumed, and named | "we assume", "the [name] premise" |
| C, thesis or conjecture | a thesis is asserted and defended; a conjecture is proposed without yet being asserted; either can lose | "the central thesis", "we hold", "we conjecture"; always with what would count against it |

- **Status tables.** Every chapter ends with a status table listing its
  claims under these six labels.
- **Firewalls.**
  - A model result bears on a thesis or conjecture only through a sentence that says how
    far it bears.
  - The subject distinction never licenses an ethical conclusion by itself.
- **Formal results (Appendix A and chapters citing it).** Every formal result
  carries one of these statuses:

  | Status | Meaning |
  |---|---|
  | established elsewhere | proved in the literature; outlined or proved here, with the source |
  | proved here | proved in Appendix A under its stated assumptions |
  | conditional on physical premises | proved given named physical hypotheses, which are not proved |
  | conditional on a normative premise | proved given a named normative premise, which does the normative work |
  | analytical model with stipulated payoffs | algebra in a decision model whose gains and losses are stipulated, not computed by the measure |
  | definition | a stipulation |
  | adopted | a methodological choice |

- **One status per result.** Each numbered result carries exactly one
  status. The literature a proof draws on (its machinery) is cited separately
  and does not change the result's status.
- **Results that rest on premises.** State the premise in the sentence that
  uses the result. Write "given the physical premises F1–F3, a credence below
  the threshold cannot outweigh the largest available gain", never "the
  measure proves the threshold". From Appendix A r9 onwards, no result rests on a
  normative premise, and nothing is adopted. The central thesis (status C) is
  a correspondence, never a definition: write "what we recognize as harm
  corresponds to contraction", never "harm is contraction" or "good is
  breadth".
  Report a stipulated-payoff model as a property of that model, never as a
  theorem of the measure.
- **Pending decisions.** Text that depends on an open decision carries a
  bracketed marker in the draft (for example [P4]). Markers are removed once
  the originator decides.

- **Lushness in prose.** "Lushness" may be used for ordinal comparisons, since
  L and ℓ rank developments the same way. Every number quoted is log lushness
  in bits.
- **Valuer** (Definition A.7.1): anything that physically instantiates a frame
  relative to which its possible continuations are defined. Plain English:
  "anything with a frame of its own, from which its futures can be
  projected". Every physical system qualifies, gas included. The whole world
  is the ultimate frame and counts. A summary of the field, such as a total or
  a parity, is a projection, not a valuer. Valuing implies neither experience
  nor agency, persistence nor present branching. In Appendix A, "projection"
  is the formal term for any map; "subject" means a valuer.
- **Mechanism words carry no sign.** Organization, cooperation, protection,
  concentration and domination matter only through what they do to the
  continuation structure. Never give any of them a value of its own.
- **Narrowing and foreclosure.** In prose, harm "narrows" the futures. Do not
  say it "closes every alternative" unless foreclosure (Definition A.9.3) is
  meant. The largest contraction leaves a determined future, which is not
  total foreclosure.
- **Persistence.** "Metavalue" means that the persistence of valuing is a
  condition of every value not yet realized. In the measure, persistence is
  worth the rate it sustains beyond what would replace it (Proposition A.4.8).
  Never present persistence as valuable in itself.
- **Kept out of manuscript text:** ledger codes (A17, C5), ruling numbers, and
  the word "originator". They belong in the working files and in Appendix H.

## 4. Mathematics

- **Units.** Logarithms are base 2, so entropies are in bits.
- **Notation.** The notation is fixed in section A.1 and used everywhere. The
  core symbols:

  | Symbol | Meaning |
  |---|---|
  | K | kernel |
  | g | step entropy |
  | ℓ_n | log lushness |
  | Π | Cesàro limit |
  | Z = (I − K + Π)⁻¹ | fundamental matrix |
  | h = Πg | rate |
  | b = Z(g − h) | offset |
  | T× = −Δb/Δh | crossover horizon |
  | N, λ | random horizon, hazard |
  | H, h₂ | entropy, binary entropy |

- **Boxed results.**
  - A result's statement is set as a blockquote containing the display.
  - It is followed by a line beginning *In words:*, written without symbols.
  - Every boxed equation has an In words line.
- **Display math.** \[ … \]. Inline math in plain Unicode where it is
  legible (ℓ_n, Δh, T×).
- **Appendix A numbering.** Results are numbered within their section, with
  one counter for all kinds: Definition A.3.1, Lemma A.3.2, Theorem A.3.3,
  and so on.
- **Parts of a result.** Each result gives:
  - its assumptions, where they go beyond the standing ones;
  - the statement;
  - the In words line;
  - a proof, or the source with an outline of the proof;
  - its scope.

  A **conditional proposition** rests on physical premises. The premises are
  named and are not proved.
- **Chapters do not prove.** They state results in words, cite them ("Theorem
  A.4.2") and show a worked example.

## 5. Citations

- **Author–year,** for example (Cover and Thomas 2006). Up to three authors are
  named; four or more are "first author et al.".
- **Pinpoints** (theorem, page or section numbers) are given only when they

## 6. Chapter structure

1. **A TL;DR** (on trial, ruling 40), then **an opening paragraph** stating
   what the chapter claims, with what status, and what it does not claim.
2. **Numbered sections,** n.1, n.2, …. Read in order, the section headers
   hint at the shape of the argument: a looser version of the TL;DR's
   bold-only test, which a well-ordered argument mostly passes on its own
   [111]. A header may be a claim or the reader's question; it is not a bare
   topic label when a claim or question would carry the argument.
3. **A status table,** then the references.

Cross-references take the forms "chapter 5", "section 5.3", "Theorem A.4.2"
and "Appendix C".

**Hyperlinks.** Every cross-reference to a result, chapter, appendix, section
or model result is a link. Anchors are stable placeholders, independent of
revision numbers, and resolved at compilation (the PDF edition makes them
internal links):
- Appendix A results: `#a-4-9` for Proposition A.4.9, and so on;
- chapters: `#chapter-21`; ranges: `#chapters-24-27`; appendices: `#appendix-c`;
- model results in Appendix C: `#c-<model>`, for example `#c-domination`;
- glossary entries: `#glossary-<term>`;
- the Overview's sections, from chapters: `#overview-6` for section 6;
- sections within a file link to their headings directly.
