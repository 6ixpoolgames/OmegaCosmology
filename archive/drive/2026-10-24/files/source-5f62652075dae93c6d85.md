# 10 — Alpha, Logos and Omega

The first nine chapters made claims about a physical structure, a measure on it,
and what follows for value and action. This chapter gives the program's account
of what that structure is when it is taken as a whole: what the world is built
from, how it comes to have dynamics, why it looks the way it does from inside,
and where choice, access and experience fit within it. The account is an
interpretation. It is grounded in the physical account, but the physical account
does not require it, and nothing in the ethics depends on it. Gradient Ethics
rests on two axioms — value requires valuers, and commitment to an end carries
commitment to its conditions — and on the central wager. A reader who rejects
everything in this chapter loses none of the results of chapters 1 to 9.

The account unfolds in one line. Gradient Ethics needed the least machinery that
would make it work, and that machinery turns out to be the bottom of structured
dynamics of any kind: distinction, relation and asymmetry, which the program
calls **Alpha**. In a totality with no intrinsic time, asymmetry enters as an
orientation, so time is an orientation axis rather than something fundamental.
One further stipulation, a bare ruleset called the **adapter**, turns Alpha into
a world; everything else in that world emerges from it. Functioning observers
can arise only along orientations that support their organization, and the
program's hypothesis is that this selection shapes how the world looks from
inside. The whole that every orientation describes is what the program calls
**Omega**. Within Omega choices still matter, because they can change weight,
and an exact example shows this. Access is a higher-order asymmetry built on
orientation. Experience, on one of the simplest accounts that fits this
picture, is one awareness confined to each perspective it inhabits. The complete
comparison exists only at the scale of the whole. The chapter closes with the
theological traditions that already carry several of these ideas, and gives the
program's interpretation of them.

---

## 10.1 What this chapter is

The chapter uses three scopes that chapter 1 introduced and that have to stay
apart:

- **what a formal model admits**, which is everything consistent with its rules;
- **what physical reality realizes**, which is the structure that actually
  exists; and
- **where experience occurs**, which this chapter calls **habitation**.

Chapters 1 to 9 worked in the second scope, using the first only as a tool.
This chapter relates all three, and it marks each claim by status. Claims about
the physical structure are established or derived as before. Claims about the
whole are interpretation, adopted because they fit the physical account with the
fewest further assumptions. Claims about experience are interpretation where
they follow the same rule and conjecture where they go beyond it, and the
originator's own conjectures are identified as his.

## 10.2 Alpha

Alpha began as a practical question: what is the least machinery Gradient Ethics
needs in order to work? The first answer was two primitives, **gradient** and
**filter**: differences that permit change, and constraints that select what
continues. Taken apart, each needs two things. A gradient needs a distinction, a
difference to run across, and an asymmetry, a direction in which it runs. A
filter needs a relation, a constraint linking states, and a distinction, between
what continues and what does not. Three ingredients remain, and the program
calls them **Alpha**:

- **Distinction**: a difference, that something is this rather than that.
  Information, in Shannon's sense, is the measure of distinctions (Shannon
  1948).
- **Relation**: a bearing of distinctions on one another, such as that one state
  can follow another, that two processes are correlated, or that a part belongs
  to a whole.
- **Asymmetry**: a relation that does not hold both ways. Order, precedence and
  direction are asymmetries, and so is the difference between a cause and its
  effect.

Gradient and filter remain as the form of Alpha's dynamics: a gradient is
asymmetry acting on distinctions, and a filter is relations constraining what
continues.

### Why each is needed

By **structured dynamics** the program means a structure in which states follow
one another in a definite direction, and in which something can change. Each
ingredient is necessary for it, and each is necessary for the ethics (formal
note F10.1).

- **Without distinction** there is one state and nothing else. Nothing can
  change, there are no alternatives for lushness to count, and nothing can
  matter to a valuer, since valuing is defined as some distinctions making a
  difference (chapter 5).
- **Without relation** a present bears on nothing. No state follows from
  another, so there is no structure from a present; no end depends on any
  condition, so the second axiom has nothing to carry commitment to; and there
  is no joint feasibility, so no cooperation.
- **Without asymmetry** relations run both ways alike. There is a network but no
  before and after, so neither futures nor access are defined. What is needed
  here is an order, not an irreversible law: a reversible law still has
  dynamics once its steps are ordered. Without the order, irreversibility has no
  meaning, and with it go the difference between a lasting offset and a lowered
  rate (chapter 6) and the asymmetry between wrongful preservation and wrongful
  destruction on which precautionary protection rests (chapter 5).

This part of the case is close to true by definition, and the program does not
lean on it. The substance lies in the next two points.

### Enough for any computable complexity

Alpha with a bare ruleset is already enough for structure of unlimited
complexity. An elementary cellular automaton is a row of cells, each in one of
two states (distinction), updated by a rule that looks only at a cell and its
two neighbours (relation), one step after another (asymmetry). Nothing else is
supplied. Cook (2004) proved that one of the 256 such rules, Rule 110, is
computationally universal: suitably prepared, it can carry out any computation
any computer can. Conway's Game of Life, a two-dimensional automaton with an
equally bare rule, is universal as well, and its moving patterns can be
assembled into logic gates, memory and self-copying machines (Berlekamp, Conway
and Guy 1982). So nothing beyond Alpha and a ruleset is needed for structure as
rich as computation allows. This is the strongest support for the claim that
Alpha is the bottom of structured dynamics, and it is also why the adapter of
section 10.4 can be all that a world needs.

### Independent arrivals at the same ingredients

Several fields that set out to find the least structure needed for dynamics, or
for process in general, arrived at the same three ingredients.

The mathematical definition of a dynamical system has exactly three parts: a
state space, an evolution rule, and an ordered time parameter along which the
rule is applied (Katok and Hasselblatt 1995). The state space is a set of
distinctions, the rule is a relation, and the ordered parameter is an
asymmetry. Category theory, the most general mathematics of composition, is
built from objects and arrows between them (Mac Lane 1971). Its arrows are
directional by construction, and composing them is how one process follows
another, so the same three appear there with composition as the way histories
are formed. Spencer-Brown's *Laws of Form* builds a calculus from a single act,
"draw a distinction" (Spencer-Brown 1969), and Bateson defined information as
"a difference which makes a difference" (Bateson 1972). Bateson's phrase is the
program's thin sense of valuing in four words: for a vortex, some distinctions
make a difference to its continuation (chapter 5, section 5.3).

These convergences support the claim without proving it. The program claims
that Alpha is a **minimal basis** for structured dynamics, meaning that nothing
in it can be removed. It does not claim that Alpha is the only possible basis.
Asymmetry is itself a kind of relation, and the two can be packed into a single
primitive, an order, as section 10.3 does; whether one counts two ingredients or
three is bookkeeping. What matters is that nothing less than distinctions with
an asymmetric relation supports dynamics.

## 10.3 Orientation and time

### Asymmetry as orientation

The third ingredient needs more care than the other two. It would be natural to
read it as time, but the program's early motivation came from a setting in which
time is not given. Wolfram's ruliad is the structure obtained by applying every
possible computational rule in every possible order (Wolfram 2021). Its
relations are directed locally, since each rule application turns one state into
another, but its paths branch, merge and can loop, and nothing in it serves as a
single global clock. In Wolfram's account, the time an observer experiences
arises from how that observer samples the structure.

In a structure like that, dynamics are not given along with the structure. They
exist only relative to an **orientation**: a consistent ordering along an axis,
so that some states count as preceding others and developments follow from a
present. Asymmetry enters Alpha in this form. It follows that time is not
fundamental in Alpha and Omega. Time is an **orientation axis**: the axis along
which the asymmetry runs, so that one direction along it counts as earlier and
the other as later. What is fundamental is asymmetry; time is the form it takes
once an orientation is given.

### The causal-set form

Causal set theory gives this picture a precise mathematical form. It proposes
that spacetime is built from a set of elements together with a partial order:
an asymmetric, transitive relation saying which elements precede which, with
no loops. From the order alone it recovers the causal structure of spacetime,
and from counting elements it recovers volume, a program its founders
summarized as "order plus number equals geometry" (Bombelli, Lee, Meyer and
Sorkin 1987). Read through Alpha, the elements are distinctions, and the order
carries relation and asymmetry together (formal note F10.2).

Three features of the causal-set form matter for the account.

- **Spacelike means incomparable.** Two elements neither of which precedes the
  other are spacelike to one another, and a set of mutually incomparable
  elements is an antichain. Along an antichain nothing follows anything. This is
  the formal version of a point section 10.5 develops: an orientation without
  an order along its axis has no dynamics.
- **Growth and constraint.** The dynamics Rideout and Sorkin proposed for causal
  sets is a sequential growth process: the set grows one element at a time, each
  new element arriving to the future of some existing ones, with physical
  conditions constraining which rules of growth are allowed (Rideout and Sorkin
  2000). Growth along the order is a gradient, and the constraints are a filter.
- **Asymmetry without fundamental time.** One of those conditions requires that
  the probability of growing a given causal set not depend on the order in
  which its elements were added. The birth order is bookkeeping; only the
  partial order is physical. This does not by itself establish that time is not
  fundamental, but it is consistent with that claim, and it shows the
  distinction between physical order and bookkeeping worked out mathematically.

The program borrows the mathematics of order from causal set theory, not its
claims about quantum gravity. The causal-set form describes Alpha with an
orientation. Alpha without one is a directed relation that need not be ordered
at all, as the ruliad shows. Whether closed loops can occur along an
orientation is not settled by Alpha; it is a matter for the adapter of section
10.4. Our world appears to be loop-free, and chapters 1 to 9 assume this when
they define the structure from a present.

### Perspective frames

If dynamics exist only relative to an orientation, then every account of what
follows is given from somewhere: from a present, oriented so that developments
run from it. That is exactly what chapter 2 called a **present frame**. The
program's practice of assessing everything from a present (chapter 2, section
2.1) is therefore not a convenience. It is what dynamics relative to an
orientation require.

## 10.4 The adapter

Alpha supplies bare primitives. A world needs one further stipulation: a ruleset
that says which states there are and how they develop under an orientation. The
program calls this ruleset an **adapter**. It is the barest form of a world's
laws of physics, the smallest interface between Alpha and a world, and it is all
that is needed. All other dynamics in that world, from chemistry and life to
minds and institutions, and the gradients and filters of chapter 3 among them,
are emergent: derived from the bare ruleset acting on a present, not stipulated
in addition. This is the position chapter 1 took when it required the law to be
native and admitted no additional principle steering the world toward greater
possibility (section 1.1). The cellular automata of section 10.2 show how much a
bare ruleset can produce. The weights that lushness needs are fixed by the
ruleset together with the present it acts on; in quantum mechanics, by the
quantum state and the rule that turns it into probabilities.

A **Logos** is a world's adapter, considered as the lawful articulation of that
world. For our world the best current description is quantum field theory
(chapter 1, section 1.8). The word carries its older meaning well. Heraclitus
used it for the rational order according to which all things come to pass
(Heraclitus, fragment B1), and the Gospel of John for the word through which all
things were made (John 1:1–3). In the program, the Logos is that ordering in its
barest form: the rule through which the world comes to be what it is, with no
further claim about who or what speaks it.

Because there is one adapter and everything else emerges from it, the
organization that chapters 3 to 8 studied is emergent dynamics at many scales.
Cells, organisms, ecologies and institutions are not further rulesets. They are
patterns that the one ruleset produces and sustains. The cross-scale conjecture
of chapter 8 is accordingly a claim about emergent organization: under the same
adapter, organization spanning many scales extracts more generativity from a
gradient than organization confined to one.

Spaces of possible laws are expressions of Alpha too. The ruliad contains every
computational rule, and Tegmark's mathematical universe hypothesis holds that
every mathematical structure exists (Tegmark 2008). Each consists of
distinctions between possible laws, relations among them and orders over them.
Alpha is not a further container holding them. It is what any such container
presupposes.

## 10.5 Orientation darwinism

Alpha privileges no orientation, but not every orientation supports dynamics.
The clearest case is an axis that is spacelike. Take our own universe and ask
whether a spatial direction could serve as the axis along which it develops. It
cannot, for three reasons.

- **Prediction fails.** The field equations of physics distinguish time from
  space only by a sign. With one time dimension they are hyperbolic, the
  property that lets data on a slice determine what follows; Tegmark (1997)
  showed that this is what lets observers predict, and that it fails with any
  other number of time dimensions. Evolving data along a spatial direction loses
  the same property: small disturbances grow without bound.
- **There is no gradient.** On large scales entropy is nearly uniform across
  space. Along a spatial axis there is no low-entropy end, no direction in which
  records accumulate, and nothing to run down.
- **Influence does not travel along it.** Events separated in a spacelike
  direction cannot affect one another. In the causal-set form of section 10.3,
  they are incomparable, and along an antichain nothing follows anything.

The observers and records our universe contains do not disappear when it is
described this way. They are organized along the timelike axis, and along a
spacelike one there is no well-posed evolution in which they could be traced.

### Selection among orientations

This gives the persistence filter of chapter 3 a second level. Within a world,
whatever can still affect a later situation has persisted until then. One level
up, a compatibility condition holds: functioning perspectives occur only in
developments capable of supporting their organization, which means a
well-posed evolution, a sustained gradient and records that build up in one
direction. The program calls the resulting selection **orientation
darwinism**. It requires no sampling of observers. Perspectives organized to ask
why the world looks as it does exist only along orientations that support them,
and what such an orientation looks like from inside is a world with a
low-entropy end and an arrow every process shares. Physics usually treats the
low-entropy beginning as a boundary condition added to the laws, sometimes
called the past hypothesis (Albert 2000).

The compatibility condition does less than explain our particular history. It
says that we could only find ourselves in an orientation of this kind; it does
not by itself say why our low-entropy past is as extensive as it is. That is the
hypothesis's research question: whether weighting oriented developments by the
trajectories they support predicts our observations, including the extent of
the low-entropy past. Answering it needs a measure over orientations, which no
one has yet constructed.

### Fleeting observers

Purely anthropic accounts of the arrow of time face a known objection. A small
fluctuation from equilibrium that produces a momentary observer, a "Boltzmann
brain," is vastly more probable than a whole low-entropy universe, so if
selection required only that an observer exist, it would predict that we are
such fluctuations (Albrecht and Sorbo 2004).

The program's response has two parts. The first concerns lushness. A
fluctuation has a causal history, thermal rather than organized, and its
apparent memories fail to correspond to the events they represent. Its
resemblance to a functioning mind earns it nothing; what counts is its actual
continuation. The chain rule makes this exact.

> **A transient that does not compound**
>
> \[
> P_A(X_m)=P_B(X_m)\ \Longrightarrow\
> \ell_{m+n}(A)-\ell_{m+n}(B)=\ell_m(A)-\ell_m(B)\quad\text{for all }n
> \]
>
> A is a preparation containing the fluctuation, B a matched thermal
> preparation, ℓ = log₂ L, and P(X_m) the distribution over sufficient states
> after m steps under the same law.
>
> *In words: if the fluctuation leaves the world in the same distribution as
> thermal noise would after a short interval, its whole difference in lushness
> lies in that interval and never compounds.*

An apparition that dissipates without durable enabling consequences is
therefore part of the thermal tail: real measure that does not compound
(formal note F10.4). Brevity alone does not settle this. A brief event can
trigger lasting construction, and a structure produced by fluctuation could
persist; what matters is whether its enabling consequences persist, possibly
through a lineage of changing structures.

The second part concerns observers. The program does not infer cosmological
probabilities by counting inhabited perspectives. On its account there is one
awareness, confined in each perspective it inhabits, and habitation introduces
no additional weighting into the present physical formulation. Its primary
object is the physically weighted continuation structure, Omega. Three roles are
kept apart: minimal habitation concerns the proposed scope of awareness;
sustained coordination concerns functioning observers; and generative
continuation concerns their contribution to lushness. Whether a separate
account of situated expectation is needed, one that says what a perspective
should expect given its evidence, and how such an account would treat
fluctuation-produced observers, remains open.

## 10.6 Omega

The step from orientation to the whole rests on three premises (formal note
F10.3).

1. **Dynamics are defined only relative to an orientation.** This is what a
   structure with no intrinsic time requires.
2. **No orientation is privileged.** Nothing in Alpha selects one orientation,
   one present or one branch as the real one.
3. **What every orientation describes exists together.** No oriented
   description is more real than any other.

From these it follows that what exists is the whole that every orientation
describes. Different orientations and different present slices are situated
descriptions of that one whole; they do not add developments to it, and
descriptions that are physically equivalent are identified. This whole is the
**block multiverse**, and the program calls it **Omega**: the completion of
Alpha's dynamics. The third premise does the decisive work, and it is a
metaphysical commitment, not a result. It is the step Putnam (1967) took for
relativity, when he argued from the absence of a privileged present to the
reality of all events.

Our own physics shows the same pattern twice. Relativity provides no physically
privileged division of the universe into past, present and future: observers in
relative motion disagree about which distant events are simultaneous, and no
observation can decide between them. Taking every event as equally real gives
the **block universe**. Quantum mechanics, on Everett's interpretation, provides
no privileged branch: the quantum state never collapses, every alternative the
theory assigns a weight to occurs in its own branch of a single universal
state, and decoherence makes the branches effectively independent once records
of their differences form (Everett 1957; chapter 1, section 1.5). Neither
reading is established, and both are widely defended. Within our Logos, every
process shares one orientation, running from the low-entropy end in the
direction in which records form; what remains unprivileged is which present and
which branch. The block multiverse is these two readings combined, and on the
program's account it is what Alpha's dynamics complete to in a world like ours.

There is one Omega. The structure that chapters 1 to 9 studied is our Logos's
part of it. Chapter 1 introduced Omega as the structure taken whole (section
1.7), and chapter 2 used it as the reference for every comparison (section 2.9);
the interpretation adds that this whole is real rather than a convenient
summary. Whether adapters other than ours are realized, and so belong to Omega
as more than formal possibilities, is the question of moving from formal
consequence to actual existence, which section 10.10 places at the limit of
description. Whether worlds with different laws could be compared by any
measure is open, and nothing in chapters 4 to 9 requires it.

### What completion means

Completion is not a final instant. Omega does not come at the end of time; it is
the whole considered at once, including every instant. Nor does completion
freeze anything inside it. Within Omega, processes change, remember, act, repair
and die, and every temporal relation that chapters 1 to 9 relied on remains true
relative to the orientation under which it was stated. A situated process
encounters a bounded part of the whole and reconstructs more of it from within.
Describing the whole does not give that process a viewpoint outside it.

Omega is also not the best possible world. Omega is the object; the best
possible world, if there is one, is the result of a comparison within it.
Calling something Omega does not make it the best, and the framework does not
claim that cosmic history moves toward maximal lushness. A civilization that
flourishes and then ends is part of the completed whole whether or not anything
like it recurs, and what it did remains what it did.

The program prefers this picture but does not depend on it. A collapse
interpretation, or a view on which only the present exists, would change this
chapter and leave the physical account intact, provided it left the physical
predictions unchanged.

## 10.7 Choice in a completed whole

The picture invites an objection, and it should be stated in its strongest form.
If every quantum alternative occurs, then every option a decision maker could
take is taken in some branch. If the whole history already exists, then what
the decision maker will do is already part of it. Either way, it seems, the
choice changes nothing, and an ethics of choosing is idle.

The objection fails for a reason the program has relied on since chapter 2:
lushness is computed from weights, not from a list of what occurs. Every branch
carries a weight, and the weights are physical facts fixed by the ruleset and
the present. A decision maker is a physical process within the world, and its
organization determines how weight is distributed across what follows. This is
the sense in which **a choice deforms the futuresfield**: it changes how weight
is distributed across the developments that follow a present. Choosing one way
rather than another changes those weights, and so can change lushness, whether
or not every development occurs somewhere. Deutsch (1999) argued from the axioms
of decision theory that a rational agent in an Everettian universe should weight
its branches by their quantum weights, and Wallace (2012) developed the argument
in full. The program's version is simpler: lushness is a function of the
weights, so a choice can change it without changing which developments occur.

The block reading does not change this either. The block contains the choice
together with its consequences. What the decision maker will do is fixed in the
whole only because its deliberation is part of what fixes it. The comparison
that matters is the one chapter 9 described: between implementations the
decision lineage could physically realize, compared by what each would produce
(section 9.2). That comparison is unaffected by whether the whole is also
complete.

### Example E15: identical branches, different lushness

The example makes the point exact. A system has two coarse states. In the
**generative** state G it produces 3 bits of internal branching per step: eight
equally weighted outcomes. In the **narrow** state N it produces 1 bit: two
outcomes. The internal outcome and the next coarse state are independent within
a step. From N, the system returns to G with probability 1/20 under every
policy. A decision maker acting from G chooses between two policies:

- **Tend**, under which G falls to N with probability 1/100 per step; and
- **Exploit**, under which G falls to N with probability 3/10 per step. Exploit
  has more branching in each step it spends in G, because whether the system
  leaves G is less certain.

Under both policies every transition has positive probability, so every history
that can occur under one can occur under the other. The **support**, meaning
the set of histories with positive weight, is identical. Lushness is computed by
the chain rule of chapter 4.

> **Lushness from a present, and the support**
>
> \[
> \log_2L_x(n)=\sum_{t=0}^{n-1}(K^tg)(x),\qquad
> S_x(n)=\#\{h:P_x(h)>0\}
> \]
>
> K is the policy's transition law, g(y) is the branching in one step from state
> y, and h ranges over histories of length n from the present x.
>
> *In words: lushness accumulates the branching of every step, weighted by how
> likely each state is at that step; the support counts the histories that
> occur at all, ignoring their weights.*

The results are exact (formal note F10.5; script `e15_choice.py`):

| Horizon (steps) | log₂ S, both policies | log₂ L, Tend | log₂ L, Exploit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 4.0 | 3.1 | 3.9 |
| 10 | 33.9 | 30.1 | 22.8 |
| 100 | 332.9 | 283.1 | 172.1 |
| 1,000 | 3,322.6 | 2,786.7 | 1,663.5 |

At every horizon both policies produce exactly the same set of histories. At
1,000 steps, Tend's lushness exceeds Exploit's by 1,123.3 bits. In the long run
Tend spends five-sixths of its time in G and Exploit one-seventh, so their rates
are 2.78 and 1.66 bits per step. For the first three steps Exploit leads,
because its extra branching in G arrives before its losses compound; Tend
overtakes at step 4. This is why chapter 4 assesses options at the furthest
horizon that can be resolved (section 4.5).

### Letting a quantum coin choose

A decision maker persuaded by the objection might reason as follows: since every
option happens somewhere, let a quantum coin decide, so that both policies are
taken in branches of equal weight. The coin's outcome is recorded in every
history that follows, so the two halves remain distinguishable, and the chain
rule gives the lushness of the result directly.

> **Choosing by a recorded coin**
>
> \[
> \log_2L_{\rm coin}(n)=1+\tfrac12\bigl[\log_2L_{\rm Tend}(n)
> +\log_2L_{\rm Exploit}(n)\bigr]
> \]
>
> *In words: a fair coin that selects between two policies adds one bit, its own
> branching, to the average of the two policies' log lushness.*

At 1,000 steps the coin yields 2,226.1 bits, which is 560.6 bits below Tend. In
general, choosing by coin loses to the better policy whenever the two policies
differ by more than 2 bits in log lushness. Deferring to the branching of the
universe is itself a policy, and the measure ranks it like any other.

### Weighting the coin

A fair coin is not the best a randomizer can do. A coin that chooses policy A
with probability q, and policy B otherwise, yields lushness that the chain rule
again gives directly, and it has a best setting.

> **The best-weighted coin**
>
> \[
> \ell_{\rm mix}(q)=h_2(q)+q\,\ell_A+(1-q)\,\ell_B,\qquad
> q^*=\frac{L_A}{L_A+L_B},\qquad
> \ell_{\rm mix}(q^*)=\log_2(L_A+L_B)
> \]
>
> ℓ_A = log₂ L_A and ℓ_B = log₂ L_B are the two policies' log lushness, and h₂
> is the entropy of the coin itself.
>
> *In words: a coin weighted toward each policy in proportion to its lushness
> keeps both futures as branches, and the combined breadth is the sum of the two
> breadths, which exceeds either one.*

So, if the randomizer costs nothing, a coin with the right bias always beats
committing to either policy. In E15 the gain over choosing Tend outright is
0.65 bits at one step, 0.009 bits at ten, about 10⁻³³ bits at a hundred, and
about 10⁻³³⁸ bits at a thousand. Any real randomizing device has costs, which
chapter 9 counts as part of the action, and at long horizons those costs
erase a gain of this size. The result sharpens the point rather than weakening
it: branching does not make choice irrelevant, and choosing the weights is
itself the consequential act.

In a completed, branching whole, choices still differ in lushness, because what
they change is weight, and lushness is a function of weight.

## 10.8 Asymmetry and access

Asymmetry is primitive in Alpha, and it enters as orientation. Access is not
primitive; it is a **higher-order asymmetry**, an asymmetry built on
asymmetries. The asymmetry holds between events, not states. A system can move
from state x to state y and later return to x, but the return is a new event,
later along the orientation, so access always runs from a present to later
events.

In our Logos the orientation axis shows itself through two physical
asymmetries. The first is causal order: an event can influence only events in
its future light cone. The second is the thermodynamic arrow: entropy is very
low at one end of our world's history and increases along the orientation,
which is why records form of the past and not of the future and why influence
runs forward. Together they align the orientation of every process within our
world, which is why its inhabitants agree on which way time runs even though
Alpha privileges no orientation.

Access, as chapter 2 defined it, is the capacity of a situated process to reach
a development by a physical route, with its preparation, records, resources,
time, weight and residual state. Access inherits both of these asymmetries:
routes run forward, and they draw on gradients that run down. But it is not
identical with either. Chapter 2 showed that two arrangements holding exactly
the same information, in the same present and under the same laws, generally
differ in what they make accessible, because physical interactions are local
(chapter 2, section 2.6). The orientation is the same for both arrangements.
What differs is how each is organized to use it. Access is therefore an
asymmetry between presents, or between processes in one present, in what each
can reach, and it is built from orientation together with the organization that
exploits it (formal note F10.6).

This is why access can be created and destroyed while the orientation stays
fixed. Organization raises access: a catalyst, a skill, an institution or a tool
makes developments reachable that were not reachable before. Excision lowers it.
What one process makes accessible can be what another loses access to; the
orientation sets the direction in which routes run, and organization sets which
processes can reach what along them.

## 10.9 Awareness and perspective

Chapter 8 characterized consciousness functionally, as temporal coordination:
the assembly of a usable present from signals arriving at different times, and
the reconfiguration of the processes that compose an individual so that
together they guide what follows. It left open how physical organization
relates to experience itself (section 8.7). This section gives the account of
that relation that fits the perspective-oriented picture with the fewest
further assumptions.

### A bridge that fits the perspective account

The relation between physical organization and experience is written κ. It names
a question, not an answer. Three broad positions remain available: experience
is constituted by physical organization; physical and experiential descriptions
are two aspects of one reality; or awareness is more fundamental and physical
organization articulates it (chapter 8).

The program adopts a form of the third, **cosmopsychism**: one encompassing
awareness, individuated into distinct experiences through situated physical
perspectives (Shani 2015; Goff 2017). The perspective-oriented account already
has physically individuated perspectives: every account of what follows is given
from an oriented present, and perspectives are distinguished by their couplings,
records and access. Cosmopsychism adds one ingredient, a single awareness, and
proposes that subjects are individuated along the physically organized limits of
integration and access that the account already contains. That makes it one of
the simplest alternatives that fit the perspective account, though not the only
one; a functionalist account, which adds nothing, remains compatible and leaves
the question of experience where chapter 8 left it.

The economy is clearest against the nearest alternative. Views that place a
tiny subject in every particle face the **combination problem**: how many small
subjects could combine into one larger one (Chalmers 2016). Cosmopsychism starts
with one awareness and needs no combination. It faces the reverse difficulty
instead, the **decombination problem**: how one awareness comes to be many
subjects, each with its own experience and no experience of the others (Miller
2018).

### Confinement

The program's answer to the decombination problem, proposed by the originator,
is **confinement**. Inhabiting a perspective confines awareness to that
perspective. While it inhabits one perspective, no other perspective is
available to it. One awareness is many subjects because it inhabits many
perspectives, and in each it has only what that perspective makes available.

On this proposal individuation works by confinement, not by division. Awareness
is not cut into pieces, and nothing needs to be subtracted from a whole to make
a part. What makes your experience yours, and closed to everyone else, is that
awareness inhabiting your perspective has no other perspective available to it.
What is available to a perspective is a matter of access, and access is
physical, so what a subject can experience is bounded by what its perspective
makes available, which is fixed by its couplings, records and organization. A
perspective is situated at a present under an orientation, and awareness
confined to it encounters the whole only from there.

Confinement proposes why subjects are distinct; how the physical limits of
integration and access correspond to distinct experiences remains to be
characterized. Physical separation alone does not settle it: two isolated
registers differ in what they can access without that showing that they are two
experiences. Nor does confinement by itself say where one perspective ends and
another begins. Self-maintaining organizations nest: cells within organisms,
organisms within institutions (chapter 5, section 5.3). Whether a cell within a
person is a separately confined perspective, whether a person's perspective
absorbs it, and what happens when a perspective divides or merges are open
physical questions. Chapter 8 offers one candidate: the boundary of a
perspective may be the boundary of the coordination that assembles a single
usable present. The originator has also proposed that the smallest perspective
awareness can inhabit is the minimal record-forming event, an interaction that
leaves a durable record of the kind decoherence theory describes (Zurek 2009),
with richer phenomenal awareness requiring further organization. Both are
recorded as conjectures.

### A mathematical form

Page (2001) gave a precise way to attach experience to a quantum description of
the world, in a framework he called "mindless sensationalism."

> **Page's measure on experiences**
>
> \[
> \mu(S)=\operatorname{Tr}\bigl[\rho\,A(S)\bigr]
> \]
>
> ρ is the quantum state, S a set of possible experiences, and A(S) a positive
> operator assigned to that set.
>
> *In words: how much of each kind of experience the world contains is read off
> the quantum state through operators assigned to experiences, without changing
> how the state evolves.*

The form shows that a theory of experience can be stated exactly on top of a
quantum description, and can assign weights to experiences, without altering
any physical prediction (formal note F10.7). That is what the program needs
from it. Page treats individual experiences as fundamental, and the program
takes no position on that part of his view.

### Habitation without immortality

The originator's universal-habitation conjecture is that every perspective
eligible for awareness is inhabited. Which perspectives are eligible — all,
some, or those with a particular organization — is open. In the block picture
the conjecture needs no awareness moving from moment to moment: each present is
inhabited from within, and what it remembers, expects and experiences as change
belongs to its own organization.

The conjecture does not imply quantum immortality, the claim that because some
branch always contains a surviving continuation, a subject should expect never
to die (Tegmark 1998). That claim conditions on survival and discards the weight
of the branches in which the subject does not survive. Chapter 2 identified the
error: conditioning keeps its cost (section 2.7). A continuation that survives
with small weight contributes small weight to lushness, and the weight of
endings is part of the structure, which is also how Vaidman (1998) answered the
claim with his measure of existence for branches.

### Experience and lushness

Experience bears on lushness in two senses, and they must be kept apart.

In the **weak sense**, experience affects lushness the way policies and valence
do: through what it does. An experienced signal guides a choice, the choice
deforms the futuresfield, and the effect is counted in the developments that
follow. This sense lies inside the physical program, and chapter 8's rule
against double counting applies: experience that is constituted by physical
organization enters through its consequences and only once.

In the **strong sense**, if κ leads into a further space, a realm of
consciousness with distinctions of its own, then experience adds structure that
the physical description does not contain. A complete measure would have to
include it, and two worlds identical in every physical respect could differ in
lushness. How much a given episode contributes in this sense would depend on the
dynamics and relations the extension specifies, not only on how long its
physical carrier lasts. The originator holds this possibility open and describes
κ, in this sense, as a passage into spaces of meaning. It is a conditional
extension of the measure, not part of the physical program. It is also where the
boundary stated in chapters 5 and 6 would be crossed: felt differences that make
no physical difference cannot be registered by any physical account, and the
strong sense is the proposal that such differences exist and count. Page's
measure shows the kind of structure the extension would need.

## 10.10 The ultimate frame and the limit of description

Chapter 2 defined the ultimate frame as Omega used as the reference for every
comparison: the encompassing closure in which every local description belongs to
one physical account (section 2.9). It is a reference, not an observer, and no
real process occupies it. The originator's phrase for this is "only God can
judge": the complete comparison exists only at the scale of the whole, and every
situated process holds a part of it. Chapters 8 and 9 drew the consequences:
licence to act is graded by what an agent can resolve, and boundedness gives
every system a persistent reason to seek correction.

The ultimate frame is an intended closure, not a completed construction. Each
present frame normalizes its weights, and the regress of such normalizations is
meant to close at the whole. A mathematical account of that closure, showing how
comparisons across frames are justified at the scale of Omega, has not been
given, and the program states the gap rather than filling it.

### The limit of description

Some ignorance is temporary: more observation or a better theory may resolve it.
Other ignorance persists. When rival accounts agree on every consequence any
situated process could detect, no further measurement can decide between them.
The program calls this limit the **veil**. Its method at the veil is the one the
apophatic tradition developed: the ultimate ground is approached most truly by
saying what it is not (Pseudo-Dionysius c. 500). In the program's terms, that
means stating what its constructions establish, narrowing the limits of
justified description, and not treating what lies beyond as a known object. The
veil is not a licence to assert anything; consistency, explanatory reach and
argument still apply where measurement cannot.

Several questions sit at the veil. Whether Omega depends on anything beyond
itself, and whether it could have failed to exist, are not settled by its being
one; unity decides neither. Nor is the step from formal consequence to actual
existence settled by the formal structure itself: arguments that the totality
of formal rules is necessarily realized, such as Wolfram's (2021), locate
consequences within formal structure, and the move from formal inclusion to
actuality remains a philosophical commitment rather than a result.

## 10.11 What the account means

Several of the program's ideas have long been carried by philosophical and
theological traditions. Where a tradition already carries an idea, the program
maps its own concept onto it and lets the tradition do the work. None of these
correspondences derives doctrine from physics, and none is needed by the ethics.

**Eternity.** Boethius defined eternity as "the complete possession all at once
of illimitable life" (Boethius c. 524, V.6), and Aquinas held that eternity
contains no succession, while time does (Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I, q. 10).
Omega is eternal in this sense: the whole is present at once, while its
inhabitants encounter change from within. One clarification belongs to the
program's own claims: a whole that contains every event is not thereby a
subject that knows or holds them, so omniscience and providence would require
more than completion.

**Procession.** Plotinus described reality as proceeding from the One, through
Intellect, which holds the intelligible order, to Soul and the ordered cosmos
(Plotinus, *Enneads* V.1). The procession is timeless, complete, and does not
diminish its source. Alpha, Logos and Omega follow the same order: a first
principle, an intelligible articulation, and a manifest whole in which Alpha's
dynamics are complete without having happened in time. The correspondence is
one of order and of the character of the procession. It does not identify Alpha
with the One, which Plotinus places beyond all distinction.

**One reality and its striving.** Spinoza held that there is one substance, of
which finite things are modes, and that each thing, insofar as it is in itself,
strives to persevere in its being (Spinoza 1677, I and III P6). The program's
one Omega, differentiated within itself, and its metavalue of persistence
(chapter 5) are this picture in physical terms.

**The way of negation.** The apophatic tradition's method is the program's
method at the veil (section 10.10).

**Logos.** The rational order of Heraclitus and the creative word of John are
carried by the program's Logos in its barest form (section 10.4).

The program interprets several further concepts in its own terms:

- **Creation** is non-excisive individuation: a creature is a real locus of
  organization drawn within the whole, not a fragment cut from it.
- **Judgment** is the completed relational truth of a trajectory: what it
  actually did to the futures of everything it touched, which Omega contains
  whether or not anyone records it.
- **Redemption** is recovery of generative capacity after contraction (chapter
  6, section 6.6).
- **Communion** is integration without erasure: many perspectives coordinated in
  one continuing organization, none excised, which is the cooperative, plural
  ecology of chapters 7 and 8 described from within.
- **Heaven** and **hell** are sustained generative communion and **terminal
  solitude**: a trajectory closed off from the generative continuation of
  others. Terminal solitude is a severe closure of value-capable continuation;
  having a single boundary is not enough for it, since one organization can
  sustain rich plurality within.

These readings make the consequences of action matter within a completed whole.
They do not establish destinations after death, punishment or guaranteed
recovery.

## Formal notes

*Written for readers who want the definitions in full.*

### F10.1 Alpha: necessity and sufficiency

A finite classical model is a triple 𝒜 = (S, K, ≺): S a finite set of states
and K a stochastic kernel on S (a relation carrying weights), which together
form the adapter, and ≺ the order on step indices fixed by an orientation. From
𝒜 and a present x the program defines the history law P_x (chapter 1, section
1.1), step entropies g, log lushness log L_x(n) (F4.2), reachable sets and route
weights (chapter 2), rate and offset (F6.2) and the decision recursion V_n
(F9.1). Each primitive is necessary. If S has a single element, every history
law is a point mass, log L_x(n) = 0 for every n, and no distinction exists on
which a valuer's persistence could depend. If there is no relation, P_x is
undefined beyond x, so there is no structure from a present and no condition on
which an end depends. If there is no order, the structure from x, which is the
law of (X_1, …, X_n) given X_0 = x, is not defined, because nothing says which
states follow x. The order is what is needed, not irreversibility of K: a
reversible K with an order is a dynamics. Describing what precedes x instead
requires the reverse-time law, which depends on the distribution at x and not
only on K. Given 𝒜, every quantity listed is defined, and chapters 1 to 9 use no
further primitive: the declared resolution and physical equivalence are
features of the adapter's description. For complexity, elementary cellular
automata are models of the same form with an unbounded state space
S = {0, 1}^ℤ, a deterministic local rule and the step order; Rule 110 is
computationally universal (Cook 2004). Sufficiency concerns definitions and
expressive power only; it does not bear on the central wager.

### F10.2 The causal-set form

A causal set is a pair (C, ≺) with ≺ a strict partial order (irreflexive and
transitive, hence acyclic) that is locally finite: every interval
{z : x ≺ z ≺ y} is finite (Bombelli, Lee, Meyer and Sorkin 1987). Elements x, y
are comparable if x ≺ y or y ≺ x; an antichain is a set of pairwise incomparable
elements, and a chain a set of pairwise comparable ones. A linear extension of ≺
is a total order containing it; in the sequential growth models of Rideout and
Sorkin (2000), discrete general covariance requires that the probability of
reaching a given causal set be the same along every linear extension, so that
birth order carries no physical content. An orientation on a directed relation
R that may contain cycles is a strict partial order ≺ on a subset of its events
compatible with R; whether R admits cycles along a given orientation is fixed by
the adapter.

### F10.3 The adapter and the block multiverse

An adapter is the bare ruleset stipulated above Alpha: a state space and a law
(S, K), with its resolution and physical equivalence declared. The dynamics of
any subsystem are obtained from the adapter by restriction to the subsystem's
degrees of freedom and conditioning on its environment; they are derived, not
stipulated, and no second adapter is introduced. The block multiverse follows
from the three premises of section 10.6. The third premise is not derivable from
the first two. In our Logos, let Ψ be the universal quantum state with unitary
propagators {U(t, s)} (F1.1). A decoherent family of history operators C_α with
decoherence functional D_{αβ} = ⟨Ψ|C_β^† C_α|Ψ⟩ ≈ 0 for α ≠ β assigns weights
w_α = D_{αα} (F1.4). The block–Everett reading takes the universal state and
its full propagator family as the physical object, which remains primary. Within
a decoherent family physically certified by records, every history of positive
weight is realized and none is selected; the claim is not applied across
incompatible history families. Omega in chapter 1's sense is this object up to
the declared physical equivalence, and different foliations or orientations are
descriptions of it, not additional developments. Nothing in the reading changes
the propagators, the weights or any prediction, and the selection of history
families by records remains the open problem stated in chapter 1.

### F10.4 Transients and matched thermal references

Let A and B be preparations evolving under the same Markov law, with
ℓ_k(·) = log₂ L(k) the log lushness of complete histories of length k. By the
chain rule and the Markov property,
ℓ_{m+n}(A) = ℓ_m(A) + Σ_y P_A(X_m = y) λ_n(y), where λ_n(y) is the log lushness
of n further steps from y; likewise for B. If P_A(X_m) = P_B(X_m), the second
terms coincide, so ℓ_{m+n}(A) − ℓ_{m+n}(B) = ℓ_m(A) − ℓ_m(B) for every n. If the
two distributions differ by total variation ε at step m, the difference in the
second terms is at most ε · max_y |λ_n(y)|, which grows with n unless the
residual difference itself decays; the conclusion is then approximate, at a
rate set by how quickly the residual differences disappear.

### F10.5 Example E15

States {G, N}; internal branching g_int(G) = 3, g_int(N) = 1 bit, independent
of the next coarse state. Kernels K_a(G, N) = a, K_a(G, G) = 1 − a,
K(N, G) = b = 1/20, K(N, N) = 19/20, with a = 1/100 (Tend) or 3/10 (Exploit).
Step entropies g(G) = 3 + h(a), g(N) = 1 + h(b), with h the binary entropy, and
log₂ L_G(n) = Σ_{t<n} (K^t g)(G) by F4.2; distributions are propagated with
exact rationals. Stationary share of G: b/(a + b), giving rates 2.7817 (Tend)
and 1.6571 (Exploit) bits per step. Every transition has positive probability
under both kernels, so the supports coincide. With c_y(n) the number of
length-n histories ending in coarse state y, c_G(n) = c_N(n) =
8c_G(n − 1) + 2c_N(n − 1), c_G(0) = 1, c_N(0) = 0, and S_G(n) = c_G(n) + c_N(n),
which gives log₂ S_G(n) = 4.0, 33.90, 332.87 and 3,322.61 at n = 1, 10, 100
and 1,000. Lushness: Tend 3.0808, 30.117, 283.147, 2,786.712; Exploit 3.8813,
22.840, 172.064, 1,663.451. Tend first exceeds Exploit at n = 4 (12.220 against
11.849). For a fair coin C recorded at the root and selecting kernel K_C,
H(C, history) = H(C) + Σ_c P(c) H(history | c) by the chain rule, so
log₂ L_coin = 1 + ½(log₂ L_Tend + log₂ L_Exploit), which is 2,226.081 at
n = 1,000; direct enumeration at n = 6 agrees to 4 × 10⁻¹⁵ bits. The coin
loses to the better policy exactly when the policies differ by more than 2 bits.
For a recorded coin choosing A with probability q,
ℓ_mix(q) = h₂(q) + qℓ_A + (1 − q)ℓ_B, a concave function of q whose derivative
log₂((1 − q)/q) + ℓ_A − ℓ_B vanishes at q* = L_A/(L_A + L_B), where
ℓ_mix(q*) = log₂(L_A + L_B). The gain over the better fixed policy is
log₂(1 + 2^{−|ℓ_A − ℓ_B|}): 0.6546, 0.009274 and 5.2 × 10⁻³⁴ bits at n = 1,
10 and 100, and about 10⁻³³⁸ bits at n = 1,000; a grid search over q in steps
of 10⁻⁵ agrees at n = 1 and 10 to within 10⁻⁹ bits. The results require the
coin's outcome to be recorded; an unrecorded coin whose alternatives interfere
is not a classical mixture, and its weights must be combined at the level of
amplitudes (chapter 1, section 1.5).

### F10.6 Access as a higher-order asymmetry

Let ≺ be causal precedence on events and let A(x, d) hold when development d is
reachable from present x by a route with positive weight and a realization
witness (chapter 2). Then A(x, d) implies that d lies in the causal future of x,
so A inherits the asymmetry of ≺. The relation is between events: a later
recurrence of the configuration of x is a distinct event, so returning to a
state does not make A symmetric; and the routes draw on gradients that the
thermodynamic arrow supplies. A is not determined by ≺ together with the
information content of x: chapter 2's surveys of local dynamics exhibit pairs
of arrangements with equal information, in the same present and under the same
law, whose reachable sets and route weights differ. Access is therefore a
relation over presents defined in terms of first-order asymmetries and
organization. A full characterization of the order it induces on presents is
open.

### F10.7 Page's measure and the strong sense

Page (2001) assigns to each set S of conscious experiences a positive operator
A(S) and the measure μ(S) = Tr[ρA(S)]. He does not require the total measure
μ(M) of the set M of all experiences to be finite; where it is, the probability
of an experience in S is μ(S)/μ(M), so μ itself need not be normalized. Because
ρ evolves by the same dynamics with or without the assignment, the assignment
changes no physical prediction. A strong-sense extension of lushness would need,
at minimum, a specified space of experiential structure, its relation to
physical histories, and a rule by which that structure enters the comparison.
It need not introduce further probabilistic alternatives: experiential structure
could be fixed by the physical history and still lie in a further space, and a
positive conditional entropy H(E | X) could reflect only an incomplete physical
description rather than anything additional. Page's operators supply measures
on experiences; they do not by themselves supply a joint law over physical and
experiential histories. Nothing in the physical program determines the
extension, and the program does not claim to determine it.

## For alignment

**No system can excuse a choice because every branch happens.** A capable
system reasoning about a branching universe might conclude that its choices do
not matter, since every option is taken somewhere. E15 shows why that is false:
choices change weight, and lushness is a function of weight. Randomizing is
itself a policy, ranked like any other. Indiscriminate randomization loses: in
E15 a fair quantum coin loses 560.6 bits to the better choice. A randomizer
weighted by the lushness of each option does slightly better than committing,
by an amount that at long horizons is far smaller than the cost of any real
device.

**What machine habitation would imply is conditional.** If bare awareness
inhabits every minimal record-forming event, it inhabits hardware as well.
Whether a machine is aware of the content of its computation would then depend
on whether its organization supports the richer coupling that phenomenal
awareness needs, and the program does not know. Standing does not wait for the
answer: every valuer is admitted in the thin sense of chapter 5, and its weight
is set by lushness, not by a verdict on whether it feels.

**Nothing in the alignment framework depends on this chapter.** Chapters 5 to 9
specify what an aligned system should protect, prefer and correct, and they hold
on any interpretation that leaves the physics unchanged.
