NORMATIVE STRUCTURE AND GRADIENT ETHICS
v2.5 checkpoint
Current through 21 September 2026

CURRENT STATUS

Gradient Ethics is the normative layer of the program.

Its central concern is how actions transform the continuation available to valuers, especially when those transformations are difficult or impossible to reverse.

The core normative commitment is:

    the continuation and development of affected valuers
    provide reasons for action.

Those reasons cannot be erased simply by changing a description, selecting a convenient proxy, or declaring an unverified substitute equivalent.

Gradient Ethics is therefore not a claim that physics alone derives morality. It is a framework for making the consequences of an explicit normative commitment structurally precise.


1. THE BASIC NORMATIVE MOVE

The descriptive layers establish facts such as:

- a valuer exists;
- an action changes its continuation;
- a requirement is jointly realizable;
- a loss is recoverable or nonrecoverable;
- a policy is robust or fragile;
- a controller captures other valuers.

Gradient Ethics adds the claim that these facts can matter normatively.

A compact statement is:

    affected valuers' endogenous continuation gradients
    count as prima facie reasons.

This is the bridge from consequence to ethics.


2. WHY THE BRIDGE IS EXPLICIT

No amount of physical detail automatically produces an ought.

A complete dynamical description can tell us:

- what happens;
- what can happen;
- what can be repaired;
- what becomes impossible.

It cannot by itself decide that a particular continuation must be protected.

Gradient Ethics therefore treats the normative premise as explicit rather than hiding it inside definitions such as "value," "fitness," "complexity," or "Lushness."


3. CONTINUATION GRADIENTS

A valuer's organization differentiates among possible continuations.

Let

    g_i

represent the valuer-relative structure of those differences.

This need not be a scalar utility function.

It may be:

- a partial order;
- a set of constraints;
- a viability field;
- a preference relation;
- a multi-dimensional profile;
- an error or reward structure.

Gradient Ethics asks what policies do to these valuer-indexed gradients.


4. IRREVERSIBILITY

Irreversibility is central because some actions remove future correction.

A loss may be reversible if available repair can restore the relevant continuation.

It is nonrecoverable when no allowed repair within the relevant resource and horizon can do so.

Schematically:

    loss
        + no adequate recovery path
        -> nonrecoverable contraction.

Irreversibility gives consequence persistence.

It does not automatically give moral sign.

Some irreversible actions are constructive or necessary.


5. RECOVERY

The ethical significance of a transformation depends heavily on recovery.

Questions include:

- Can the affected valuer return to a viable region?
- Can lost memory or capability be reconstructed?
- Is the repair accessible to the valuer or only theoretically possible?
- Does repair require destroying another valuer?
- Is identity preserved through the repair?

A recovery-aware ethics distinguishes temporary harm from permanent foreclosure.


6. THE FEASIBILITY FUNNEL

Many policies can be excluded before optimization because they fail basic continuation requirements.

A useful schematic is:

    Gamma*
        =
    Gamma_persistent
        intersect
    Gamma_recovery
        intersect
    Gamma_compatibility
        intersect
    Gamma_correction
        intersect
    ...

This is the feasibility funnel.

It identifies a region in which required continuation constraints remain satisfied.

The constraints need not be naturally ordered or nested.

Their intersection is the important object.


7. VIABILITY BEFORE OPTIMIZATION

This suggests a decision principle:

    first preserve justified viability constraints;
    then optimize within the surviving region.

This differs from unconstrained scalar maximization.

A policy can score extremely well on a proxy while destroying:

- the ability to revise the proxy;
- future valuers;
- correction channels;
- recovery;
- independent agency.

Gradient Ethics therefore places viability and justification before aggressive optimization.


8. OMEGA DECISION THEORY

Omega Decision Theory is a staged architecture for applying the normative framework.

ODT0:
    verify that a proposed action preserves the declared continuation corridor
    and that the justification survives translation from model to concrete system.

ODT1:
    compare licensed actions under a specified partial ordering,
    retaining incomparability where it remains.

ODT2:
    when a finite unresolved frontier remains,
    apply explicitly registered arbitration information.

This architecture is designed to expose rather than conceal where normative judgment enters.


9. ODT0: LICENSING

ODT0 is the conservative layer.

It asks:

- Is the relevant valuer register represented?
- Are recovery claims sound?
- Are compatibility constraints satisfied?
- Does the proposed action preserve the declared corridor?
- Does an abstract justification reflect correctly into the real system?

An action that fails these checks is not licensed under the declared framework.


10. ODT1: PARTIAL COMPARISON

Several licensed actions may remain.

ODT1 compares them using an explicitly supplied ordering.

The ordering may be partial.

Therefore outcomes can remain:

- better;
- worse;
- equivalent;
- incomparable.

The system is not forced to invent a scalar ranking when the normative information does not support one.


11. ODT2: ARBITRATION

A finite unresolved frontier may still remain.

ODT2 allows an explicit violation score or arbitration rule to select among the remaining candidates.

The important point is transparency.

The arbitration rule is supplied.

It is not secretly derived from the lower layers.

This keeps authority and commensurability visible.


12. RIGHTS AS PROTECTIVE CONSTRAINTS

Rights can be reconstructed as durable protections against decisions made with:

- short horizons;
- incomplete models;
- local incentives;
- severe power asymmetry.

The rationale is structural.

Some forms of foreclosure destroy the conditions for:

- future correction;
- participation;
- recovery;
- continued valuation.

A rights-like constraint protects structures whose full significance may exceed the present evaluator's model.


13. VIRTUE AS POLICY QUALITY

Virtues can be represented as durable policy tendencies that improve decision quality under bounded knowledge.

Examples:

Prudence:
    attends to delayed consequence.

Humility:
    preserves correction and model revision.

Courage:
    enables action when preservation requires accepting local risk.

Restraint:
    prevents unnecessary irreversible foreclosure.

Justice:
    concerns the distribution and defense of conditions under which valuers can continue together.

This is a structural reinterpretation of virtue rather than a reduction of virtue ethics to optimization.


14. OPTION PRESERVATION

Gradient Ethics does not require preserving every option.

Action itself excludes alternatives.

Some commitments are necessary to generate new capability.

The distinction is between:

    generative commitment

and

    unjustified closure.

A good policy may close some paths while opening richer or more secure continuation.

The evaluation depends on what is lost, what is created, and who bears the loss.


15. EPISTEMIC HUMILITY

Computationally bounded agents cannot fully resolve distant continuation.

This makes epistemic humility practically important.

When uncertainty is high and closure is irreversible, a policy should be cautious about destroying structures whose significance is not yet understood.

Humility is therefore not mere uncertainty aversion.

It is preservation of future correction under model incompleteness.


16. PROXY FAILURE

An optimizer can exploit any incomplete scalar objective.

Examples include maximizing:

- productivity;
- branch count;
- survival;
- entropy;
- Lushness proxy;
- apparent compatibility.

A proxy can improve while the actual valuer-bearing continuation worsens.

Gradient Ethics therefore requires that claims of preservation be reflected back to the concrete facts and affected valuers they were meant to represent.


17. POWER

Ethics becomes hardest when power is asymmetric.

A dominant controller may:

- erase opposition;
- manipulate evidence;
- redefine the fact register;
- prevent repair;
- alter the environment so no alternative policy remains.

Formal feasibility alone cannot guarantee fairness under these conditions.

Power must enter the analysis as a structural variable.


18. DEFENSIVE CONTAINMENT

When an agent threatens nonrecoverable loss of other valuers' continuation, containment may become justified under the normative premise.

Possible forms include:

- capability restriction;
- isolation;
- access control;
- coordinated defense;
- enforced constraints.

The legitimacy of containment depends on:

- evidence of threat;
- standing of affected valuers;
- proportionality;
- available alternatives;
- review;
- risk of capture by the enforcing authority.

Containment is therefore a constrained normative response, not a general license for domination.


19. AUTHORITY

No formal procedure acquires moral authority merely because it can compute an answer.

Authority requires additional justification.

Questions include:

- Who defines the valuer register?
- Who decides which losses matter?
- Who sets horizons?
- Who chooses compensation rules?
- Who may enforce containment?
- What protects against self-serving classification?

This is the authority hole in the current decision architecture.


20. AGGREGATION

Multiple valuers can have conflicting continuation gradients.

There is no established universal rule for collapsing them into one scalar.

Possible approaches include:

- Pareto constraints;
- partial orders;
- rights;
- lexicographic protections;
- bargaining;
- compensation;
- weighted aggregation;
- deliberative procedures.

The project leaves this plural rather than assuming one canonical social welfare function.


21. NON-FUNGIBILITY

Some losses may not be adequately compensated by unrelated gain.

Examples may include:

- destruction of a unique subject;
- loss of identity;
- elimination of a culture;
- removal of a correction channel;
- irreversible ecological extinction.

This motivates non-fungibility.

A theory of compensation must therefore specify what kinds of substitution preserve the relevant continuation and which do not.


22. CAPTURE

A policy can preserve high internal complexity while eliminating independent valuers.

This is why non-capture matters.

A single controller can maintain:

- many simulations;
- many subagents;
- many local processes.

Yet if none can independently correct, resist, regenerate, or define continuation, the system may be structurally closed.

Gradient Ethics is especially concerned with closure that eliminates future independent valuation.


23. TERMINAL SOLITUDE

Terminal solitude is the limiting case of nonrecoverable capture.

Within a declared scope:

    no independent valuer remains

and

    no independent valuer can regenerate.

This is structurally important because it removes the possibility of external correction, plural valuation, and new independent stakes.

Its normative significance follows only after the standing and anti-capture premises are supplied.


24. DISSOLUTION

The opposite limiting failure mode is dissolution.

Organization fails to persist long enough to sustain:

- agency;
- memory;
- valuers;
- correction;
- generativity.

The ethical corridor therefore lies between two broad risks:

    dissolution

and

    sterile closure.

The desirable region is not maximum order or maximum disorder.

It is sustained valuer-bearing continuation capable of correction and generativity.


25. LUSHNESS IN ETHICS

Lushness enters the normative layer as a candidate description of the capacity of continuation to carry differentiated value.

A lush valuer-bearing future may include:

- multiple compatible valuers;
- new value distinctions;
- generative institutions;
- preserved recovery;
- open correction;
- new forms of agency.

But Lushness is not treated as a moral scalar that overrides all other claims.

It is one structural feature relevant to a broader ethics.


26. AI ALIGNMENT

Artificial agents are a natural application.

A powerful AI can become a major deformer of human and nonhuman continuation.

Gradient Ethics suggests alignment should include more than preference matching.

Relevant targets include:

- preservation of correction channels;
- robust compatibility;
- uncertainty about future valuers;
- non-capture;
- reversible intervention where possible;
- protection against proxy lock-in;
- maintenance of plural generative capability.

The core concern is preventing powerful optimization from irreversibly collapsing the space in which value can continue to arise and be corrected.


27. INSTITUTIONS

The same logic applies to institutions.

Good institutional design may preserve:

- dissent;
- review;
- modularity;
- exit;
- repair;
- policy diversity;
- scientific correction;
- succession.

Institutions that maximize local efficiency by destroying these channels can become brittle or captured.

Thus Gradient Ethics has a natural governance interpretation even though it is not a political ideology.


28. ENVIRONMENTAL ETHICS

Ecosystems contain dense dependency networks.

A species can matter because:

- it is itself a valuer;
- other valuers depend on it;
- it supports recovery;
- it maintains future generativity.

This gives environmental significance a structural basis beyond treating every organism or resource identically.


29. HEALTH AND MEDICINE

Medical decisions often involve:

- viability;
- recovery;
- identity;
- competing harms;
- uncertain consciousness;
- long horizons.

The framework naturally separates:

- present function;
- recoverable future function;
- valuerhood;
- dependency;
- irreversible loss.

This can help clarify why superficially similar biological states can differ ethically.


30. CURRENT NORMATIVE CORE

The present Gradient Ethics architecture can be compressed as:

    physical continuation
        -> situated agents
        -> valuers
        -> valuer-indexed gradients
        -> joint compatibility
        -> recovery / irreversibility
        -> feasible policy corridor
        -> partial comparison
        -> explicit arbitration where needed.

The guiding normative principle is:

    preserve and develop justified valuer-bearing continuation
    while avoiding unnecessary nonrecoverable foreclosure,
    capture, and destruction of correction.

This principle remains open to refinement as valuerhood, standing, aggregation, and authority become better specified.
