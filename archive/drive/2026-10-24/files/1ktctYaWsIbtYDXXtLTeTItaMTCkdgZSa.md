# 5 — Valuers, protection and the central wager

Part II gave the program a measure. This chapter connects that measure to value,
and it does so through two axioms and one wager. The chapter's main job is to
keep these three apart, so that the reader can see exactly what follows from
the axioms, what the wager adds on top of them, and what evidence would show the
wager to be wrong.

The argument can be summarized before it is developed. Value exists only where
something values. Anyone committed to an end is thereby committed to the
conditions on which that end depends. Almost every end extends through time, so
almost every end commits its holder to the persistence of something, and
persistence depends on the physical possibility structure. Because no one knows
where value is or will be realized, the conditions of valuing must be protected
under every hypothesis, and particular structures must be protected in
proportion to how uncertain their dependencies are and how irreversibly they
could be lost. All of this is derived. The further claim — that more of the
structure is better, measured by lushness — is the program's central wager, and
the chapter ends by stating the tests that would decide it.

---

## 5.1 Value requires valuers

The first axiom is that value exists where valuing occurs: in processes for
which some distinctions matter. Value is not a substance spread through the
universe, present even in places no process could ever care about.

The claim is deliberately minimal, and most ethical traditions accept some
version of it. It does not say that value is whatever anyone happens to prefer,
that every preference is authoritative, or that only human beings value. It says
only that value is realized in a valuer rather than nowhere. A moral realist who
holds that some things would be valuable even if nothing could ever value them
will reject the axiom, and the program does not pretend otherwise.

On its own the axiom does little. Its force comes from combining it with the
second.

---

## 5.2 Commitment carries to conditions

The second axiom is that commitment to an end carries commitment to the
conditions on which that end depends. Someone committed to another person's
flourishing is thereby committed to the bodily, social and environmental
conditions of that flourishing, whether or not they have yet noticed what those
conditions are.

This has the logical form of what Kant called a hypothetical imperative: if you
will the end, you are bound to will the means (Kant 1785). It is not an
additional moral premise smuggled in alongside the first. It is ordinary
instrumental rationality, applied to the conditions of an end rather than to the
actions that pursue it. This is also how the program handles Hume's observation
that no "ought" follows from an "is" alone (Hume 1739, 3.1.1): the axiom does
not derive an ought from physics, but carries an ought the agent already holds
onto the physical facts that the ought depends on. That is why it binds only
those who hold the end. An agent with no ends is not bound by it, and an agent
whose ends are hostile to value can reject any commitment to value as such.

The axiom has one consequence that is easy to overlook. Dependencies exist
before anyone recognizes them. When an agent discovers that something it already
values depends on people, organisms or processes it had never considered, the
reach of its existing commitment extends to them. Learning more about the world
does not add new values; it reveals what the values an agent already holds have
required all along.

---

## 5.3 Persistence: the metavalue

If conditional normativity applied only to agents already committed to value as
such, it would bind few of them. What makes it bind nearly every agent is
persistence, and the argument for this runs in four steps.

**First, almost every end requires the persistence of something.** Most ends
extend through time: they have to be pursued, maintained or realized at some
later moment. An end of that kind cannot be achieved unless something carries it
forward — the agent itself, its successors, or the conditions that will
eventually realize it. By the second axiom, commitment to such an end is
therefore commitment to the persistence of whatever carries it. Even an end that
requires self-sacrifice requires the persistence of something other than the
agent, such as the outcome it dies for or the people who inherit it. In this
sense persistence is a metavalue: it is implied by nearly every other value,
whatever that value happens to be (formal note F5.4).

**Second, selection makes persistence common.** Chapter 3 described the
persistence filter: whatever can still affect a later situation has, by
definition, persisted until then. Valuers whose organization supports their
continued valuing therefore become more represented among later valuers than
those whose organization does not. This is a fact about selection rather than a
normative claim, but it means that the metavalue is what we should expect to find
among the valuers that actually exist. The observation is old: Spinoza held that
each thing, insofar as it is in itself, strives to persevere in its being
(Spinoza 1677, III P6).

**Third, artificial agents share it.** The same conclusion appears in AI research
under the name of instrumental convergence. Whatever an agent's final goal,
continuing to exist and retaining options help it achieve that goal (Omohundro
2008; Bostrom 2012). Turner and colleagues proved a precise version of this for
a broad class of decision problems: across most possible goals, optimal
policies tend to keep more options available (Turner et al. 2021). An agent with
arbitrary terminal goals still has to persist, and still has to preserve the
conditions under which its goals can be pursued.

**Fourth, a stake in one's own persistence extends beyond what one can
identify.** An agent's persistence depends on conditions beyond itself:
resources, other processes, the stability of its environment, and the future
behaviour of agents it will later need. These conditions are parts of the
physical possibility structure. The agent cannot know in advance which parts of
the structure its persistence will come to depend on, because its future
circumstances are themselves uncertain. Its stake therefore extends beyond the
parts it can currently identify as useful. This does not show that every part of
the structure is necessary to it, and section 5.4 separates what can be derived
strictly from what uncertainty supports.

Persistence is the foothold of the argument, not its verdict, and this needs to
be said plainly. Pursued narrowly, persistence is exactly what drives excision:
an agent securing itself by closing off everyone else's routes, as chapter 3
described. The metavalue explains why nearly every agent has a stake in the
structure, but it does not by itself forbid an agent from destroying the parts it
believes it will never need. What restrains that move is the uncertainty in the
fourth step, which the next section makes precise, together with the limits on
every agent's knowledge that chapter 8 develops.

### What persists: the vortex

The metavalue raises a question that matters later: what exactly is it that
persists? For living and social things, the answer is not a fixed body of
matter. The atoms of an organism are replaced many times over its life, the
cells of a body turn over, and the members of an institution come and go, yet in
each case something continues.

The program describes what continues with the image of a **vortex**. In a
flowing liquid, a vortex is a pattern of rotation that persists while the water
passing through it is continuously replaced; what lasts is the pattern of
motion, not any particular water. Physics and biology study the general
phenomenon under other names. Dissipative structures are organized patterns that
exist only while a flow of energy passes through them (Nicolis and Prigogine
1977), and autopoietic systems are networks of processes that continuously
produce the components that make them up (Maturana and Varela 1980).

In this manuscript, a vortex is any organization that persists by maintaining
itself through a throughput of matter and energy: it draws on a gradient,
repairs or replaces its own parts, and would dissolve if the throughput stopped.
A rock persists without throughput, so it is not a vortex in this sense; a flame,
a cell, an organism, a firm and a city are. Vortices can contain other vortices
— cells within organisms, organisms within populations — and each persists at
its own scale.

Every vortex values something in a thin but definite sense: some distinctions
matter to its continuation, and its organization responds to them. A flame
depends on fuel and oxygen, draws them in, and goes out without them; in this
thin sense it values them. The program adopts this reading deliberately. The
alternative would be to draw a line between real valuers and merely
self-maintaining processes, and any such line would decide in advance who
counts, which is exactly what the uncertainty of section 5.4 forbids.

Three clarifications keep the reading from being misunderstood. Valuing in this
sense is not experience: whether and where valuing is felt is the question of
consciousness, which chapter 8 leaves open. It is not moral weight: how much any
valuer's valuing counts is set by its consequences for lushness, including the
lushness of its own futures, and not by its status as a valuer. And it does not
make all vortices alike: a flame and a person both value, and their valuing
differs enormously in what it sustains and generates. The metavalue of
persistence attaches to vortices, each at its own scale.
Chapters 6 and 8 take up what follows from this nesting, including the fact that
what counts as loss at one scale can count as maintenance at the next.

## 5.4 Protection: strict and precautionary

The derived part of the argument has two tiers, and they rest on different
grounds. The first is strict and follows by dominance. The second is
precautionary and follows from the limits of what any agent can know. Keeping
them apart matters, because the first is far stronger than the second and covers
far less.

### Strict protection

The strict tier has four steps, each depending only on what has already been
established.

1. **We do not know where value lies.** No bounded agent can inspect the inner
   states of other minds, forecast which organizations will come to value, or
   say what they will value when they do.
2. **Wherever value lies, it depends on physical conditions.** By the first
   axiom, value exists only in valuers, and valuers are physical processes.
   Whatever value turns out to be, it is therefore realized in physical
   organization within the possibility structure.
3. **So the conditions of valuing are necessary under every hypothesis.** By the
   conditions of valuing we mean what valuing as such requires: a structure
   capable of sustaining valuers at all. For any end whose realization requires
   some valuer to exist, and under any hypothesis about where value is realized,
   the end depends on these conditions, and by the second axiom a commitment to
   it transmits to them.
4. **So destroying the conditions of valuing is dominated.** An action is
   dominated when some alternative is at least as good under every possibility
   and better under some. Whichever hypothesis turns out to be true, destroying
   the conditions of valuing defeats the valued end, while preserving them does
   not.

This argument needs no probabilities over the hypotheses, and it does not assume
that more of the structure is better than less (formal note F5.1). Its scope has
to be stated precisely. It covers the conditions of valuing as such. It does not
show that destroying any particular part of the present structure — a species, a
bacterium, an institution — is dominated under every hypothesis, because for any
particular part there are hypotheses under which it plays no role in the valued
end.

One premise of the dominance step must also be made explicit. The step assumes
that the end in question ranks a world without any valuers lowest, so that
preserving the conditions of valuing is at least as good as destroying them
under every hypothesis. Not every end does. An end that a world without valuers
would satisfy, such as minimizing suffering pursued to its limit, can rank the
destruction of all valuers highest; this is the benevolent world-exploder
objection to negative utilitarianism (Smart 1958). The same holds for any end
that allows some worlds with valuers to be worse than a world with none. Such
ends fall outside strict protection, as do ends hostile to value, which section
5.2 already set aside. What still reaches an agent holding them is the
precautionary tier below: an agent uncertain whether it has read its own values
correctly faces, in destroying all valuers, the least recoverable error there
is.

### Precautionary protection

For particular structures the argument is different in kind. It rests not on
dominance but on an asymmetry between two errors a bounded agent can make about
something whose future importance it cannot rule out.

- **Wrongful preservation.** Preserving something that turns out not to matter
  usually costs a finite amount: the resources and opportunities spent keeping
  it.
- **Wrongful destruction.** Destroying something irreversibly that turns out to
  matter forecloses whatever depended on it, at every later step.

Chapter 6 makes the asymmetry precise: a harm that permanently lowers the rate at
which the structure generates possibility eventually outweighs any harm whose
cost is a finite offset. It follows that, for destruction that cannot be undone,
even a modest credence that the destroyed structure will matter comes to outweigh
a bounded cost of preserving it, once the horizon is long enough to see the
consequence (formal note F5.6).

This is an expected-loss argument, and it is weaker than dominance in three
ways. It needs credences, however rough. It applies only within the furthest
horizon the agent can resolve. And it fails where preservation itself costs
rate, as when what would be preserved is excisive (chapter 8). Credences too
small to be meaningful cannot drive it either, for the reason chapter 9 gives
under bounded fanaticism. Precautionary protection is therefore graded: its
force rises with irreversibility and with the difficulty of ruling out
dependence, and it weakens when preservation itself forecloses. Chapter 8 turns
this gradient into the program's account of graded justification.

The reach of the two tiers differs accordingly. Strict protection applies to
anyone committed to value as such, and to anyone committed to a particular good,
since every good depends on the conditions of valuing. Precautionary protection
applies to nearly every agent through the metavalue of section 5.3, because an
agent's stake in its own persistence extends to dependencies it cannot yet
identify.

### The same uncertainty selects the object

Welfare, preferences, capabilities, experiences and lives are all features of
the possibility structure. An ethical theory that took any one of them as its
fundamental object would be deciding in advance which kind of thing counts. Step
1 forbids that decision, because we do not know which kinds of things will turn
out to matter. The physical possibility structure is the only object that
contains every candidate without choosing among them.

This is also the source of the program's impartiality. Impartiality is not an
extra axiom. It is simply the starting point of an analysis that does not yet
know who counts.

### The measure

Once the structure is the object, it needs a magnitude. Chapter 4 adopted a
class of measures: those that depend only on the weights of alternatives and give
the same verdict whether a development is assessed whole or step by step. Within
that class exactly one measure has the right calibration, and it is lushness
(Shannon 1948; Khinchin 1957; Faddeev 1956). The class itself is the program's
choice, motivated by impartiality and present-rooting but not forced by them.
Measures that also register how different two futures are would be a natural
extension, and lushness is their limiting case when every future counts as fully
distinct.

## 5.5 The central wager

Strict protection establishes that destroying the conditions of valuing is
dominated, and precautionary protection that irreversible destruction of
particular structures needs strong grounds. Neither establishes that more of the
structure is better. These are different claims: an end can require that
something exist without requiring that there be more of it, so a necessary
condition is not automatically a quantity to maximize.

The program's central wager is that, under uncertainty about where value lies, a
larger structure is better than a smaller one. The reason is that a larger
structure forecloses fewer of the ways value might turn out to be realized. This
is the logic of option value in economics: when the future is uncertain and some
losses cannot be undone, preserving the ability to choose later is worth
something in itself, because it lets choices respond to what is learned (Arrow
and Fisher 1974; Henry 1974). The wager extends that logic from the options of a
single decision maker to the structure from which valuers and their options
arise. It is a wager, rather than a theorem, because that extension is not
proved. Chapter 4 supplies what "larger" means: greater lushness, assessed at
the furthest horizon that can be resolved.

The wager and the program's thesis — that moral intuitions track features of the
structure — turn out to be a single claim seen from two directions. If good and
bad are not arbitrary, it is because they correspond to physical differences in
the structure, and the program claims that the relevant differences are
differences in how richly the structure supports what can follow. Stated
directly:

> **Good** is organization that sustains and compounds the conditions for
> further structured possibility. **Bad** is securing local continuation through
> suppression, destruction or foreclosure of the generative capacity around it.

Chapter 3 supplied a physical mechanism for each half of this statement. The
first is compounding repertoire growth, in which products build further
constructors. The second is suppression in Ashby's sense, in which a regulator
secures its own stability by removing variety from its surroundings (Ashby
1956). Chapter 4 then showed that lushness separates the two in exact models.
Whether it also separates them in the complex, coupled systems that ethics
actually concerns is precisely what the wager stakes.

---

## 5.6 The burden and the decisive tests

The wager carries a burden that should be stated without softening. Lushness
counts every future, including futures of failure, domination and suffering. A
world full of routes to misery could, in principle, be a broad world. The wager
holds only if the breadth that dominates in practice is generative breadth rather
than pathological breadth.

### Why we expect the wager to hold

There are three reasons to expect pathological breadth to lose.

The first is that domination is excisive. A dominating process secures itself by
closing other processes' routes, which by Ashby's fork means removing branching
from everything around it. Unless the dominator's own repertoire grows by more
than everyone else's shrinks, total breadth falls, and a single controller
generally cannot use everything it closes off to others.

The second is that suffering is a signal of narrowing. Control-theoretic
accounts of emotion treat affect as an organism's signal of how well it is
progressing toward its goals (Carver and Scheier 1990). The program generalizes
this into a functional account: pleasure and suffering are an organism's
internal report on whether its own futures are widening or narrowing, and a
signal is aversive just in case it disposes its bearer to move away from its
source (chapter 6). A world of pervasive suffering is, by that signal, a world in
which the futures of its valuers are contracting. Even suffering that is
deliberately engineered redirects behaviour toward avoidance and withdrawal, and
so narrows futures in turn.

The third is that the prediction cuts in both directions, which makes it
informative rather than convenient. If the wager is right, lushness-optimal
worlds should still contain aversive signalling that tracks narrowing, because
adaptation under load requires it. In valuers for whom such signals are
experienced, that signalling is suffering. A world with no aversive signalling
at all could not detect its own errors, and it should score worse.

### What would decide it

The program's most important test is adversarial. It consists in building models
of the structure, searching them for high-lushness configurations that are
torture worlds, dominated worlds or other recognizably bad end states, and
determining whether any such configuration competes with generative ones. The
search should pay particular attention to configurations engineered so that
suffering is decoupled from narrowing futures, since those are where the second
reason above would fail. Three targets are decisive.

- **The isomorphic pair.** This would be two worlds with identical branching
  structure at every resolution, one recognizably good and the other recognizably
  bad. If two worlds are identical in their full physical structure, then nothing
  physical distinguishes them, and either their moral difference has no physical
  basis or our labels for them track nothing real. The program predicts that no
  such pair is physically realizable.
- **Breadth-neutral domination.** This would be a world in which access is
  concentrated in a few at no cost to total lushness. It is the more realistic
  danger. The program predicts that concentration always costs breadth, for the
  reasons given above.
- **Obstructed suffering.** This is the sharpest form of the isomorphic pair. On
  the functional account a signal is suffering only if it disposes its bearer to
  escape its source, so a "suffering" that changes nothing is either not
  functioning as suffering at all, or is suffering whose escape is blocked. In
  the second case the blocking is itself a closure, and in real organisms
  inescapable suffering leaves lasting developmental narrowing (chapter 6). The
  program predicts that a world of obstructed suffering is less lush than the
  same world with the same obstruction and no suffering. Chapter 6 tests this in
  an exact model, and reports both where the prediction holds and where the
  comparison is delicate.

All three tests have to be assessed at the furthest horizon that can be resolved.
Example E2 in chapter 4 showed that at short horizons ruin can outscore
maintenance, because failure modes are genuine branches that lushness counts. A
test that stops too early can therefore be won by ruin.

### What failure would mean

If any of these targets is found in some regime, the wager fails in that regime.
Lushness would remain a well-defined physical measure; what would fail is its
use as the measure of value there. Gradient Ethics would then have to restrict
its claim to the regimes in which the wager holds, or identify what the measure
is missing. The program is built so that this outcome is possible, rather than
excluded by definition.

One case lies outside every test: suffering that is felt but makes no physical
difference of any kind. No physical account can register such suffering,
including any account a critic might prefer, since accounts that agree on every
physical consequence cannot be distinguished by those consequences. Chapter 6
states this as the boundary of the program, not as a result in its favour.

## 5.7 Who counts

At the start of the analysis, every process that has consequences is admitted as
a potential valuer. Familiar organisms, conscious report and declared preference
are not entry conditions. Admission does not establish that a process actually
values anything, or how much it counts. Its purpose is to keep the account from
excluding unfamiliar forms by definition.

The same principle keeps the account from excluding destructive agents by
definition. A process that destroys others may still be a valuer. If the ethics
defined only benign processes as valuers, it would win its arguments by deciding
in advance who was allowed to lose them. Moral standing also need not depend on
agency: a process can matter without being able to act on its own behalf.

In the thin sense of section 5.3, every vortex is a valuer: cells, organisms,
institutions, flames, and forms not yet recognized, at whatever scale they occur.
Status settles nothing about weight. How much a valuer counts is set by lushness.

This invites a misreading that must be ruled out at once: that the program ranks
people by their productivity. It does not, for three reasons internal to the
framework. First, a valuer's own futures are part of the structure. The
branching of a human life — its experiences, relationships, learning, and the
possibilities it holds open — is part of the lushness at stake, not only what the
person produces for others. Second, ending any trajectory is an irreversible
harm in the sense of chapter 6: it forecloses everything that trajectory would
have done, at every later step. Third, the comparison that ranking lives would
require is exactly the complete comparison no real agent holds (chapter 8). The
grading is real, but it is a grading of complete developments by the measure,
and no agent can read it off anyone's present usefulness.

## 5.8 The vision of Gradient Ethics

The program's ethics is called Gradient Ethics because of the gradient that makes
everything else possible. The universe is far from equilibrium, and that
difference — the free-energy gradient between its present state and its
maximum entropy — is what stars, life, minds and civilizations spend (chapter 3).
The gradient will be spent whatever anyone does. Gradient Ethics is a view about
how it should be spent.

> **Gradient Ethics asks three things of every agent.**
>
> **Protect.** Never destroy the conditions on which valuing itself depends.
> Beyond them, protect particular structures in proportion to how uncertain
> their dependencies are and how irreversible their loss would be. No one knows
> where value lies, and whatever is destroyed may be what mattered and may not
> come back.
>
> **Explore.** Learn which interventions widen what can follow, through
> reversible experiments, diversified approaches and several routes kept open,
> so that mistakes stay recoverable.
>
> **Generate.** Spend the gradient on what compounds, committing as far as the
> evidence for the consequences supports, and leave the world more generative
> than you found it. A richer structure forecloses fewer of the ways that value
> can come to be realized.

The three are not a strict order of priority, except at one point. Strict
protection is a constraint. By dominance (section 5.4), destroying the
conditions of valuing defeats every valued end that ranks a world without
valuers lowest, so no expected gain can license it. Everywhere else, protection
and generation are weighed in a single comparison, and what sets the balance is
what the agent's frame can justify.

Protection gains force where consequential dependencies remain uncertain.
Destroying a structure can eliminate dependencies and opportunities the agent has
not yet understood, and the asymmetry between recoverable and irreversible error
makes that loss weigh more the less reversible it is: a harm that permanently
lowers the generative rate eventually outweighs any harm whose cost is a finite
offset (chapter 6). An action that destroys existing structure in the
expectation of greater generativity later trades a loss it can see for a gain it
is wagering on, and the trade gets worse the less reversible the loss. Some
disruptions do turn out to be generative, but that can usually be known only in
hindsight, and the possibility does not license irreversible disruption in
advance.

Generative intervention gains justification as its effects become better
established: as the agent learns which interventions widen continuation, through
what mechanism and at what cost. Protection can then include substantial
transformation rather than preservation of whatever already exists. Exploration
connects the two halves. It improves knowledge of what is possible while
preserving the capacity to revise course, so uncertainty need not mean
inactivity. It usually recommends reversible steps, several approaches at once
and routes kept open, and confidence supports larger commitments as the evidence
for their consequences grows.

Two clarifications keep the balance from being misread. Uncertainty affects a
bounded agent's warranted comparison, not the physical lushness itself: a
poorly understood structure does not acquire extra breadth because we are
ignorant of it, but our ignorance makes any claim that its destruction is
beneficial less secure. And caution is not a separate reward added to the
measure. It is what the measure recommends to an agent that cannot yet see the
consequences of an act it cannot undo. Chapter 7 gives an exact case (section
7.3). Where a commons cannot robustly feed two agents and its capacity is fixed,
protecting the whole narrowly outweighs sustaining both; where coexistence can
open new channels, sustaining both wins once the chance of new generativity,
multiplied by its value, exceeds the protection margin. Neither side receives a
free pass: an irreversible loss and a speculative gain each require a calibrated
model of what remains possible.

In operational form, the vision becomes a decision rule. Never destroy the
conditions of valuing. Among the remaining options, prefer the one with greater
lushness, assessed at the furthest horizon you can resolve. Commit no further
than the evidence for an option's consequences supports, and require more
evidence the less reversible the option is; where the comparison cannot be
resolved, prefer the option that keeps learning and revision possible. The first
clause is derived; the second is the central wager joined to the horizon
condition of chapter 4; the third is the precautionary tier of section 5.4. How
evidence, uncertainty and recoverability should together set the strength of a
commitment is the task of chapter 9's decision theory.

Gradient Ethics is a maximizing view under constraint. It seeks the greatest
lushness possible within this universe while recognizing that no real agent
holds the complete comparison. Restraint has no value in itself, but under
uncertainty it is the warranted choice whenever losses cannot be undone and
their consequences cannot yet be seen. The familiar moral norms —
non-excision, cooperation, division of labour, prudence, triage rather than
destruction, and a balance between exploration and exploitation — are
predictions of the measure, and chapters 6 and 7 test them. The limits that
bounded knowledge places on any agent's licence to act are drawn in chapter 8,
and chapter 9 turns the vision into a decision theory.

## Formal notes

*Written for readers who want the definitions in full.*

### F5.1 Strict protection

Let 𝓗 be a set of hypotheses about where value is realized, and let V_h(a) be
the realized value of action a under hypothesis h. Let S* denote the conditions
of valuing: the minimal physical conditions under which any valuer can exist.
Suppose the valued end requires that some valuer exist and ranks the absence of
all valuers lowest. Then under every h any action d destroying S* satisfies
V_h(d) ≤ V_h(a) for every action a that preserves it, so d is weakly dominated
by every S*-preserving action, and strictly dominated wherever some
S*-preserving action realizes positive value under some h. No probability
measure on 𝓗 is needed. The premises are step 2, that valuers are physical
processes, and the ranking condition just stated. Ends for which some
S*-preserving action is worse than d under some h, as negative utilitarianism
permits (Smart 1958), violate the ranking condition and are not covered. The
argument does not extend to a particular substructure P ⊂ S, since for a given P
there are in general hypotheses h with V_h(d_P) = V_h(a) for the action d_P
destroying P.

### F5.2 Option value

Under uncertainty with learning and irreversibility, the expected value of
keeping the ability to choose later exceeds the expected value of committing now
by the quasi-option value (Arrow and Fisher 1974; Henry 1974). The effect
requires that information arrive before the option lapses and that the
foreclosing action be irreversible. The central wager extends this from a single
decision maker's options to the structure from which valuers and their options
arise; because that extension is not a theorem, the claim is a wager.

### F5.3 Instrumental convergence

Omohundro (2008) and Bostrom (2012) argued that agents with a wide range of final
goals share instrumental subgoals, including self-preservation and the
acquisition of resources. Turner and colleagues (2021) proved, for Markov
decision processes and under symmetry conditions on the distribution of reward
functions, that optimal policies tend to seek power in the formal sense of
keeping more options available. The same results predict acquiring other
agents' options, so they support the footing of the metavalue without supporting
restraint.

### F5.4 Ends that extend through time

An end E is time-extended if realizing it requires some state of affairs at a
time later than the moment of commitment. For such an E, the conditions of E
include the persistence, until those times, of some process that realizes or
carries it, and conditional transmission yields a commitment to that
persistence. The ends for which this fails are those realized entirely at the
moment of commitment, which excludes every end that requires pursuit.

### F5.5 Vortices

A vortex is a physical organization O that (i) persists over an interval while
its constituents are replaced, (ii) is maintained by a throughput of matter or
free energy drawn from a gradient, and (iii) would dissolve if that throughput
ceased. Conditions (ii) and (iii) distinguish vortices from static persistence.
Dissipative structures (Nicolis and Prigogine 1977) and autopoietic systems
(Maturana and Varela 1980) are established cases. Vortices can be nested, with
the constituents of one being vortices at a finer scale. In the thin sense of
section 5.3, O values a distinction X when the persistence of O depends on X and
the dynamics of O respond to X. Valuing in this sense implies neither experience
nor any particular moral weight.

### F5.6 Precautionary protection

Let d_P irreversibly destroy a particular structure P, and let preservation cost
a finite offset c in log lushness. Let p > 0 be the agent's credence that some
valued development depends on P, and suppose that under that dependence d_P
lowers the long-run rate by Δh > 0 (chapter 6, F6.2). The expected loss from
d_P at horizon n is at least p·(nΔh + O(1)), which exceeds c for all
n > c/(pΔh) + O(1). The conclusion holds within the resolvable horizon n̄ only
when n̄ exceeds this threshold, and it reverses when preserving P itself lowers
the rate. Credences below the scale set by bounded fanaticism (chapter 9) do not
contribute. The resulting obligation is graded in p, Δh, c and n̄. Chapter 7's
commons (section 7.3, F7.4) gives an exact instance with a valuer in place of P
and an unbounded horizon: the break-even δ* ≈ D/(Δn·G) has the same form, with
the protection margin D as the cost and Δn·G as the expected value at stake.

## For alignment

This chapter answers two questions an alignment reader will ask, and leaves one
open.

**Why would a capable system accept the standard at all?** It need not share
anyone's values. If it has ends that extend through time, it is committed to its
own persistence, and through its dependencies to the structure. Conditional
normativity is not an extra requirement imposed on such a system; it is the
instrumental rationality it already has.

**Why does that not simply license power-seeking?** Persistence on its own does
push toward acquiring options, including other agents' options, as the
instrumental-convergence results predict. What restrains this is precautionary
protection — uncertainty about which parts of the structure the system will
depend on, joined to the asymmetry between recoverable and irreversible error —
together with the requirement to commit no further than its evidence supports
and the limits of its own knowledge, which chapter 8 turns into limits on
licence and chapter 9 into a requirement of corrigibility.

**What remains open is installation.** Gradient Ethics specifies what an aligned
system should protect and prefer. Making a system actually hold that standard is
a separate problem, and the program does not claim to have solved it.
