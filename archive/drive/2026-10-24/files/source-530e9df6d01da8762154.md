Chapter 2 argued that whatever can matter happens through some physical
development, and that what can follow from a present is defined before anyone
is chosen to count. This chapter builds that object, Omega, from the present
outward, and develops [Overview section 1](#overview-1). It places each idea
in Omega as it arrives, and ends with access, the concept through which
organization, harm and choice enter the rest of the manuscript. The chapter
combines the program's definitions with established results of physics, each
under its stated assumptions. It defends no comparison and makes no ethical
claim. The program's ethics falls out of the study of this object, through two
further steps argued in Part III: value requires valuers, and the central
thesis.

## 3.1 Everything starts from a present

**What can follow is always what can follow from a present. The program's
object is built from there.**

Every assessment in the program starts from a present: the way some part of
the world is now. The program is present-rooted in method, and three
commitments make that precise.
- **Every assessment is made from a present.** What can follow is always what
  can follow from some state of things now.
- **The past acts only through traces.** Whatever bearing the past has on
  what follows, it has through the physical traces it left in the present.
- **Futures are what a present can give rise to.** What follows is defined
  from the present and the law that carries it forward, not taken as a
  separate given.

These are commitments of method, not a metaphysical thesis. The program does
not assume that only the present exists. Relativity singles out no universal
present, and the physical theories taken up in [chapter 29](#chapter-29)
differ about what ultimately exists; the program treats each as a way of
describing one object.

A state cannot be a present on its own. To have something before it and
something that can follow, it needs three things:
1. an **orientation**, which gives before and after (section 3.2);
2. a **frame**, whose present it is (section 3.3);
3. a **law**, which fixes what it can give rise to (section 3.5).

Section 3.4 states exactly what a present must contain. Gathered from every
present, what can follow is **Omega**, the whole physical structure of what
can follow (section 3.6). Each idea is placed in Omega as it arrives, in a box
headed "In Omega".

## 3.2 Orientation

**Nothing follows from anything unless occurrences are ordered into before
and after. That order is what lets a state be a present.**

"What follows from a present" assumes that some occurrences come after
others. Remove that assumption and the phrase has no content: there are
states of the world, but nothing is a continuation of anything else. A
present therefore needs an orientation.

> **Plain-English definition 3.1: Orientation**
>
> The order of before and after along which one occurrence can follow
> another. It assumes nothing more: not that the law is irreversible, and not
> that every pair of events is ordered.

Three points fix what orientation does and does not include.
- **It orders occurrences, not kinds of state.** A system can pass from a
  state to another and back again. The same state then occurs twice, at two
  places along the orientation, and the order is between the occurrences.
  Where events are spacelike separated, the order can leave them unordered:
  it is an order of before and after where there is a before and after, not
  a single line through everything.
- **It does not require irreversibility.** A law under which every step could
  be undone still orders its steps, and still has a future once they are
  ordered. Irreversibility enters separately, as the case in which what an
  act removes is never restored. Chapters 6 and 17 show how much that
  changes.
- **It is an order, not a law.** It says which occurrences can come after
  which. How likely each continuation is comes from the law (section 3.5).

Orientation is not taken as basic. [Chapter 28](#chapter-28) gives the
program's proposed account of where it comes from: Alpha, in which
distinctions, the relations between them, and an asymmetry in those relations
give before and after. That account belongs to the program's cosmology. The
argument of Parts II to VII needs only the result, and no physical test
depends on the account. The general construction presupposes an orientation;
each result in Appendix A adds the dynamical and mathematical assumptions
stated with it ([section A.1](#a-1)).

> **In Omega**
>
> Orientation gives Omega its direction. Everything that can follow from a
> present follows along it.

## 3.3 Frames

**What follows is always what follows for something. The standpoint it is
defined from is a frame.**

What can follow for a single particle over the next hour is one set of
possibilities. What can follow for a cell that contains the particle, over
the same hour and at a compatible level of detail, is another, and what can
follow for the planet is a third. Each is defined from a standpoint of its
own: the particle's state, the cell's, the planet's. A future without such a
standpoint is undefined, because there is nothing for it to be the future of.

> **Plain-English definition 3.2: Frame**
>
> A physical standpoint within the world: something with a present of its
> own, from which what follows is defined. A particle, a cell, a person and
> the whole world each have one.

Frames have five properties that the rest of the manuscript uses.
- **Frames are physical.** A frame is a part of the world: a physical locus
  with a present of its own. An analyst can write down a description of a
  region without anything in the world corresponding to it; what makes a
  description a frame is the physics that realizes it. A frame need not keep
  records. A particle keeps none, and the whole world has nothing outside it
  to couple to; both are frames. Records enter where a construction uses
  them: in quantum theory, a frame's couplings and the records they form fix
  the law of what it records (section 3.5).
- **A frame's present sits on a section.** The development of the whole world
  can be cut, along the orientation, into sections: in a relativistic setting,
  appropriate spacelike sections, where the spacetime admits the required
  construction; in a finite model, the state at a declared step. A frame's
  present is specified on such a section, and what follows from it runs to
  later sections ([section A.1](#a-1)).
- **Frames can be nested and can overlap.** The particle is inside the cell,
  the cell inside the organism, the organism inside the planet; a family
  overlaps its members, and an institution the people in it. Over a common
  horizon and at a compatible level of detail, the larger frame's continuation
  is a joint continuation, and following the smaller frame within it recovers
  the smaller frame's continuation. Nesting does not mean that the larger
  frame always has more breadth; chapter 8 states exactly how the two relate.
- **There is a widest frame.** The whole world is a frame, the **ultimate
  frame**. It contains every other frame, and no part of the world occupies
  it ([section A.1](#a-1); [Definition A.7.1](#a-7-1)).
- **What exactly makes a composite system a frame is open.** For a particle
  or for the whole world the answer is plain. For composite systems with
  moving or porous boundaries, a criterion is still to be found
  ([chapter 33](#chapter-33)). No result in this chapter depends on where the
  line falls.

Frames are also where value will be found. [Chapter 9](#chapter-9) argues
that value requires valuers, that anything with a frame of its own is a
valuer, and that valuing, in this thin sense, is a structural relation
between a frame and its futures. Nothing in this chapter depends on that
argument. It is noted here because the frame is introduced here.

> **In Omega**
>
> Every future in Omega is a future of some frame. From each of its
> presents, the ultimate frame's futures are everything in Omega that can
> follow that present; every other frame's futures are part of that.

## 3.4 What a present contains

**A frame's present is a physical specification of its state, full enough
for the law to fix what can follow.**

The present has to be **sufficient**: it has to contain everything the law
needs to fix the continuation law being assessed. That includes positions and
momenta, fields, chemical stocks, records, correlations with the surroundings,
and the state of any apparatus that will act. An effective model may leave out
microscopic detail, but it has to keep whatever its own law of motion
requires.

What that includes depends on the physics.
- **In a finite classical model,** the present is the full state of the
  model, and the law gives the probability of every continuation from it.
- **In quantum theory,** the program assesses what physical frames can record.
  The present is then the quantum state, the dynamics, the couplings that are
  actually in place, and the correlations with the environment that matter
  for later records. From these, quantum theory fixes the probabilities of
  the records a frame can form (section 3.5).

> **Plain-English definition 3.3: Present**
>
> A frame's state now, specified fully enough for the law to fix what can
> follow from it, including the frame's couplings and its correlations with
> its surroundings.

Four consequences follow.
- **Two routes, one present.** A process cannot tell from its present how it
  arrived, unless the route left a trace. Two routes to the same complete
  physical state have the same continuations. The past is not lost: it
  remains in Omega, where it can be asked about. It is not an extra
  ingredient of the present.
- **Forgetting and erasing differ.** An analyst who forgets something about a
  system has not changed the system's present. Erasing a physical memory
  does change it: that memory no longer supplies the record to the processes
  that read it. Other copies, or routes to reconstructing the record, may
  remain, each with its own physical requirements, and under unitary
  dynamics the information may have moved into correlations with the
  environment rather than vanished. The difference matters for access
  (section 3.8).
- **A part's futures depend on its surroundings.** A cell's next hour depends
  on its nutrients, its neighbours and its temperature, not on the cell
  alone. So a frame inside the world has its futures defined by its own
  history, conditioned on the present of the whole world it sits in
  ([Definition A.7.1](#a-7-1)). A description of a part alone is usually not
  sufficient, because what follows for the part depends on correlations it
  shares with its surroundings. [Chapter 8](#chapter-8) develops this.
- **Spacelike records do not depend on the order of application.** Where two
  frames make records at spacelike separation, using local operations of the
  kind the theory allows, the joint law of their records does not depend on
  which is applied first ([Proposition A.14.8](#a-14-8)). That does not make
  every choice of sections, horizons or records give the same verdict; the
  general question of comparisons across quantum reference frames is open
  ([chapter 29](#chapter-29)).

> **Note: conditioning on a hidden configuration**
>
> Some descriptions of physics add variables that no record can fix exactly.
> In Bohmian mechanics, the exact configuration of every particle is such a
> variable: under quantum equilibrium no subsystem can record its own
> configuration more finely than its wavefunction allows (Dürr, Goldstein
> and Zanghì 1992). Conditioning the present additionally on that exact
> configuration is a different construction from the one above. Under
> deterministic guidance it leaves one way to go on from every present, so
> the perplexity of complete histories would equal one for every option, and
> every comparison by that measure would be a tie. Other structural
> comparisons would remain possible. The program uses the
> construction above, in which the record law is fixed by the quantum state,
> dynamics and couplings, and names the other wherever it is meant.
> [Chapter 29](#chapter-29) takes up Bohmian mechanics as one description of
> Omega among several, and whether the encompassing frame should ever
> condition on such variables is listed in [chapter 33](#chapter-33). Under a
> fully deterministic classical law, the construction above also leaves one
> continuation from every present; the program's models have stochastic
> laws, and the world's law, as far as physics knows, is quantum.

> **In Omega**
>
> Omega contains every present of every frame. Each present is a starting
> point, and the part of Omega that unfolds from it is what follows from that
> present.

## 3.5 What a present gives rise to

**From a specified present, the law fixes what can follow. Classical
histories, and the records physical frames make, carry probabilities.**

### The law

The third thing a present needs is the law: the actual dynamics of the world,
or of the physical model that stands in for it. It is not a menu of actions, a
list of tasks or a set of scenarios someone finds interesting. Choices,
controllers and agents enter only as physical processes governed by the same
law. A person deciding, a thermostat switching and a reaction proceeding are
all parts of what the law carries forward.

In most situations the law does not fix one outcome. A radioactive nucleus
may decay in the next second or it may not, with a probability physics
specifies. Larger systems show the same thing at the level of detail at which
we can describe them: a seed in a given patch of soil may grow or rot, a
small population may spread or die out. Each way things can go has a
probability.

### Histories, not end points

What follows from a present is not a list of places the world might end up.
In a classical model, and for the records a frame makes, it is a set of
complete histories: the full sequence of states passed through.

> **Plain-English definition 3.4: History**
>
> One complete way things can go on from a present: the sequence of states,
> or of recorded outcomes, passed through, not just where it ends.

The histories that can follow a present, with their probabilities, are its
**futures**. "Future" is used in this plain sense throughout. An option an
agent faces is a physically implemented way of going on from its present, and
each option has futures of its own; comparing them begins in
[chapter 5](#chapter-5).

The difference between histories and end points matters before any ethics is
stated. Take a system that stays idle, and the same system making an
excursion and returning to the same state of its own. The system's end
states agree. The histories do not: during the excursion, the system coupled
to its surroundings, used resources and occupied apparatus that something
else might have used, and its surroundings may carry the traces. A
description that keeps only the system's end state treats the two as the
same. The histories keep both. In the same way, a delay that made a rescue
impossible is part of what happened, even if the final state matches that of
a timely rescue, and a loss followed by recovery is not the same history as
no loss at all.

Three further distinctions keep histories honest.
- **Reconvergence, and its limits.** In a classical model, different
  histories can reach the same later state. From the original present they
  remain two histories with their own probabilities; from the later state,
  taken as a new present, what follows is the same for both. Both statements
  are true, and ordinary conditioning links them. Quantum theory is different
  in two ways. Under one unitary evolution, two distinct complete states of
  the whole world never become the same state, so reconvergence happens only
  in a part, in a coarser description, or in an account of records. And
  coherent alternatives that recombine are not two histories that meet: their
  amplitudes interfere.
- **Ending is not unknowing.** The history of a process can end: a machine
  halts, an organism dies, a reaction stops. That is a real outcome with its
  own probability, and the wider physical development goes on after it. An
  unfinished calculation is different: it marks the edge of what has been
  computed, not an end of what follows.
- **Only what the law permits.** The histories are those the physical law
  allows, not everything that can be described. What a formal model admits,
  what physics realizes, and where experience occurs are three different
  questions, and they stay distinct throughout the manuscript.

> **Primer: probability over histories**
>
> In a model with finitely many states, the law can be written as a table K:
> the entry K(x, y) is the probability that the next state is y, given that
> the present state is x. If the state is the full, sufficient state of the
> model, the probability of a whole history is the product of the
> probabilities of its steps, because each step depends only on the state it
> starts from. This is the Markov property of a complete state description
> (Norris 1997). Two cautions. A part's own history, read without the rest
> of the state, usually lacks this property, because what happens to it next
> depends on things outside it ([chapter 8](#chapter-8)). And in continuous
> time, the probability of a history also depends on how long it waits in
> each state, so rates and waiting times take the place of step
> probabilities. Conditioning on part of a history, such as "the system has
> reached state c", gives a new law for what follows from there.

> \[
> P_x(x_1,\dots,x_n)=\prod_{t=0}^{n-1}K(x_t,x_{t+1}),\qquad x_0=x .
> \]
>
> *In words:* in a finite classical model whose state is complete, the
> probability of a history from a present is the product of the
> probabilities of its steps.

The law at every state matters, not only at the first step. Two models can
agree on what happens next and disagree entirely one step later. The futures
of a present are this whole law over histories, at every horizon
([section A.1](#a-1)).

### The quantum case

In quantum theory the present includes a quantum state, and alternatives
carry amplitudes rather than probabilities. Amplitudes can reinforce or
cancel, so a quantum process is not simply a list of weighted branches, and
drawing its paths as separate outcomes to be counted misdescribes it.

Probabilities attach to records. When a distinction becomes correlated with
the surroundings, and the states of the surroundings that carry it become
distinguishable, interference between the alternatives is suppressed for
processes that cannot reach and act on the relevant part of the
environment. How far it is suppressed depends on how distinguishable those
environmental states are and on which operations are available. This is
decoherence, and alternatives that have decohered behave as ordinary
outcomes with ordinary probabilities (Gell-Mann and Hartle 1993; Zurek 2003).

Decoherence does not by itself say which alternatives count as distinct. The
same quantum state gives one effective alternative under one question and two
under another ([Proposition A.14.1](#a-14-1); Dowker and Kent 1996). A frame
settles it. A frame's own couplings fix what it records, and quantum theory
then gives the probabilities of the recorded histories
([Proposition A.14.3](#a-14-3)). Everything said above about histories
applies to them unchanged. Different frames' record laws need not admit a
common physically realizable history. Whether they do depends on the
compatibility of their couplings and records, not on whether the frames
agree; where they do not, the comparison between their descriptions is
unresolved ([Proposition A.14.7](#a-14-7)). [Chapter 29](#chapter-29)
develops the quantum case.

## 3.6 Omega

**Gathered from every present, what can follow is one physical object.**

Now take this from the present of every frame, at every moment, for the whole
world. In a classical model the picture is a branching one: from each way
things can go in the next moment, more ways open in the moment after, each
with its probability. In quantum theory the underlying process is not a tree
of weighted branches, but every frame's records still carry probabilities,
fixed by the process. Either way, the result is not a guess or a model of
anyone's opinion. It is one definite physical object.

> **Plain-English definition 3.5: Omega**
>
> The whole physical structure of what can follow, from every present, taken
> as one object. From each present, its law fixes what can follow, and the
> histories that physical frames can record carry probabilities.

Omega is one physical whole. Describing it from many frames, or from many
sections, does not multiply it. Different frames describe the same physical
structure from different standpoints. Their record histories need not be
jointly realizable, and where they are not, that is a fact about the frames'
couplings and records, not a second world.

Omega is easy to mistake for four other things.
- **It is not a prediction.** A prediction names what will happen. Omega
  contains everything that can.
- **It is not a belief.** A belief belongs to someone and can be mistaken.
  Omega's probabilities are set by physical law, not by anyone's credence.
- **It is not a ranking or a utility.** Nothing in its definition says that
  any history is better than another. Whatever ordering the program finds, it
  finds in the structure of Omega, not in an assumption about what is good.
- **It adds nothing to physics.** It contains no extra substance and no
  extra force. Nothing in it steers the world towards more possibility.
  Catalysts, reservoirs, interfering amplitudes and deliberating organisms
  shape it through their ordinary physical relations.

Different physical theories describe Omega differently. Standard quantum
theory, Bohmian mechanics and the many-worlds reading give the same
probabilities for records, and differ about what is ultimately actual.
Collapse theories modify the dynamics and predict small departures from
standard quantum theory; experiments constrain those departures, and some
versions and parameter ranges are already excluded (Donadi et al. 2021;
Carlesso et al. 2022). The program treats each theory as a way of describing
Omega, not as an identification of it ([chapter 29](#chapter-29)).

Omega is far too large to write down, and no question in the program needs it
written down. Every question compares a few options from the same present,
and what matters is the part of Omega the options affect. Parts the options
leave unchanged and independent of the rest drop out of the comparison.
Parts left unchanged but correlated with what the options affect do not drop
out, because the correlations stay in the comparison; [chapter 8](#chapter-8)
states exactly when the rest drops out, and [chapter 10](#chapter-10) shows
that the comparison stays finite even when the unchanged world outside is
infinite. In the small exact models that test the thesis, the part that
matters is computed in full ([chapter 12](#chapter-12)).

> **In Omega**
>
> Omega is the object. Every later concept in this manuscript is defined from
> it: what follows from a present is a part of it, a measure of how much
> follows is a measure of that part, and every comparison sets two parts of it
> against each other.

## 3.7 Why present stocks do not determine what follows

**No stock quantity of the present, such as energy, order or stored
information, settles what can follow. What follows depends on the dynamics
and on how the world is organized.**

Once the law is fixed, what follows from a present is fixed by the present.
So any measure of what follows, including the one [chapter 4](#chapter-4)
derives, is a function of the present, given the law. That is not the idea
this section rejects. It rejects a different idea: that options can be ranked
by some stock the present holds, read off without asking what the law does
with it.

The idea is natural. Rank options by the order the world is left in, the
energy left available, or the information stored. Each fails for the same
reason: the consequences of a stock depend on the dynamics and on the
organization around it.
- **Order.** Two presents with the same order can differ in what follows,
  depending on what can use that order and how. A crystal and a cell can both
  be highly ordered. What follows from them differs enormously.
- **Energy.** A store of energy matters through what it drives. The same
  store opens different continuations in the presence of machinery that can
  use it and in its absence, and in either case its own thermal dynamics
  belong to what follows ([chapter 4](#chapter-4)).
- **Information.** Under the unitary dynamics of standard quantum theory, the
  von Neumann entropy of the whole world's state does not change (Nielsen and
  Chuang 2000). Every option, carried forward, leaves that quantity where it
  was. So it cannot rank options. Other quantities called information do
  change: correlations between parts, and what any part can access. That is
  where the differences lie.

Two options that leave the same global quantity can differ greatly in how
the whole is organized: in their correlations, their records, and which
developments the parts of the world can reach and with what probability. The
differences between options lie there, in what can follow and for whom. That
is the structure the program studies.

## 3.8 Access

**What a part of the world can reach is set by machinery, records,
resources, couplings and repair. These act through the law. They never
multiply the measure.**

Section 3.7 located the differences between options in what parts of the
world can reach. This section names that.

> **Plain-English definition 3.6: Access**
>
> The physically realized availability of continuation: which developments a
> part of the world can actually reach, and how likely each is, given its
> machinery, records, resources, couplings and capacity for repair.

Access is not a quantity added to Omega. It is how the law plays out from a
particular present. Machinery that can catalyse a reaction makes that
reaction's continuations more likely. A record that a controller can read
lets its next step depend on what it reads. Resources consumed by one process
are not available to the next. Each of these changes which histories occur
and how likely each is, and it does so through the ordinary dynamics. When
[chapter 4](#chapter-4) measures how much can follow, access has already done
its work in the law; nothing multiplies or weights the count by access
afterwards.

### What an access claim contains

A claim that something is accessible names a physical route, and everything
the route involves:
- **the preparation** needed before the route can be used;
- **the records** available along the way;
- **the resources** consumed: energy, material, apparatus time, space;
- **the time** it takes;
- **the probability** that the route succeeds;
- **the state it leaves behind** for whatever comes next.

Probabilities compose along a route. If preparing an apparatus succeeds with
probability p, and using it succeeds with probability q given a successful
preparation, the route succeeds with probability pq. Costs compose too, but
not as one number: sequential work adds, peak occupancy takes a maximum, and a
stock can be replenished. Taking the cheapest entry in each column from
different implementations can describe a combination that no single
implementation achieves.

### Permitted, ready, reachable

Three questions about any transformation are easily run together.
1. **Is it permitted?** The laws allow it.
2. **Is it ready?** Something exists that performs it reliably.
3. **Is it reachable?** That something can actually be obtained from here,
   with the resources, time and records available.

Each is a separate physical fact. A transformation can be permitted with
nothing ready to perform it, and an apparatus can be possible in principle
with no route to building it. Readiness is a property of the present; the
route to readiness is a property of what follows from it. The distinction
between what the laws permit and what a physical constructor can reliably
bring about is also the starting point of constructor theory (Deutsch 2013;
Deutsch and Marletto 2015). We call the physical account that backs an
access claim, covering the controller, the preparation, the resources, the
boundary conditions and the resulting law, a **realization witness**.

Two further rules follow.
- **A controller acts only on what it has.** A control policy can depend only
  on records the controller actually holds, not on facts an analyst knows
  about its hidden state. An operation is available only if something
  physically implements it. Naming a reaction does not make it a choice; it
  stays part of the native dynamics until some process controls it.
- **Joint feasibility needs one witness.** Two repairs, each possible, may
  compete for the same unit of fuel. Any two of three processes may fit
  within a budget while all three do not. That several requirements can be
  met together does not follow from each being feasible, or even from every
  pair being feasible. It needs one physical development in which all hold
  at once.

### Access is not the record of the past

A frame's history grows over time. What it can reach need not. A record can
be lost to a process: the history still contains the fact, but the process
can no longer use it, either because the record has been physically erased
or because no route to it remains. Recovering its use needs a physical route
of its own, with its own delay and cost. An archive that an analyst holds
gives the process nothing.

Access over time also has several senses that must be kept apart: whether
and when a target is first reached, how much of the time it is occupied,
whether it is kept for as long as required, and whether it returns after
being lost. A process that reaches a target early and loses it at once scores
well on the first and badly on the rest. [Chapter 6](#chapter-6) uses these
distinctions to separate recovery from mere return.

### What access does not do

- **Inaccessible possibility adds nothing.** A transformation the laws permit
  but no route can reach from the present opens no future from that present.
  An accessible one opens futures even when it is unlikely to be used
  ([chapter 4](#chapter-4); [Proposition A.2.7](#a-2-7)).
- **Holding information is not having access.** Two arrangements can hold the
  same information and make very different things reachable. That is the
  subject of [chapter 14](#chapter-14).

> **In Omega**
>
> Access is how the law plays out from a present: the machinery, records,
> resources and couplings in that present decide which histories in Omega
> follow from it, and with what probabilities. It is not a second structure
> laid over Omega.

## 3.9 What the object gives the rest of the manuscript

**Omega fixes what chapter 2's four problems are about. The next chapters
answer them.**

Chapter 2 named four places where earlier attempts to ground value in
possibility stalled: what counts as a different option, how likely options
count, whose options, and whether noise counts as possibility. This chapter
has not answered them. It has fixed what they are questions about. The
options are continuations in Omega. Their likelihoods are the probabilities
physics assigns. The "whose" is a frame. And noise, like every other physical
process, is part of what follows. [Chapter 4](#chapter-4) measures how much
follows; [chapters 5 to 8](#chapters-5-8) follow that measure through time,
through resolution and through parts and wholes.

## 3.10 What this chapter does not claim

- **That comparison comes first.** Comparing options is something done within
  the account, from [chapter 5](#chapter-5) on, not a premise of it.
- **That any future is better than another.** Omega carries no ranking. The
  ordering the program studies is found in it later, and the claim that our
  judgements of good and harm track that ordering is the central thesis
  ([chapter 11](#chapter-11)).
- **That orientation is basic.** It is presupposed here. The program's
  proposed account of where it comes from is in [chapter 28](#chapter-28).
- **That conditioning on hidden variables is never appropriate.** The
  program's construction of the present is named, and so is the alternative.
  Whether the encompassing frame should ever condition on variables no record
  can fix is open ([chapter 33](#chapter-33)).
- **That every composite system is a frame.** The criterion for composite
  frames is open ([chapter 33](#chapter-33)).
- **That frames' records always share one history.** Frames whose record
  constructions are incompatible need not have a common physically
  realizable history ([chapter 29](#chapter-29)).
- **That one physical theory describes Omega finally.** Quantum field theory
  is the best current description of matter, and the program's finite models
  are exact calibration cases, not claims about Omega's ultimate form.
