Ethics guides action. Every time we ask whether one outcome is better than
another, we need a standard of comparison. This chapter looks at the first
thing every standard does: it decides whose gains and losses count. It argues
that this decision comes before any comparison, that the standards disagree
about it at the edges, and that evidence cannot settle, by itself, which test
should decide it. It ends with three requirements that a different starting
point would have to meet.

The chapter does not argue that existing standards are wrong. Each captures
something real, and several have careful answers to hard cases. Its claim is
narrower: the test for who counts is where the standards part, and none of
them can establish its test for the others.

## 1.1 Every standard begins with who counts

**Each standard picks a kind of good, and the good sets the test for who
counts.**

Suppose we ask whether draining a wetland is better or worse than leaving it.
Better for whom, or for what? Before we can compare the two outcomes, we need
to know whose gains and losses enter the comparison. Every standard of good
answers that question, and it answers it first.

> **Plain-English definition 1.1: Standard of good**
>
> A way of judging whether one outcome is better than another. It says what
> matters, and so whose gains and losses count.

The standards differ in what they take the good to be, and each answer brings
its own test for who counts.

- **Welfare.** If the good is faring well, the beings that count are those
  that can fare well or badly. Bentham (1789) put the test in one question:
  can they suffer? Singer (1975) used it to argue that animals count.
- **Preference.** If the good is getting what one wants, the beings that count
  are those that want things.
- **Rights.** If the good is respect for rights, the beings that count are
  those that hold rights. In Kant's tradition, these are rational agents
  (Kant 1785). Other rights theories extend rights to animals (Regan 1983).
- **Contract.** If the good is what could be justified to others, the beings
  that count are those to whom justification is owed (Rawls 1971; Scanlon
  1998).
- **Capabilities.** If the good is what a being is able to do and to be, the
  beings that count are those with lives of their own to lead (Sen 1999).
  Nussbaum (2006) extends this to animals.
- **Life and land.** If the good is the flourishing of living things, every
  organism counts (Taylor 1986). If it is the health of a biotic community,
  the community counts as a whole (Leopold 1949).

Virtue ethics begins elsewhere, with the character of the one who acts. But it
meets the same question as soon as it asks to whom kindness or justice is
owed.

None of these choices is arbitrary. A standard built on suffering counts the
beings that can suffer because suffering matters to the one who suffers. A
standard built on agreement counts those who can be reasoned with because
justification is owed to someone who can receive it. Each test follows from a
view of what the good is, and each view has serious defenders.

But the pattern is the same in every case. The standard picks a kind of good
first. The kind of good then sets the test for who can have it. The test is in
place before any outcome is examined.

> **Plain-English definition 1.2: Standing**
>
> Being among those whose gains and losses a standard of good counts.

## 1.2 The hard cases shape real decisions

**The standards agree about many cases. They divide at the edges, and the
edges are where many decisions are made.**

For a healthy adult human, the standards mostly agree: she counts. The
disagreements begin further out.

**Insects.** Do they fare well or badly? A welfare standard needs to know
whether they can suffer, and the evidence is still argued over. Most rights
theories leave insects out, though some extend rights to animals more widely.
A standard built on life counts every insect.

**Rivers.** A river does not suffer, want or reason. Most standards leave it
out except through the people and animals that use it. Yet some legal systems
now treat rivers as persons with interests of their own. New Zealand did so
for the Whanganui River in 2017 (Te Awa Tupua Act 2017). Stone (1972) asked
the question decades earlier: should trees have standing?

**Future people.** They do not exist yet, and which people will exist depends
on what we do now. Parfit (1984) showed how hard this makes it to say that a
choice is worse for anyone in particular. Contract theories have developed
accounts of what is owed to future generations, such as Rawls's principle of
just savings (Rawls 1971). But those owed are not present to agree, and the
accounts remain contested. A welfare standard must decide how to count lives
that may never begin.

**Companies and institutions.** The law treats many of them as persons. They
act, own property and sue. Do they want things? Do they hold rights of their
own, or only on behalf of their members?

**Artificial agents.** A system that models the world, pursues goals and
adjusts its behaviour fits some standards' tests partly and others not at all.
Whether it can fare well or badly is unknown.

These are not puzzles for seminars only. Whether insects count affects how
pesticides are judged. Whether a river counts affects whether a dam is built.
Whether future people count, and how, affects every decision about climate.
Whether artificial agents count will affect how they are built and used. Each
standard has answers to some of these cases, and some of its answers are
careful. But no standard answers all of them in a way the others accept.

## 1.3 Why evidence cannot choose the test

**Evidence can show whether something passes a standard's test. It cannot
show, by itself, which test should decide who counts.**

Some disputes about standing can be settled. Whether insects can suffer is a
question of fact, and evidence can answer it. If the answer is yes, a welfare
standard counts them. The standard needs no revision to do so. Its test is
general, and the new evidence simply shows that insects pass it. That is real
progress.

But it is progress in applying a test, not in choosing one. A defender of a
rights standard can accept every fact about insect suffering and still deny
that insects hold rights. A defender of a life standard can count insects
whether they suffer or not. The three agree on the facts. They disagree about
which facts matter, and that is a disagreement about the good itself.

Argument still has work to do here. It can show that a standard contradicts
itself, or that its test gives results its own defenders reject. Arguments have
changed ethical standards, and they have also extended existing standards to
beings that were overlooked. What argument inside a standard cannot do is
compel someone who rejects that standard's test. A defender of welfare and a
defender of rights can each argue soundly from their own premises and reach
different verdicts about the same case. Neither has made a mistake the other
can point to without assuming their own test.

Philosophers have developed methods for working across standards. The most
important is reflective equilibrium (Rawls 1971). We test general principles
against our judgements about particular cases, and revise each in the light
of the other until they fit. Neither side is fixed. This manuscript uses the
method itself ([chapter 10](#chapter-10)). But the method starts from our
judgements about cases, and at the edges those judgements divide. It can
bring them into tension and move them. It has not yet brought the standards
to agree on who counts at the edges, and it does not supply a test that all
of them must accept.

## 1.4 Facts alone do not settle what is good

**No description of what happens, however complete, says by itself what
ought to happen. This manuscript accepts that.**

A natural hope is that science could settle the matter. If we knew enough
about the world, perhaps we could read off who counts and what is good. Hume
(1739) gave the classic reply. However much we know about what is, we cannot
conclude what ought to be without some further premise about value. More
detail adds facts. It does not add verdicts.

This manuscript accepts the gap. It does not claim to derive what is good
from a description of physics. Its route has two steps.

First, [chapter 3](#chapter-3) argues that value is a relation within the
physical world. Nothing can be better or worse unless it is better or worse
for something, and what connects the two is consequence: what happens next
depends on what is here now. Both sides of that relation are physical. It
follows that the futures still open from a present are where value can still
be gained or lost. That locates value without yet saying what is good.

Second, [chapter 10](#chapter-10) states the central thesis. Widening the
range of futures open to the world as a whole is a gain in value, and
narrowing it is a loss. This is not a proposal to find a physical stand-in
for our existing moral opinions. It is a claim about what value is, argued
from chapters 3 and 4.

The thesis can be put under pressure in three different ways. The physical
properties of the measure can be tested, as any physical claim can. The
argument that links the measure to value can be examined, step by step. And
the thesis's verdicts can be set against our judgements about cases,
including hard ones. Our judgements are evidence, not an infallible standard.
When a verdict and a judgement conflict, the fault may lie in the physical
model, in the measure, in the thesis, or in the judgement. Chapter 10 sets out
how such conflicts can be investigated. No disagreement identifies its own
cause, and deciding what one shows may take further argument and experiment.

So the manuscript does not cross Hume's gap by logic. It argues for a
connection across it and puts the connection to the test.

## 1.5 Why artificial agents make this practical

**Agents that act on a standard need one that does not depend on who built
them, and that keeps working as they learn.**

For most of history, the question of who counts was argued by philosophers,
lawyers and activists, and settled case by case in practice. That is
changing. We are building artificial agents that act in the world, and some
may come to understand it better than we do. Every such agent acts on some
standard for comparing outcomes, whether that standard is written down or
learned.

> **Primer: what an AI system optimizes**
>
> An AI system is built to pursue an objective. The objective may be a reward
> signal, a model of human preferences learned from data, or a set of rules.
> Each is a standard of comparison in the sense of this chapter, and each
> encodes a decision about whose values count (Gabriel 2020). One
> well-known difficulty is that a system can meet the letter of its objective
> while missing its point. Another is that a fixed objective may stop making
> sense as the system's model of the world improves. Russell (2019) argues
> that systems should remain uncertain about what people want, and defer to
> them.

Three things follow for the standard such an agent should use.

**It should not depend on whose preferences trained it.** A standard learned
from one group's choices carries that group's view of who counts. Another
group would have built a different standard. The verdicts would then depend
on who held the keyboard.

**It should favour no group.** This is not the same as the first point. A
standard could be built carefully and still count some beings and not others
for reasons that serve its builders.

**It should keep working as the agent's understanding grows.** A standard
written in terms of today's categories may stop applying when the agent learns
that those categories were wrong. If the agent discovers new kinds of beings,
or finds that familiar ones are not what we thought, the standard should
still give answers.

These requirements are distinct, and a standard can meet one while failing
another. An exclusion with no argument behind it favours some beings over
others, which the second forbids, even if it is precise and consistently
applied. A fixed list risks leaving out beings only because no one anticipated
them, which the third forbids. A standard with a general test can meet all
three. Its test can be impartial, independent of its builders and able to
include new kinds of beings. What it still needs is a reason why its test, and
not another, should decide who counts. That is the question of section 1.3, and
an artificial agent inherits it.

## 1.6 What a different starting point needs

**It must choose no group in advance, stay well defined and be testable.**

The previous sections suggest three requirements. They are demanding, and
this manuscript does not claim to meet them by assertion. Each is met, or
fails to be met, by later argument.

**No group chosen in advance.** This does not mean that a standard can avoid
saying who counts. Any useful standard must say. It means that the answer
must follow from an argument more basic than a chosen kind of good, and that
nothing is left out without a reason. [Chapters 3](#chapter-3) and
[4](#chapter-4) take this up. Chapter 3 argues that every physical system
counts. Chapter 4 argues that the comparison must then be made for the whole
world at once, with no part singled out.

**Well defined.** The standard must give an answer for any situation physics
allows, at any scale, and it must keep giving answers as knowledge grows. It
cannot rely on categories that may turn out to be wrong. Part II builds a
measure from physics with this aim, and states the conditions under which it
holds.

**Testable.** The standard must make claims that could turn out false, and
say how. A standard that can absorb any result says nothing. Part III states
the central thesis and how it is tested. Parts IV to VI test it against
organization, loss and many valuers, and Part IX collects what would count
against it.

The manuscript begins, then, with a physical object rather than a list. That
object is the ***universal possibility structure***: every way the universe
can continue from every present, and how likely each way is. The manuscript
calls it Omega, and [chapter 2](#chapter-2) builds it. Who counts is then
something the argument reaches, not something it assumes.

> **In Omega**
>
> Omega is built before anyone is chosen to count. Chapter 3 argues that
> standing belongs to every physical system whose development runs through
> it.

*Nihilism, briefly.* One response to a dispute like this is to deny that
anything matters at all. One argument for that denial runs: nothing matters,
because nothing is watching and everything ends. [Chapter 29](#chapter-29)
grants its premises and shows that the structure of what can happen is still
not flat. Moral error theory denies, on different grounds, that there are any
moral facts (Mackie 1977). It challenges every ethical theory, this one
included, and this manuscript does not claim to refute it.

**What this chapter does not claim.**
- That existing standards are wrong, or that their insights are lost. Each
  captures something real.
- That disputes of fact about standing cannot be settled. Many can.
- That argument cannot change a standard. It can, and it has.
- That a standard with a general test fails the requirements of section 1.5.
  What it still needs is a justification for its test.
- That this manuscript avoids saying who counts. It reaches an answer, by
  argument.
- That facts alone settle what is good. The manuscript accepts Hume's gap.
- That our judgements about cases are infallible. They are evidence.
- That the three requirements are met here. Later chapters must meet them.
