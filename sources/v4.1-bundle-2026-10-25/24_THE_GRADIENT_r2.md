[Chapter 23](#chapter-23) showed that a decision-maker ranking its plans by
expected whole-field lushness inherits a theory of planning, and that each of
its rules arises from a property of the measure. This chapter asks what that
machinery amounts to. Comparing the continuations a present makes available,
by that measure, gives every present a direction in which the whole field
widens. The chapter sets up that gradient and argues that, if the central
thesis holds, it is an agent-neutral standard of prospective better and
worse: a universal ethics, in a sense the chapter makes precise. It then
faces the fact that no one stands where the top of the gradient can be read
off. It names what deciding without the whole view entails, says what this
book hands on to a separate book that develops it, and says how far this
book can answer the worry that a single measure of the good licenses a
powerful agent to do whatever raises it.

The argument has five steps, and they should not be run together.
1. **The physical continuation structure** fixes what is accessible from a
   present ([chapter 2](#chapter-2)).
2. **Lushness** measures the effective breadth of those continuations
   ([chapter 5](#chapter-5)).
3. **The measure's mathematical properties** give the conditional planning
   results of [chapter 23](#chapter-23).
4. **Comparing the available continuations by the measure** defines the
   gradient (section 24.1).
5. **The central thesis** gives the gradient its proposed ethical
   interpretation (section 24.3).

The gradient, the measure, the universal standard and the instruction to
maximize are connected, but they are four different things. Keeping them
apart is what the chapter is for.

## 24.1 Every present has a direction

**From any present, the continuations that can be brought about differ in
how far they widen the whole field. Compared by lushness, they give the
present a direction of widening, with ties, unresolved comparisons and
possibly no top.**

Stand at a present. Some continuations are open: the plans the participants
in it can carry out, and the developments those plans would set going.
[Chapter 4](#chapter-4) fixed whose futures a comparison of these
continuations compares: the whole field's, counted once, naming no part.
[Chapter 5](#chapter-5) fixed the measure. [Chapters 6](#chapter-6) and
[7](#chapter-7) fixed how it is read over time. Where regimes end, the
comparison is of expected lushness over the expected persistence. Where they
do not, it is the whole profile of the difference, with rate deciding first
and offset second.

> **Plain-English definition 24.1: The gradient**
>
> The comparison, from a given present, of the continuations that can be
> brought about, by expected whole-field lushness over the expected
> persistence (or by the profile, where regimes do not end): the direction in
> which whole-field lushness increases from that present.

> **In Omega**
>
> From each present, Omega holds several continuation processes, one for
> each way the present can be carried forward. The gradient compares those
> processes by the effective breadth of what follows. It is a feature of
> Omega itself, not of anyone's view of it.

Six points fix what the word means here.
- **It is not a derivative.** Nothing in the manuscript defines a smooth
  function whose slope is being taken. "Gradient" names a direction of
  increase among the continuations a present actually has.
- **It can have ties.** Different continuations can have equal lushness at
  every horizon, and the thesis counts them equal in prospective value
  ([chapter 10](#chapter-10), section 10.6).
- **Some comparisons can be unresolved.** Where a comparison can only be
  bounded, and the bounds overlap, the gradient does not say which way is up
  ([chapter 11](#chapter-11), section 11.6).
- **It need not have a top.** If ever-better continuations approach a limit
  that none reaches, no continuation is best. Over a finite persistence the
  comparison can still choose, but in general a best continuation need not
  exist ([Proposition A.13.5](#a-13-5)).
- **It is local to a present and global in scope.** It is read from one
  present, and it changes as the present changes. But it ranks what each
  continuation does to the whole field, not to the part that brings it about
  ([chapter 9](#chapter-9)). Under a deterministic law, a fully specified
  present has one continuation, and there is no gradient
  ([chapter 3](#chapter-3)).
- **It belongs to the world, not to a model of it.** The gradient is fixed
  by the present and its law. A decision-maker's model may represent it well
  or badly. The gradient does not change with the representation.

## 24.2 The rules come from the measure behind the gradient

**The planning rules of chapter 23 arise from properties of the measure that
defines the gradient. An ordering alone would not give them.**

The gradient is defined by comparing continuations, so it is easy to think
of it as nothing more than an ordering. That would lose most of what
[chapter 23](#chapter-23) found. Apply any strictly increasing
transformation to lushness and the order of complete continuations at a
fixed horizon is unchanged. But the transformed quantity no longer splits
into the branching of a first step plus the expected value of what follows.
Without that split there is no backward recursion, no exact value of
information, no bound on what a horizon misses and no cost threshold for a
fragile gain. These results belong to lushness, the entropy of the whole
history, together with the physical continuation structure it measures.

So the relation runs one way. The measure, with its composition and its way
of accumulating over time, gives the planning rules. The same measure,
applied to the continuations a present makes available, gives the gradient.
The rules are how a decision-maker using the measure moves; the gradient is
the direction the measure points. Neither needs anything added from outside.

## 24.3 The best move and a universal ethics

**If the central thesis is true, the gradient is an agent-neutral standard of
prospective better and worse. It is universal in who it counts, in whose
standpoint it takes, and in where it applies. The options available to
particular agents are not universal, and ranking a development highest does
not by itself justify bringing it about.**

[Chapter 10](#chapter-10) stated the central thesis: what we recognize as
good corresponds to widening the whole field's futures, and what we recognize
as harm to narrowing them. Apply it to the gradient. If the thesis holds, the
physical structure supplies a standard of prospective better and worse that
is the same for everyone: from any present, a continuation that widens the
whole field more is better, in prospect, than one that widens it less. The
continuation ranked highest, where one exists, corresponds to the best that
could happen from that present.

That standard is universal in three senses.
- **Universal in who counts.** Every physical system that takes part in
  consequence is a valuer ([chapter 3](#chapter-3)). The gradient is computed
  over all of them, with no list of who belongs, and with no weights. Nothing
  is excluded by being unfamiliar, small, artificial or not yet born.
- **Universal in standpoint.** The comparison names no part of the field
  ([chapter 4](#chapter-4)). So the standard is the same for every
  decision-maker that shares the present: a person, an institution, an
  animal or an artificial agent. Philosophers distinguish reasons that hold
  for anyone from reasons that hold for a particular agent (Nagel 1986). The
  gradient supplies the first kind without assuming that every valuer
  deserves equal weight. It assigns none.
- **Universal in where it applies.** The gradient is defined wherever there
  is a present with more than one continuation. It does not depend on any
  culture, species, set of preferences or training history. That meets the
  requirement [chapter 1](#chapter-1) set for a standard artificial agents
  could hold: it must not depend on whose preferences trained them.

> **Plain-English definition 24.2: The best move on the gradient**
>
> From a given present, a continuation that widens the whole field at least
> as much as any other available, judged over its whole profile and
> expected persistence, where such a continuation exists.

Five limits keep this claim honest.
- **The standard is shared; the options are not.** Every decision-maker that
  shares a present faces the same standard. But each can bring about only
  the continuations its own implementable plans reach
  ([chapter 23](#chapter-23), section 23.1). A person, a government and an
  artificial agent judged by one standard still have different options, and
  so different best moves.
- **Evaluating is not authorizing.** The standard ranks developments.
  Whether a particular agent is justified in bringing about the
  highest-ranked development, given what it knows and what its acting does
  to others, is a further question. Section 24.4 says why it cannot be read
  off the ranking.
- **It is a correspondence, not a derivation.** That the best move
  corresponds to the good is the central thesis, applied. It inherits the
  thesis's status: defended by argument, tested case by case, and able to
  lose ([chapter 10](#chapter-10), section 10.7). If the thesis fails, the
  gradient still exists, but its best move no longer tracks the good.
- **It is not a single rule for every case.** What the best move is depends
  on the present: on what the field holds, what it can reach and what is
  related to what. Value is contextual ([chapter 10](#chapter-10), section
  10.3), and so is the best move. The same act can widen the field in one
  present and narrow it in another.
- **It is not a list of separate goods to be added.** The gradient does not
  score each valuer and sum the scores ([chapter 4](#chapter-4)). It ranks
  joint continuations. So it is not a welfare total, and it carries none of
  the machinery of weights and units that additive theories need.

That is the sense in which the gradient gives a universal ethics: if the
thesis is true, the universe contains an objective standard of prospective
better and worse, the same for everyone who decides. Whether that standard is
right is the thesis. What it is not is a command, and the next section says
why.

## 24.4 No one has the whole view

**The best move on the gradient is what a decision-maker with the whole view
would choose. No participant has that view, and acting as if it did is
itself a consequential error. Deciding without the whole view is a problem of
its own.**

The best move is defined by the whole field. Every decision-maker is a part
of it. That gap is not a temporary shortage of information. It is
structural, for four reasons.
- **No part holds the whole.** A physical system can represent only part of
  the field it belongs to, at limited resolution and over a limited horizon.
  To measure exactly how far its model falls short, it would have to hold the
  whole field. It can estimate parts of its error. It cannot certify all of
  it.
- **What it misses is weighted towards what it knows least.** Beyond any
  finite horizon, the unseen remainder of a comparison is bounded only if the
  world comes to forget the act. Where the act changes a lasting rate, the
  remainder grows with time, and its sign is set by the long-run effect the
  decision-maker can estimate least well
  ([Proposition A.12.1](#a-12-1); [chapter 23](#chapter-23), section 23.5).
- **Its own process is part of what it judges.** A decision-maker acts
  through a physical process guided by its model. Its errors, the choices it
  overrides and the chances for correction it keeps or destroys are all
  consequences, and the comparison counts them ([chapter 10](#chapter-10),
  section 10.6). Overriding another valuer discards choices made from records
  the overrider lacks, and can silence the response that would show it was
  wrong.
- **Its uncertainty adds nothing to the field.** Not knowing which future will
  occur adds no branching, and the chance of being wrong subtracts none.
  What counts is what the act physically brings about. So uncertainty cannot
  be priced into the gradient. It has to be handled in how the gradient is
  followed.

So the instruction "take the best move" cannot be carried out as written. A
decision-maker that treats its model as the whole field, and acts on the
result, has made a move the gradient itself counts: one whose consequences
include every error the model contains and every correction the act forecloses.
Refusing to act does not escape the problem. Refraining is also a move, and
failing to act while a window closes can be just as irreversible.

Deciding within the structure, as a participant who sees only part of the
field and must act anyway, is what this manuscript calls **Gradient Ethics**.

> **Plain-English definition 24.3: Gradient Ethics**
>
> What deciding within the structure entails for a participant who sees only
> part of the field: following the gradient by a partial model, when neither
> acting nor refraining is guaranteed to be harmless.

Gradient Ethics is not a second standard beside the gradient. The standard
stays the same: a continuation that widens the whole field more is better in
prospect. Gradient Ethics is the account of how a participant can move well
by that standard when it cannot see the terrain. This book establishes the
gradient, the planning rules its measure gives, and the quantities from which
protection margins could be built. A separate book
develops the account itself. The rest of this chapter states what that book
inherits and what it must answer.

## 24.5 The maximizer worry

**A single measure of the good can seem to license a powerful agent to do
whatever raises it. This book diagnoses the worry and partly constrains it.
It does not solve it.**

The worry is old and serious. Give an agent one quantity to maximize, make
the agent capable, and it will override, sacrifice and seize whatever stands
between it and a higher score. A theory that offers one universal standard
seems to make that worse, not better: now the agent can claim to act for
everyone.

This book can constrain the worry in four ways.
- **A ranking is not a command.** The gradient says which move widens the
  whole field most. It does not say that anyone is authorized to make it.
  [Chapter 10](#chapter-10) separated the two, and section 24.4 showed why
  the separation is structural: no agent stands where the ranking can be
  read off.
- **The scope is the whole field, not the agent.** The same count applied to
  the agent's own futures is a local comparison, and it can favour seizing
  control of what others depend on ([chapter 4](#chapter-4), section 4.4).
  That is a misuse of the measure, and the framework's own standard rejects
  it. The program predicts that blocking others' access narrows the whole
  even when it widens the blocker's own futures; that prediction is under
  test ([chapter 20](#chapter-20)).
- **The agent's own process counts against it.** Overriding others discards
  their records and can silence correction. An irreversible loss costs the
  whole lost excess when nothing can rebuild it
  ([Proposition A.4.9](#a-4-9)). For destruction that cannot be undone, even
  a modest credence that the destroyed structure matters can outweigh a
  bounded cost of preserving it ([Proposition A.9.5](#a-9-5)). An agent that
  overreaches is charged, by the measure, for what its overreach does.
- **Long shots are capped.** In a finite world, a small enough chance of a
  capped gain loses to a smaller, certain one ([chapter 23](#chapter-23),
  section 23.6). The measure resists being talked into giving up something
  real for an astronomical promise.

What this book cannot do is solve the worry. Neither the whole-field scope
nor the counting of the agent's own consequences shows that a well-informed
whole-field maximizer would never choose a frightening tradeoff. The thesis
permits such tradeoffs in principle. At known odds the
measure is neutral to risk. With the odds known, it accepts a 70% chance of
ruin for a 30% chance of something better ([chapter 23](#chapter-23),
section 23.9). A decision-maker with the whole view could act on that. A
participant without it never knows the odds that well, and the consequences
of acting as if it did are counted. But a situated agent that is highly
confident about the odds is not restrained by uncertainty alone. So this
book offers a diagnosis and some constraints, not a theorem. The maximizer
worry is a worry about situated decision, not about the ranking, and
answering it is the first task of the situated account.

## 24.6 What the next book inherits

**The situated account starts from the gradient, the rules its measure
gives, and a proposed mechanism of protection built from quantities the
measure supplies.**

It inherits four things from this book.
- **The standard.** The gradient, and the central thesis, which makes it an
  agent-neutral standard of prospective better and worse (this chapter;
  [chapter 10](#chapter-10)).
- **The rules.** The planning theory of [chapter 23](#chapter-23), which
  arises from the measure: implemented
  plans, plans built backwards, the decision value of information, the role
  of irreversibility, the remainder beyond a horizon, and the limits on
  fragile gains and long shots.
- **Protection as a concept.** A protection is a margin an agent must clear
  before trading an irreversible loss for a gain elsewhere. It is built from
  quantities the measure supplies: the cost of an early end
  ([Proposition A.4.9](#a-4-9)), the robustness floor
  ([Corollary A.4.6](#a-4-6)), the long-shot bound
  ([Proposition A.5.3](#a-5-3)) and the bounds beyond a finite horizon
  ([Proposition A.12.1](#a-12-1)). Such a margin behaves like a right without
  giving anyone extra weight. It is derived from consequences under
  uncertainty, so it can in principle be outweighed
  ([chapter 17](#chapter-17)). Protection is proposed as the main mechanism
  by which the situated account will work out how bounded agents must act.
  Its calibration and its authority are still to be developed. That the
  quantities are useful does not yet make a rights-like policy.
- **Special protection, not special standing.** Some losses call for far more
  caution than others: losses that cannot be rebuilt, that slow the opening
  of futures rather than costing a fixed amount, and that are hardest to
  estimate. A particular person's death is the central case
  ([chapter 10](#chapter-10), section 10.6). The margin around such losses is
  wide because of what they are, not because anyone counts for more.

## 24.7 What it must answer

**Six questions are left open by this book, and each needs an answer before
anyone can follow the gradient well.**

1. **The decision rule.** How should a participant with a partial model
   choose among plans when the ranking it computes may be wrong in ways it
   cannot measure? The expected-lushness rule of chapter 23 is a starting
   point, not an answer, because it trusts the model.
2. **Calibrating margins.** How wide should a protection margin be, and from
   what? The quantities that build margins are known in principle. How a
   participant should estimate them, and how much to widen a margin for the
   chance that its own model is wrong, are open.
3. **Identity and standing.** Does the pattern that makes a person this
   person warrant special protection under uncertainty, and how much? Can a
   valuer that shapes much of the field around it claim protection on that
   ground, and where does that claim stop ([chapter 22](#chapter-22))?
4. **The incumbent problem.** A protection margin must protect generative
   interdependence without protecting an incumbent's control over it. A rule
   that shields whatever currently holds a channel open will also shield
   whoever holds it closed ([chapter 17](#chapter-17)).
5. **How gains and losses are shared between lives.** The thesis can count
   two options equal when one valuer gains what another loses
   ([chapter 10](#chapter-10), section 10.6). Many readers will see a
   sacrifice there. Whether a situated agent may ever act on such a tradeoff,
   and under what conditions, is a question for the situated account.
6. **Deference and correction.** When should a participant defer to another
   decision-maker, and when should it keep open a channel through which its
   own mistakes can be corrected? A decision model with stipulated payoffs
   shows the shape of the answer: a correction channel is worth the harm it
   turns from lasting to repairable, weighted by the remaining doubt, less
   what it costs ([Proposition A.13.3](#a-13-3)). Computing those payoffs from
   the measure is open. Related work studies when a system should accept
   being switched off (Hadfield-Menell et al. 2017).

## 24.8 Constraints any answer must meet

**Whatever the situated account proposes, it must respect six constraints
that follow from this book.**

1. **Uncertainty is not branching.** No future may be valued more because it
   is unknown, and none discounted because the decider might be wrong about
   it. Uncertainty is handled in how the gradient is followed, never in the
   gradient.
2. **Refraining has consequences.** There is no default option guaranteed to
   be harmless. A rule that treats inaction as safe misprices every closing
   window.
3. **The ranking stays the standard.** Rules, margins, procedures and
   institutions manage incomplete knowledge. They are judged by what they do
   to the whole field. They do not replace the gradient as the measure of
   better and worse. A protective rule can help a bounded agent that makes
   errors even though, for a single exact objective, removing options never
   raises the best attainable value.
4. **Mechanism words carry no sign.** No rule may value organization,
   persistence, protection or caution for its own sake. Each counts only
   through what it does to the continuation structure.
5. **The scope is the whole field.** No rule may turn a local comparison into
   authority. A gain for the decider bought at a larger cost to the rest is
   not a gain.
6. **Rules are physical.** A decision rule is carried out by a physical
   process, with costs, delays and failure modes. Those are consequences,
   and the rule is assessed with them included.

## 24.9 What would count as failure

**The situated account can fail. So can the claim that the gradient is worth
following at all.**

Four results would count against the program at this point.
1. **Following the gradient does no better than ignoring it.** In fair
   bounded-agent experiments ([chapter 11](#chapter-11), section 11.3),
   decision-makers that use the measure, with the constraints above, keep the
   whole field no more open than decision-makers that ignore it. No such
   experiment has yet been built ([Appendix D](#appendix-d), section D.4).
2. **The margins swallow the standard.** The protection margins needed to
   avoid catastrophe turn out so wide that the gradient never decides
   anything. Every choice is settled by margins, and the ranking is idle.
3. **The constraints cannot all be met.** No decision rule can respect both
   "refraining has consequences" and the limits on fanaticism, or both the
   whole-field scope and the protection of incumbents' dependants, in the
   cases that matter.
4. **The correspondence fails.** The best move on the gradient turns out,
   in cases where judgement is secure and the models are fair, to be what we
   recognize as harm. That is the central thesis failing
   ([chapter 10](#chapter-10), section 10.7), and it would take the universal
   ethics of section 24.3 with it.

## 24.11 What this chapter does not claim

- **That the gradient tells anyone what to do.** It ranks what can be done.
  Following it well, without the whole view, is the situated account's
  problem.
- **That the universal ethics is derived from physics.** It is the central
  thesis applied to choice, and it stands or falls with the thesis.
- **That the best move is the same in every situation.** It depends on the
  present, as value does.
- **That caution, protection or inaction is safe.** Each is a move, with
  consequences.
- **That the maximizer worry is solved.** It is diagnosed and partly
  constrained here. Its part about situated decision is open.
- **That the gradient alone gives the planning rules.** They arise from the
  measure that defines it.
- **That the situated account exists yet.** This chapter states what it
  inherits, what it must answer and how it could fail.
